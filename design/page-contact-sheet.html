Chairing a WG21 Room

3.2 Taking a Straw Poll

The chair states the question clearly and makes sure the room understands what is being asked. If needed, the chair restates the question in neutral terms before calling for the poll.

The chair then counts the room, recording the number of hands in each column. WG21 uses five columns: strongly favor (SF), favor (F), neutral (N), against (A), and strongly against (SA). The results are reported back to the room as five numbers, for example 9 14 5 2 1.1 Read the full poll guidance before your first meeting.

Before you call the poll

Work through these steps in order:

  1. Read the abstract
  2. Find the motivating example
  3. Check the proposed wording
  4. Write down one question

A good proposal keeps four things in view:

  • Problem: What precisely is difficult, ambiguous, or missing?
  • Motivation: Who runs into this, and what does it cost them?
  • Design: What is the smallest language change that removes the problem?
  • Wording: How would you explain the change to a working C++ programmer?

Consensus is more than a majority. It asks whether the whole delegation can live with the outcome.

3.4 Managing the Queue and the Timebox

A good chair keeps the discussion moving by holding a clear speaker queue and letting the room know where it stands in the timebox. Announce the time remaining at natural breakpoints: at the start, when half the time has gone, and again when only a few minutes remain.

If the queue is long, politely remind speakers to be concise and to address the question before the room.

Your goal is not to let every person speak as long as they wish, but to give everyone a fair opportunity while moving the room toward a decision.

If the discussion drifts or repeats, summarize the common positions you are hearing and invite the room to confirm or correct your summary.
