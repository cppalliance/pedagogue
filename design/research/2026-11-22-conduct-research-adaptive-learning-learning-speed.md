## Top findings

1. **Practice testing beats restudying, in the lab and in classrooms.** Lab: g = 0.50 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.58) over 159 effect sizes, and g = 0.61 (fixed) or 0.70 (random) over 272 effect sizes with 15,427 participants. Classrooms: g = 0.499 over 222 studies and 48,478 students. Grade A. [Rowland2014] [Adesope2017] [Yang2021]
2. **The size of the testing effect depends heavily on the comparison group.** In classrooms it is g = 0.610 against no activity, 0.330 against restudying and only 0.095 against other elaborative strategies. Grade A. [Yang2021]
3. **Feedback and retrieval success decide how much a quiz teaches.** With feedback g = 0.73, without g = 0.39. With no feedback and 50% or less correct on the practice test, the effect was g = 0.03. In classrooms, quizzes with corrective feedback gave g = 0.537 against 0.374 without. Grade A. [Rowland2014] [Yang2021]
4. **Testing also helps transfer, by a smaller amount.** d = 0.40 over 192 effect sizes, rising to 0.58 when the final test asks for the same responses and falling to 0.28 when it asks for different ones. Transfer was weakest for worked-example problems. In classrooms, quizzing lifted untested material by g = 0.321 and problem-solving outcomes by g = 0.453. Grade A. [PanRickard2018] [Yang2021]
5. **Spacing is the other technique with the strongest record.** Spaced against massed retrieval practice: g = 1.01 uncorrected (the abstract reports 0.74). A re-analysis of the Dunlosky study set puts distributed practice at d = 0.85, the highest of the ten techniques. Grade A. [Latimier2021] [DonoghueHattie2021] [Dunlosky2013]
6. **The best gap between sessions grows with how long the learning has to last.** In a study of more than 1,350 people the best gap was about 20 to 40% of a 1-week delay and about 5 to 10% of a 1-year delay. For a test 7 days out the best gap was 1 day, for 35 days it was 11 days, for 70 days it was 21 days. Expanding schedules did no better than even ones (g = 0.034). Grade A. [Cepeda2008] [Carpenter2012] [Latimier2021]
7. **How well a student does during practice is a poor guide to what lasts.** After 5 minutes, rereading beat testing (81% against 75% recalled). After 1 week, testing beat rereading (56% against 42%, d = 0.83). In a flashcard study spacing worked better for 90% of participants while 72% believed massing had worked better. Grade A for the reversal, B for the belief data. [RoedigerKarpicke2006] [Kornell2009] [SoderstromBjork2015]
8. **Interleaving helps when the learner has to tell similar things apart, and hurts with word lists.** Overall g = 0.42 (238 effect sizes). Paintings g = 0.67, mathematics g = 0.34, words g = -0.39, expository texts not significant. A preregistered classroom trial (54 classes, 787 students) found 61% against 38% on a surprise test one month later, d = 0.83. Grade A. [BrunmairRichter2019] [Rohrer2020]
9. **Worked examples help novices, with a medium effect.** g = 0.48 over 55 studies and 181 effect sizes in mathematics. Correct examples alone did better than incorrect or mixed ones. In the founding study the gain held only for problems with the same structure as the examples. Grade A. [Barbieri2023] [SwellerCooper1985]
10. **Self-explanation prompts help, and the evidence on combining them with worked examples conflicts.** Prompts gave g = 0.55 (69 effect sizes, 5,917 participants), and still g = 0.41 when study time was matched. But in the worked-example meta-analysis, adding self-explanation prompts lowered the effect. Grade A. [Bisra2018] [Barbieri2023]
11. **Doing beats watching and reading.** Active learning raised exam scores by 0.47 SD and cut failure from 33.8% to 21.8% across 225 undergraduate STEM studies. In courseware data, one SD more doing went with 0.44 SD higher quiz scores, more than six times the figure for reading or video, and the pattern repeated in seven courses with 18,546 students (ratios 1.3 to 6.2). Grade A for the meta-analysis, B for the doer effect, which rests on observational data. [Freeman2014] [Koedinger2015] [VanCampenhout2023]
12. **Unguided discovery loses to explicit instruction, guided discovery wins.** Unassisted discovery against explicit instruction: d = -0.38 (580 comparisons). Discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations against other instruction: d = 0.30 (360 comparisons). Grade A. [Alfieri2011]
13. **Problem solving before instruction is the real counter-case, with tight limits.** g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.51) for conceptual knowledge and transfer, 0.25 in randomized comparisons only, and -0.03 for procedural knowledge. The sign flips for grades 2 to 5 and for domain-general skills. Grade A. [SinhaKapur2021]
14. **Mastery learning raises scores on tests matched to the course, and much less on broad tests.** +0.52 SD on end-of-course exams (103 studies), 0.71 SD at a follow-up about 8 weeks later (11 studies), for a median 4% more instructional time. On standardized tests the mean effect was 0.29 SD across 11 studies in Kulik et al., and the median was +0.04 in Slavin's seven school studies with equal time. Grade A. [Kulik1990] [Slavin1987]
15. **Bloom's two sigma is a result for a bundle on a narrow test.** It comes from two dissertations with 11 lessons over 3 weeks. The tutored students also got mastery quizzes, corrective feedback and retests, and their tutors were trained. The mastery-learning arm alone reached about 1 sigma. Grade B for the original experiments, D for the claim that tutoring by itself yields two sigma. [Bloom1984] [vonHippel2024]
16. **Typical human tutoring effects run from under half of Bloom's figure down to about a tenth of it.** d = 0.79 against no tutoring with the same content (10 comparisons). Pooled over randomized trials: 0.288 SD in the published version, 0.37 in the working paper. Across 263 trials (journal version) or 265 trials (working paper) the average is about 0.40 to 0.42 SD, falling to about 0.16 to 0.26 SD for programs that serve 400 or more students. Grade A. [VanLehn2011] [Nickow2024] [Kraft2024]
17. **Tutoring systems come close to human tutors on aligned tests and show little on broad tests.** Step-based systems d = 0.76 and answer-based systems d = 0.31 against no tutoring. ITS against large-group teaching g = 0.42, against human tutoring g = -0.11 (not significant). Median effect 0.66 SD, but 0.73 on locally made tests against 0.13 on standardized tests, and 0.30 in studies with more than 250 participants. Grade A. [VanLehn2011] [Ma2014] [KulikFletcher2016]
18. **An honest expected effect, by comparison group.** Against business-as-usual instruction on an independent or standardized test: about 0.1 to 0.3 SD. Against no tutoring with the same content, on a test aligned to what was taught: about 0.4 to 0.8 SD. Against a good human tutor or another active, elaborative method: about zero. Grade A for the pattern, drawn from the sources in findings 16 and 17. [Kraft2024] [KulikFletcher2016] [Ma2014] [SteenbergenHu2013] [Yang2021]
19. **Students differ far more in what they already know than in how fast they learn.** Across 27 datasets and 1.3 million observations, students started practice at about 65% correct after instruction and gained about 2.5 percentage points per practice opportunity. The typical student needed about 7 opportunities to reach 80%. Students at the 25th percentile of prior knowledge needed 13.13 and those at the 75th needed 3.66. Splitting by learning rate gave 7.89 against 6.94. Grade B. [Koedinger2023]
20. **Deliberate practice matters less in education and professions than its fame suggests.** Accumulated deliberate practice explained 24% of performance variance in games, 23% in music, 20% in sports, 5% in education and 1% in professions (88 studies, 11,135 participants). These are the figures from the paper's 2018 corrigendum - the 2014 paper printed 26%, 21%, 18%, 4% and under 1%. Grade A, correlational. [Macnamara2014]
21. **Page design moves learning by a medium amount, and interesting but irrelevant material costs learning.** Mayer's median effects from his own tests: coherence 0.86, signaling 0.70, segmenting 0.67, pre-training 0.78, personalization 1.00. An independent meta-analysis puts signaling at g = 0.53 for retention and 0.33 for transfer. Seductive details: g = -0.33, and -0.52 for learners with low prior knowledge. Grade A for seductive details, B for the Mayer medians. [Mayer2021] [Schneider2018] [Sundararajan2020]
22. **Far transfer is rare, near transfer is teachable, and gains fade.** In a second-order meta-analysis of cognitive training, far transfer was zero once placebo effects and publication bias were controlled. Comparing cases gave d = 0.50 over 336 tests. Fade-out of intervention effects is widespread. In the Dunlosky study set, 74% of post-tests came within one day. Grade A. [Sala2019] [Alfieri2013] [Bailey2020] [DonoghueHattie2021]
23. **Persistence goes with valuing the work, and tangible rewards for taking part cut later interest.** Across 344 samples (223,209 students), intrinsic motivation correlated .41 with adaptive outcomes, identified regulation (personal value) correlated .51 with effort and .31 with intent to continue, and external regulation correlated about zero with performance and persistence. Tangible rewards lowered free-choice persistence (d = -0.34), positive feedback raised it (d = 0.33). Grade A, mostly correlational for the first set. [Howard2021] [Deci1999]
24. **Growth mindset and other one-shot motivation boosts are small.** Mindset interventions: d = 0.08 in one meta-analysis and d = 0.05 in another, 0.02 in the six best studies. A national trial found +0.10 grade points for lower-achieving 9th graders. Among adults, mindset and achievement correlated r = .02 (first seen in a secondary summary, confirmed in the paper's full text on 2026-11-22). Planning prompts and value-relevance writing did not raise completion overall across 269,169 online learners. Grade A. [Sisk2018] [MacnamaraBurgoyne2023] [Yeager2019] [Kizilcec2020]
25. **Open online courses lose most starters, and three popular ideas trace back to opinion.** MOOC completion averaged 6.5% in early data and 3.13% at Harvard and MIT in 2017-18, with 52% of registrants never opening the course. Reviewers who traced three popular ideas back to their origins found opinion or a mis-cited paper each time: matching teaching to "learning styles", the "learning pyramid" retention percentages, and the 10 to 15 minute attention limit. Grade A for completion data, A for the learning styles review, B for the other two. [Jordan2014] [Reich2019] [Pashler2008] [LetrudHernes2018] [Bradbury2016]

### The technique reviews

- **Finding:** Five independent reviews, written for different audiences, converge on the same short list: retrieval practice, spacing, worked examples alternated with problems, explanatory questions, and combining words with graphics.
  - **Evidence:** Dunlosky et al. rated ten techniques. High utility: practice testing, distributed practice. Moderate: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. Low: summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. The IES practice guide gives seven recommendations, two of them in two parts, with evidence levels: space learning (moderate), interleave worked examples with problems (moderate), combine graphics with verbal descriptions (moderate), connect abstract and concrete representations (moderate), use quizzes to re-expose content (strong), use pre-questions (low), teach students to judge what they know (low), ask deep explanatory questions (strong). Weinstein, Madan and Sumeracki name six strategies: spaced practice, interleaving, retrieval practice, elaboration, concrete examples, dual coding. Deans for Impact organize the same findings under six questions.
  - **Caveats:** Dunlosky et al. held back a "high" rating from elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving because classroom evidence was thin in 2013. The authors' Scientific American summary says elaborative interrogation "may not be useful for material more complex than a factual list" and that the interleaving literature "includes enough negative results to raise concern". Weinstein et al. note that elaboration is too broad a term to claim it always helps.
  - **Grade:** A (reviews of experimental work)
  - **Sources:** [Dunlosky2013] [Pashler2007] [Weinstein2018] [DeansForImpact2015]
- **Finding:** When the Dunlosky study set was meta-analysed, every one of the ten techniques showed a positive average effect, and the ranking mostly held.
  - **Evidence:** 242 studies, 1,619 effects, 169,179 participants, overall mean d = 0.56. Distributed practice 0.85 (150 effects), practice testing 0.74 (374), elaborative interrogation 0.56 (254), imagery 0.56 (135), rereading 0.47 (113), interleaved practice 0.47 (104), underlining 0.44 (56), summarization 0.44 (234).
  - **Caveats:** 93% of outcomes were surface or factual and 7% deep. 74% of post-tests were given within one day, 17% at 2 to 7 days, and under 4% beyond a week. Effects were larger for near than far transfer and "much greater for lower than higher ability students" (a secondary summary gives d = -0.11 for high-ability students). 65% of participants were undergraduates and 2% other adults. The comparison is with whatever control each study used, so "low utility" techniques still beat doing nothing. The table in this paper labels imagery "moderate" and interleaving "low", which differs from the Dunlosky abstract.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [DonoghueHattie2021]
- **Finding:** Rosenshine's principles describe what the most effective classroom teachers did, and they match the cognitive findings.
  - **Evidence:** Ten principles: begin with a short review; present new material in small steps with practice after each step; ask many questions and check all students' responses; provide models; guide practice; check for understanding; obtain a high success rate; provide scaffolds; require independent practice; review weekly and monthly. The article says the optimal success rate "appears to be about 80 percent" and that the least successful teachers had a success rate of 73 percent. Effective teachers did hands-on, experiential activities "after, not before, the basic material was learned".
  - **Caveats:** The base is correlational classroom observation of school teachers plus cognitive lab work. The 80 percent figure describes what successful classrooms looked like. The article presents it as what "the research suggests" and gives one comparison point, the 73 percent of the least successful teachers.
  - **Grade:** C for the observational base, D for the 80 percent target
  - **Sources:** [Rosenshine2012]

### Retrieval practice

- **Finding:** Taking a test on material produces better delayed retention than studying it again.
  - **Evidence:** Roediger and Karpicke (120 undergraduates, prose passages): at 5 minutes restudy 81% against test 75%; at 2 days test 68% against 54% (d = 0.95); at 1 week test 56% against 42% (d = 0.83). Rowland: g = 0.50 (CI 0.42 to 0.58), 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, all with a restudy control matched for time. Adesope et al.: 118 articles, 272 effect sizes, 15,427 participants, g = 0.61 fixed and 0.70 random; g = 0.51 against restudy and 0.93 against filler or no activity (secondary summary for the last two). Yang et al.: 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.499 (CI 0.442 to 0.557); 82.9% of effects positive; bias-adjusted estimates 0.427 to 0.475.
  - **Caveats:** Rowland: published studies showed g = 0.58 against 0.25 for unpublished ones (secondary summary). Adesope: 82% of estimates are from laboratories and 228 of 272 are from college students (secondary summary); heterogeneity I2 = 81% (full text). Yang: within-subject designs gave g = 0.674 and between-subject designs 0.415.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [RoedigerKarpicke2006] [Rowland2014] [Adesope2017] [Yang2021]
- **Finding:** Feedback and retrieval success are the main boundary conditions.
  - **Evidence:** Rowland: with feedback g = 0.73 (CI 0.61 to 0.86), without g = 0.39 (CI 0.29 to 0.49). Without feedback, initial success of 50% or less gave g = 0.03 (CI -0.21 to 0.27), 51 to 75% gave 0.29, above 75% gave 0.56. Delayed feedback g = 1.38 (6 effects) against immediate 0.66 (46 effects). Yang: corrective feedback 0.537 against 0.374.
  - **Caveats:** Adesope found the feedback difference small and not significant (0.63 with, 0.60 without). Rowland warns that the delayed-feedback result rests on 6 observations and is confounded with retention interval.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Rowland2014] [Yang2021] [Adesope2017]
- **Finding:** The effect grows with delay and with repeated use over a course.
  - **Evidence:** Rowland: g = 0.41 at retention intervals under one day and 0.69 at one day or longer (secondary summary). Yang: g = 0.385 for a single class, 0.521 for less than a semester, 0.547 for a semester, 0.624 for longer than a semester. Post-class quizzes g = 0.536 against pre-class quizzes 0.186. In-class 0.514 against out-of-class 0.401. High-stakes 0.441 and low-stakes 0.477 (difference not significant).
  - **Caveats:** Adesope found larger effects for one practice test (0.70) than for two or more (0.51), which runs against the repetition pattern in Yang.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Rowland2014] [Yang2021] [Adesope2017]
- **Finding:** The three meta-analyses disagree on which test format works best.
  - **Evidence:** Rowland (lab, restudy control): cued recall g = 0.61, free recall 0.29, recognition (which includes multiple choice) 0.29; in the subset with high re-exposure, recognition 0.36, cued recall 0.72, free recall 0.82. Adesope: multiple choice 0.70, short answer 0.48, free recall 0.62, cued recall 0.58, mixed formats 0.80. Yang (classrooms): matching 0.913, fill-in-the-blank 0.773, short answer 0.638, multiple choice 0.567, cued recall 0.316, free recall 0.238; format was not a significant moderator in their recall against recognition test. Matching formats between quiz and exam helped: 0.531 against 0.399 (Yang), 0.63 against 0.53 (Adesope, secondary summary).
  - **Caveats:** Format is confounded with feedback, success rate and setting. Free recall produces lower success, and low success without feedback removes the benefit.
  - **Grade:** A for "all formats work", B for any ordering of formats
  - **Sources:** [Rowland2014] [Adesope2017] [Yang2021]
- **Finding:** Whether the effect holds for complex, highly interconnected material is disputed.
  - **Evidence:** Van Gog and Sweller argue the effect "decreases or even disappears" as element interactivity rises, citing studies with worked examples and complex texts. Karpicke and Aue reply that complexity was never measured or manipulated in those studies, that relevant studies were left out, and that the studies cited still show a small positive effect (secondary summary). Yang: quizzing helped facts (g = 0.524), concepts (0.644) and problem solving (0.453). Pan and Rickard: transfer from testing was weakest for "problems involving worked examples". A dissertation summary found retrieval of procedure steps improved memory for the steps and did not improve problem solving, while practice at solving problems did.
  - **Caveats:** Weinstein et al. record the debate as ongoing in 2018.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [VanGogSweller2015] [KarpickeAue2015] [Yang2021] [PanRickard2018] [Weinstein2018] [RetrievalThesis]
- **Finding:** Testing produces some transfer, and the amount depends on three things.
  - **Evidence:** Pan and Rickard: d = 0.40 (CI 0.31 to 0.50), 192 effect sizes, 122 experiments, N = 10,382. With response congruency (the final test asks for the same answers in a new form) d = 0.58, without it d = 0.28. Elaborated retrieval practice and high initial test performance also raised transfer. Transfer was strongest across test formats and to application and inference questions.
  - **Caveats:** After correction for publication bias the baseline estimate fell, "often indicating no positive transfer" when none of the three moderators is present.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [PanRickard2018]

### Spacing

- **Finding:** Spreading study or practice over time beats the same amount massed together, across ages and materials.
  - **Evidence:** Cepeda et al. 2006: 839 assessments in 317 experiments from 184 articles; spaced presentations 47.3% correct against 36.7% massed, and only 12 of 271 comparisons showed zero or negative effect (model-written secondary summary). Carpenter et al. 2022 call it "one of the most reliable and robust findings" with d "sometimes over 1.0" for retention over at least a day. Latimier et al.: spaced against massed retrieval practice g = 1.01 (CI 0.68 to 1.34) over 39 effect sizes from 11 studies; the abstract reports 0.74. Cepeda et al. 2009: an optimal gap "improved final recall by up to 150%".
  - **Caveats:** The 2006 review covers verbal recall tasks, and 85% of its data come from young adults (secondary summary). Latimier's typical study is a lab study with adults learning word pairs.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Cepeda2006] [Carpenter2022] [Latimier2021] [Cepeda2009]
- **Finding:** The best gap is a share of the time the learning has to last, and the share shrinks as that time grows.
  - **Evidence:** Cepeda et al. 2008 (more than 1,350 people, gaps up to 3.5 months, final test up to 1 year later): performance first rose then slowly fell as the gap grew; the optimal gap was about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay and about 5 to 10% of a 1-year delay. Carpenter et al. 2012 report the same study: best gap 1 day for a 7-day delay, 11 days for 35 days, 21 days for 70 days, "in general ... 10% - 20% of the test delay". Rohrer and Pashler 2010 summarize it as "approximately 5% to 10% of the test delay" for practical purposes.
  - **Caveats:** Three summaries of one study give three different rules of thumb (5 to 10%, 10 to 20%, 20 to 40% down to 5 to 10%). The study taught obscure facts in two sessions. Carpenter et al. 2022: "there is no universal ideal spacing schedule", and long gaps work less well for material that is not yet well learned.
  - **Grade:** A for the direction, B for any single percentage
  - **Sources:** [Cepeda2008] [Carpenter2012] [RohrerPashler2010] [Carpenter2022]
- **Finding:** Spacing across days does more for long-term retention than spacing inside one session.
  - **Evidence:** Cepeda et al. 2006 (secondary summary): for retention intervals under a minute, gaps under a minute were best; for retention of six months or more, gaps of at least a month were best; in studies with retention over a month every study favored spreading learning over weeks or months. A later paper quoted on a citation page states that "spacing has larger and longer-lasting effects on retention when practice trials for a given item are distributed across days versus within a session". Within a session spacing still helps: Kornell 2009 found one large flashcard stack (longer gaps between repeats) beat four small stacks, for 90% of participants.
  - **Caveats:** A review quoted on the same citation page found only 14 studies that compared very short gaps (under three hours) with gaps of a day or more at test delays of a day or more.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Cepeda2006] [Kornell2009] [Cepeda2009]
- **Finding:** Expanding review intervals work about as well as even ones.
  - **Evidence:** Latimier et al.: expanding against uniform schedules g = 0.034 (CI -0.10 to 0.17), 54 effect sizes. Cepeda 2006: expanding 62.0% against fixed 58.6%, not significant (secondary summary). Carpenter et al. 2012: teachers "do not need to be overly concerned" about whether gaps are equal.
  - **Caveats:** Latimier found a trend for expanding schedules to help more as the number of tests per item rises (not significant).
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Latimier2021] [Cepeda2006] [Carpenter2012]

### Interleaving

- **Finding:** Mixing items from different categories helps learners tell the categories apart, with a moderate average effect that swings widely by material.
  - **Evidence:** Brunmair and Richter: 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples; overall g = 0.42 (CI 0.34 to 0.50). Paintings g = 0.67, mathematical tasks g = 0.34, words g = -0.39 (CI -0.64 to -0.14), expository texts and tastes not significant. Interleaving worked better when categories were similar to each other, when items inside a category differed from each other, and for more complex material.
  - **Caveats:** The authors conclude interleaving "should be used with caution in certain conditions, especially for expository texts and words". Few studies varied the retention interval. Donoghue and Hattie put interleaved practice at d = 0.47 with wide variation.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [BrunmairRichter2019] [DonoghueHattie2021]
- **Finding:** In real mathematics classrooms, mostly interleaved homework produced a large gain on a delayed surprise test.
  - **Evidence:** Rohrer et al. 2020: preregistered cluster randomized trial, 54 seventh-grade classes (28 interleaved, 26 blocked), 787 students, 15 teachers, four months of assignments, then an unannounced test one month later. Interleaved 61% against blocked 38%, d = 0.83 (CI 0.68 to 0.97). The effect was positive for every one of the 15 teachers (d from 0.23 to 1.48). Teachers ran it without training.
  - **Caveats:** The authors describe it as "a low versus high dose of interleaved practice": both groups got blocked practice when each skill was first taught and both got an interleaved review. Interleaved assignments also spread practice over time, so spacing is part of the treatment. One subject, one grade.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Rohrer2020]

### Desirable difficulties and the gap between performance and learning

- **Finding:** Conditions that make practice feel harder and slower often produce better retention and transfer, and learners judge them wrongly.
  - **Evidence:** Bjork and Bjork name four: varying the conditions of practice, interleaving, spacing, and using tests (generation) as study events. Their account separates storage strength (how well a memory is tied into other knowledge) from retrieval strength (how easy it is to call up right now); current performance reflects retrieval strength only. Soderstrom and Bjork review the motor and verbal learning literature and conclude that performance during instruction is "often an unreliable index" of learning, and that some manipulations have opposite effects on the two. Roediger and Karpicke: students who restudied were more confident and remembered less a week later. Kornell: 72% believed massing had worked better when spacing had.
  - **Caveats:** Bjork and Bjork: a difficulty is desirable only when the learner can respond to it. "If ... the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties." This matches the Rowland finding that testing with half or less correct and with no feedback gave g = 0.03. The 2011 chapter is a narrative review.
  - **Grade:** A for the underlying effects, D for the framework as a whole
  - **Sources:** [BjorkBjork2011] [SoderstromBjork2015] [RoedigerKarpicke2006] [Kornell2009] [Rowland2014]

### Worked examples and self-explanation

- **Finding:** Studying worked solutions teaches novices faster than solving the same problems unaided.
  - **Evidence:** Sweller and Cooper 1985, Experiment 5 (algebra): worked-example learners took 57.9 seconds per first acquisition problem against 232.2 for conventional problem solving; on similar test problems 52.4 against 101.1 seconds with 0.13 against 0.58 errors per problem; on dissimilar problems 163.7 against 183.3 seconds with 1.06 against 0.88 errors. Barbieri et al. 2023: 8,033 abstracts screened, 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes, g = 0.48 (p = .01) for mathematics performance, whether examples were used for first learning or for practice. The IES guide rates "interleave worked example solutions with problem-solving exercises" as moderate evidence.
  - **Caveats:** Sweller and Cooper: both findings "were specific to problems identical in structure to the initial ones". Barbieri: correct examples alone gave larger effects than incorrect examples or a mix; all studies are in mathematics, and a practitioner blog notes most are middle and high school algebra and geometry. Kirschner et al.: the advantage of guidance "begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge". Pan and Rickard: retrieval practice transferred least to worked-example problems.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [SwellerCooper1985] [Barbieri2023] [Pashler2007] [Kirschner2006] [PanRickard2018]
- **Finding:** Prompting learners to explain to themselves improves understanding.
  - **Evidence:** Chi et al. 1994: 14 eighth graders prompted to explain each line of a text on the circulatory system gained 32% from pretest to posttest; 10 who read the text twice gained 22%; high explainers all reached the correct mental model. Bisra et al. 2018: 64 reports, 69 effect sizes, 5,917 participants, g = 0.55 (CI 0.45 to 0.65). Self-explanation beat being given an instructional explanation (g = 0.35). When time was matched g = 0.41; when self-explanation took longer g = 0.72 (difference not significant). The IES guide rates "ask deep explanatory questions" as strong evidence.
  - **Caveats:** In Chi et al. the prompted group studied for a mean of 2 hours 5 minutes against 1 hour 6 minutes for the control, so time is confounded. Bisra: "most research on self-explanation has, regrettably, not fully reported time-on-task". Barbieri found that adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples lowered the effect compared with examples alone. ICAP adds a condition: an explanation that repeats the text word for word counts as active only, and has to add new inferences to count as constructive.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Chi1994] [Bisra2018] [Barbieri2023] [Pashler2007] [ChiWylie2014]
- **Finding:** Elaborative interrogation (asking "why is this true?") has moderate support.
  - **Evidence:** Rated moderate utility by Dunlosky et al.; d = 0.56 over 254 effects in Donoghue and Hattie.
  - **Caveats:** The authors' summary says benefits "for learners without prior knowledge may be limited" and that it may not suit material more complex than factual lists.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Dunlosky2013] [DonoghueHattie2021]

### Active against passive learning

- **Finding:** The ICAP framework orders engagement by what the learner overtly does: interactive above constructive above active above passive.
  - **Evidence:** Passive is receiving (listening, reading). Active is manipulating (underlining, copying, rotating an object). Constructive is generating something beyond the material (self-explaining, drawing a concept map, posing questions). Interactive is a dialogue in which both partners' turns are constructive, and the partner can be a computer agent that responds to content. Chi and Wylie review studies that fit the predicted order.
  - **Caveats:** It is a framework built by classifying existing studies. The authors grant that a student can process deeply while looking passive.
  - **Grade:** D for the framework, B for the studies it organizes
  - **Sources:** [ChiWylie2014]
- **Finding:** In undergraduate STEM courses, active learning beats lecturing on exams and on pass rates.
  - **Evidence:** Freeman et al.: 225 studies. Exam and concept-inventory scores rose 0.47 SD (158 studies), about 6% in exam score. Odds ratio for failing under lecture 1.95 (67 studies): failure 33.8% under lecture against 21.8% under active learning. Randomized and quasi-random designs gave indistinguishable results.
  - **Caveats:** "Active learning" covered everything from occasional group problems to full studio courses. Gains were larger on concept inventories than on course exams and largest in classes of 50 or fewer. The control is traditional lecturing only.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Freeman2014]
- **Finding:** The doer effect: practice activities predict learning far more strongly than reading or watching, and the pattern survives a control for student diligence.
  - **Evidence:** Koedinger et al. 2015 (psychology MOOC with courseware; 939 courseware students took the final): a causal model gave a normalized coefficient of 0.44 for doing activities on quiz score against about .065 each for video and reading. Koedinger et al. 2016: within-unit doing predicted that unit's quiz beyond doing outside the unit, in four courses with over 12,500 students. Van Campenhout et al. 2021: 3,120 students, within-unit doing estimate 0.1146, outside-unit 0.1556, both significant. Van Campenhout et al. 2023: seven courses, 18,546 students, 15.2 million events; within-unit doing significant in every course; doer-effect ratios 1.3 to 6.2.
  - **Caveats:** All of it is observational. The 2023 authors say the model "does not demonstrate causality conclusively". The within-unit design rules out a general "go-getter" trait and leaves other third variables open. The replications come from one courseware vendor's research group. Final-exam models showed negative coefficients for reading.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Koedinger2015] [Koedinger2016] [VanCampenhout2021] [VanCampenhout2023]

### Explicit instruction against discovery

- **Finding:** For new material, guided instruction beats leaving learners to find things out unaided.
  - **Evidence:** Alfieri et al. 2011, 164 studies. Unassisted discovery against explicit instruction: d = -0.38 (CI -0.44 to -0.31) over 580 comparisons from 108 studies. Enhanced discovery (generation, elicited explanations, guided discovery with scaffolding or feedback) against other instruction: d = 0.30 (CI 0.23 to 0.36) over 360 comparisons from 56 studies. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark argue from cognitive architecture and fifty years of studies that minimally guided instruction is "less effective and less efficient".
  - **Caveats:** Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn reply that problem-based and inquiry learning are heavily scaffolded and were wrongly grouped with unguided discovery. That reply agrees that unguided approaches have little support. The Alfieri result itself shows that discovery plus guidance beats other instruction.
  - **Grade:** A for the meta-analysis, D for the position papers
  - **Sources:** [Alfieri2011] [Kirschner2006] [HmeloSilver2007]
- **Finding:** Letting students attempt a problem before teaching the solution helps conceptual understanding and transfer, with no gain for procedures.
  - **Evidence:** Sinha and Kapur 2021: 53 studies, 166 comparisons. Problem solving then instruction against instruction then problem solving: g = 0.36 (CI 0.20 to 0.51) for conceptual knowledge and transfer. Procedural knowledge g = -0.03 (CI -0.20 to 0.15, 51 comparisons). Randomized experiments g = 0.25 (CI 0.04 to 0.47), quasi-experiments 0.46. By subject: mathematics 0.48, physics 0.39, medicine 0.24, domain-general skills -0.17. The abstract states that effects favored instruction first for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills.
  - **Caveats:** The abstract's figure of g = 0.87 "after accounting for publication bias" is larger than the pooled estimate; a secondary critique argues it should not be used as an effect estimate. The same critique notes that several printed subgroup confidence intervals are not centered on their estimates. Studies were found by citation of the productive-failure papers. Outcomes are posttests on the targeted concept.
  - **Grade:** A, with the caveats above
  - **Sources:** [SinhaKapur2021]

### Mastery learning

- **Finding:** Holding students to a mastery standard on each unit, with feedback and correctives, raises achievement on course-aligned exams by about half a standard deviation.
  - **Evidence:** Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns 1990: 108 controlled evaluations (72 used Keller's Personalized System of Instruction in college). Mean effect 0.52 SD across 103 studies, which moves the average student to the 70th percentile. Follow-up exams about 8 weeks later: 0.71 SD in 11 studies (0.60 at the end of instruction in the same studies). Score variation in mastery groups was 77% of the control variation. Effects were stronger for weaker students. Bloom reports the mastery-learning arm of his students' experiments at about 1 SD (the 84th percentile). Cook et al. 2013 (health professionals, simulation training): 82 studies; ES 1.29 for skills against no intervention; ES 1.17 against non-mastery simulation training (3 studies).
  - **Caveats:** Slavin's best-evidence synthesis kept only studies of at least four weeks. In the seven of them that gave both groups equal time and used standardized tests, the median effect was +0.04, and effects on experimenter-made tests were positive and moderate (about +0.24, secondary summary). His reading is that mastery learning focuses teaching on the tested objectives. Kulik et al. required a mastery criterion of at least 70% correct for inclusion.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Kulik1990] [Slavin1987] [Bloom1984] [Cook2013]
- **Finding:** Mastery learning costs time, and self-paced versions lose students.
  - **Evidence:** Kulik et al.: of eight studies that reported time, six showed more instructional time for the mastery group, median 4% more. "Self-paced mastery programs often reduce the completion rates in college classes." Cook et al.: mastery simulation training beat non-mastery training "but required more time", and extra practice "again, took longer". Bloom: time was equal across arms "except for the corrective work". Koedinger et al. 2023: a student at the 25th percentile of prior knowledge needs about 13 practice opportunities to reach 80% where one at the 75th needs about 4.
  - **Caveats:** The 4% figure rests on eight studies. Time for correctives was often left unmeasured, which is one of von Hippel's points about the Bloom studies.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Kulik1990] [Cook2013] [Bloom1984] [Koedinger2023] [vonHippel2024]
- **Finding:** The working parts of mastery learning, as its main advocate describes them.
  - **Evidence:** Guskey's account (secondary summaries): pre-assessment of prerequisites, high-quality initial instruction, a formative check, feedback that tells each student what is still to learn, corrective activities that teach the material a different way, enrichment for students who have mastered it, and a second parallel assessment.
  - **Caveats:** Guskey's own passages quoted on a secondary page tie correctives to "learning styles", an idea the Pashler 2008 review found unsupported.
  - **Grade:** D
  - **Sources:** [Guskey2007] [Pashler2008]

### Tutoring effects, human and machine

- **Finding:** Bloom's two sigma result is real as reported, and it measures a bundle on a narrow test over three weeks.
  - **Evidence:** Bloom 1984: two doctoral students (Anania; Burke) randomly assigned students in grades 4, 5 and 8 to conventional classes of about 30, mastery-learning classes of about 30, or tutoring (one tutor to one, two or three students). Subjects were probability and cartography, chosen because they were new to the students. Each sub-study ran 11 periods over 3 weeks. The average tutored student scored about 2 SD above the control mean (above 98% of control students); the average mastery-learning student about 1 SD above (84%). About 90% of tutored and 70% of mastery students reached the level of the top 20% of the control class. Time on task was 65% (conventional), 75% (mastery) and over 90% (tutoring). The tutoring arm also used formative tests, feedback, correctives and parallel retests.
  - **Caveats:** von Hippel, who read both dissertations: the tests covered only the three weeks of content; tutored students who scored below 80% (Anania) or 90% (Burke) on a unit quiz got feedback and a retest the control class never got, and in week one of Anania's study they scored 11 percentage points higher on the retest; tutors received training the other instructors did not get; the mastery arm alone scored about 1.1 SD above whole-class instruction, which leaves about 0.9 SD for tutoring. Bloom's own source table (Walberg) put tutoring at 0.40. A 1982 meta-analysis found a mean tutoring effect of 0.33 SD across 65 studies, 0.84 on narrow tests and 0.27 on standardized tests. Among 96 randomized tutoring studies in a 2020 review, the two sigma mark was reached by zero. The von Hippel article appeared in an edited policy magazine and has had no peer review.
  - **Grade:** B for the original experiments (small, randomized, unpublished in part, unreplicated); D for two sigma as the general effect of tutoring
  - **Sources:** [Bloom1984] [vonHippel2024]
- **Finding:** Across controlled comparisons, human tutoring is worth about 0.8 SD against the same content with no tutoring, and step-level tutoring systems come close.
  - **Evidence:** VanLehn 2011 (comparisons that held content equal): human tutoring against no tutoring d = 0.79 (10 comparisons); step-based systems d = 0.76 (28 comparisons, 68% reliable); substep-based systems d = 0.40 (26); answer-based systems d = 0.31 (165 studies, taken from an earlier review). Human tutoring against step-based systems d = 0.21. VanLehn's reading is an "interaction plateau": moving from feedback on the answer to feedback on each step raises the effect from about 0.31 to about 0.75, and finer interaction adds little.
  - **Caveats:** Ten comparisons carry the human tutoring figure. The answer-based figure is imported from a 1991 review of different studies; a secondary summary notes the two direct step-against-answer comparisons give 0.40.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [VanLehn2011]
- **Finding:** Meta-analyses of intelligent tutoring systems agree on a moderate effect against classroom teaching and about zero against human tutors, and they disagree sharply by test type and age group.
  - **Evidence:** Ma et al. 2014: 107 effect sizes, 14,321 participants; g = 0.41 overall (random effects); against teacher-led large-group instruction 0.42, against non-ITS computer-based instruction 0.57, against textbooks or workbooks 0.35, against small-group instruction 0.05, against individual human tutoring -0.11 (the last two not significant). Kulik and Fletcher 2016: 50 controlled evaluations, median 0.66 SD (50th to 75th percentile), 46 of 50 positive. By test type: local tests 0.73 (38 studies), mixed 0.45 (3), standardized 0.13 (9). By sample size: up to 80 participants 0.78, 81 to 250 0.53, over 250 0.30. Postsecondary 0.75, elementary and high school 0.44. Mathematics 0.40, other subjects 0.72. Steenbergen-Hu and Cooper 2013 (K-12 mathematics, 26 reports, 34 samples): g = 0.01 to 0.09, larger for interventions shorter than a school year and for general students than for low achievers. Steenbergen-Hu and Cooper 2014 (college, 35 reports, 39 studies, 22 systems): g = 0.32 to 0.37; ITS did worse than human tutoring and better than every other comparison; earlier studies showed larger effects than recent ones.
  - **Caveats:** Kulik and Fletcher read the test-type gap as an alignment effect: standardized tests do not sample what the system taught. The sample-size gradient is also what small-study bias looks like. Six evaluations with non-conventional control groups and four with flawed implementations showed small effects and were kept out of the main analysis.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Ma2014] [KulikFletcher2016] [SteenbergenHu2013] [SteenbergenHu2014]
- **Finding:** Randomized trials of human tutoring programs pool to about 0.3 to 0.4 SD, and the effect shrinks as programs grow.
  - **Evidence:** Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan: pooled effect 0.288 SD (SE 0.029) in the 2024 journal version; 0.37 SD across 96 studies in the 2020 working paper. Effects are largest with teacher or paraprofessional tutors, in earlier grades, at three or more days a week, and during school. Working-paper moderators (secondary summaries): preschool and kindergarten 0.45, grade 1 0.42, grades 2 to 5 0.29, grades 6 to 11 0.16; teacher tutors 0.50, paraprofessionals 0.40, volunteers 0.21; one-to-one 0.38, one-to-two 0.29, one-to-three or more 0.36. Kraft, Schueler and Falken: 265 randomized trials from 1967 to 2023 in the working paper, 263 in the journal version; pooled effect 0.42 SD (working paper) or .404 (journal version), with a prediction interval for a single study from -0.31 to 1.16 (working paper) or -.16 to .96 (journal version). By number of students tutored (journal version): under 100, .506; 100 to 399, .302; 400 to 999, .258; 1,000 or more, .155. For programs in the United States judged on independent standardized tests (working paper): 0.21 SD at 400 to 999 students and 0.16 SD at 1,000 or more. The journal version gives .22 and .16 for programs in high-income countries judged on independent tests.
  - **Caveats:** The populations are children. Secondary summaries of Nickow report that nearly half the studies involve first graders, about 80% are literacy, only 7% reach grade 6 or above, and every outcome was measured within three months of the end of tutoring. Literacy tutoring in elementary grades makes up 73% of the estimates in the Kraft working paper. The estimate for programs of 1,000 or more students is imprecise.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Nickow2024] [Kraft2024]
- **Finding:** High-dosage tutoring for adolescents works at about 0.1 to 0.4 SD depending on how it is counted.
  - **Evidence:** Guryan et al. 2023 (Saga Education, Chicago, grades 9 and 10): 50 minutes of mathematics tutoring every school day, two students per paraprofessional tutor. Study 1 (2,633 students): intention-to-treat 0.09 SD, treatment-on-the-treated 0.18 SD, math grades up 0.57 points on a 4-point scale, math course failures down 48%. Study 2 (2,710 students): treatment-on-the-treated 0.40 SD. Gains of 0.23 SD were still present in grade 11. Cost about $3,500 per student per year, with a stated range of $3,200 to $4,800 (published version; the 2021 working paper abstract gave $3,500 to $4,300).
  - **Caveats:** Low-income urban high school students. The two studies differ by a factor of two with the same program.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Guryan2023]
- **Finding:** What an honest expected effect looks like, by comparison group.
  - **Evidence:** Against business-as-usual instruction on an independent or standardized test: 0.13 (ITS, standardized tests), 0.01 to 0.09 (ITS, K-12 mathematics), 0.16 to 0.21 (human tutoring at scale), 0.09 intention-to-treat and 0.18 to 0.40 for those who attended (Saga), +0.04 (group-based mastery learning in seven school studies with equal time, Slavin's median), 0.29 (mastery learning across 11 studies, Kulik's mean). Against business-as-usual on mixed or local tests: 0.288 to 0.42 (human tutoring trials), 0.42 (ITS against large-group teaching), 0.32 to 0.37 (ITS in college), 0.52 (mastery learning). Against no tutoring with the same content on researcher-built tests: 0.76 to 0.79 (step-based systems and human tutors), 0.73 (ITS on local tests). Against a human tutor: -0.11, not significant. Against another active strategy: 0.095 (quizzing against other elaborative strategies).
  - **Caveats:** von Hippel cites a review finding that most educational interventions produce effects of 0.1 SD or less, so 0.2 to 0.3 on an independent test is a strong result. Effects on narrow, aligned tests run about twice those on broad tests (Kraft 2020, cited by von Hippel). Adult and college samples tend to show larger ITS effects (0.75 postsecondary against 0.44 school-age in Kulik and Fletcher), and those are also the smaller studies with local tests.
  - **Grade:** A for the pattern
  - **Sources:** [KulikFletcher2016] [SteenbergenHu2013] [SteenbergenHu2014] [Kraft2024] [Guryan2023] [Slavin1987] [Nickow2024] [Ma2014] [Kulik1990] [VanLehn2011] [Yang2021] [vonHippel2024]

### Learning rate and time to mastery

- **Finding:** Students learn at nearly the same rate per practice opportunity and differ mainly in where they start.
  - **Evidence:** Koedinger et al. 2023: 27 datasets, 1.3 million observations, elementary to college courses in mathematics, science and language, all from online practice systems that give feedback and instruction on errors. Median starting accuracy after lectures and readings 65.42%. Median learning rate 0.09 log odds per opportunity, about 2.5 percentage points from the 65% starting point. Median opportunities to reach 80% correct: 7.24 (mean 12.27). By prior knowledge: 25th percentile starts at 55.21% and needs 13.13 opportunities; 50th starts at 66.05% and needs 6.54; 75th starts at 75.17% and needs 3.66. By learning rate with starting point held equal: 7.89, 7.27 and 6.94 opportunities.
  - **Caveats:** The regularity holds under favorable conditions: tasks grouped by a tested skill model, with feedback after every attempt. The authors show that a poor skill model hides the pattern. Mastery is defined as 80% correct, and the measure is accuracy on later practice opportunities inside the same system.
  - **Grade:** B (large observational modeling study)
  - **Sources:** [Koedinger2023]
- **Finding:** Tutoring systems have cut learning time by as much as two thirds in their best cases.
  - **Evidence:** Anderson et al. 1995: in best-case evaluations of the Cognitive Tutors (LISP, geometry, algebra) "students could achieve at least the same level of proficiency as conventional instruction in one third of the time". The best interaction style was immediate feedback with "short and directed error messages", and the tutors worked better when presented as tools than as imitations of human tutors. Corbett and Trask 2000: a programming tutor "was shown to speed learning by as much as a factor of three" (unconfirmed) with small, reliable test gains.
  - **Caveats:** "Best case" is the authors' phrase; early evaluations "usually, but not always" showed gains. The factor-of-three figure (unconfirmed) is for college students doing programming problems, as restated by Corbett and Trask from earlier work.
  - **Grade:** C
  - **Sources:** [Anderson1995] [CorbettTrask2000]
- **Finding:** More chances to respond per minute go with better outcomes, within limits set by the kind of response.
  - **Evidence:** MacSuga-Gage and Simonsen 2015 (systematic review of 15 studies of teacher-directed opportunities to respond): good outcomes appeared at about 3 to 5 opportunities per minute for spoken or gestured responses; studies where students wrote or typed their answers ran at about 1 per minute. Bloom: time on task was 65% in conventional classes and over 90% under tutoring. Rosenshine: effective teachers asked "a large number of questions" and checked every student's response.
  - **Caveats:** School-age classrooms, several in special education. The 3 to 5 figure describes the rates used in studies with good outcomes; the reviewers found zero studies that tested one rate against another. Studies at secondary level are scarce.
  - **Grade:** C
  - **Sources:** [MacSugaGage2015] [Bloom1984] [Rosenshine2012]

### Deliberate practice

- **Finding:** Deliberate practice is defined by four conditions, and accumulated hours of it track skill in music.
  - **Evidence:** Ericsson 2008: improvement came when people were "1) given a task with a well-defined goal, 2) motivated to improve, 3) provided with feedback, and 4) provided with ample opportunities for repetition and gradual refinements". After limited experience ("frequently less than 50 hours" for everyday activities) performance becomes automatic and more experience adds little. Ericsson et al. 1993: by age 18 the best violin students had accumulated a mean of 7,410 hours of solitary practice, the good students 5,301 and the future music teachers 3,420 (figures as restated in the 2019 replication paper).
  - **Caveats:** The 1993 hours are retrospective estimates from three groups of 10. A 2019 double-blind replication found a substantial effect (eta squared 0.26) that was "considerably smaller than the original", and did not reproduce the step-by-step match between hours and skill level. Teacher-designed practice explained no more than practice alone.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Ericsson2008] [Ericsson1993] [MacnamaraMaitra2019]
- **Finding:** Across domains, hours of deliberate practice explain a modest share of who performs best, and very little in education and at work.
  - **Evidence:** Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald 2014: 88 studies, 111 samples, 157 effect sizes, 11,135 participants. Variance explained, as corrected in the 2018 corrigendum: games 24% (r = .49), music 23% (r = .48), sports 20% (r = .45), education 5% (r = .22), professions 1% (r = .09, not significant). The 2014 paper printed 26% (r = .51), 21% (r = .46), 18% (r = .42), 4% (r = .21) and under 1% (r = .05). Practice explained more in highly predictable activities: 24% in the 2014 paper, 23% after the corrigendum (the corrected value is from a secondary summary of the corrigendum's table).
  - **Caveats:** These are correlations between hours and rank, so they speak to individual differences, and leave open how much any one person gains from practice. A corrigendum published in 2018 fixed a sample-size adjustment and moved each figure by one to two points. The authors say it left their conclusions as they were.
  - **Grade:** A (meta-analysis of correlational studies)
  - **Sources:** [Macnamara2014]

### Multimedia learning

- **Finding:** Mayer's design principles each show a medium to large median effect in his own experimental tests.
  - **Evidence:** Median d and tests supporting, as tabled in Mayer's summary chapter (based on Mayer 2021). Reducing extraneous load: coherence 0.86 (18 of 19), signaling 0.70 (26 of 28), redundancy 0.10 (8 of 12), spatial contiguity 0.82 (9 of 9), temporal contiguity 1.31 (8 of 8). Managing essential load: segmenting 0.67 (7 of 7), pre-training 0.78 (10 of 10), modality 1.00 (18 of 19). Fostering generative processing: personalization 1.00 (13 of 15), voice 0.74 (6 of 7), embodiment 0.58 (16 of 17), image 0.19 (4 of 7), generative activity 0.71 (37 of 44). The segmenting example is a narrated animation cut into 16 segments, each ended by a "continue" button the student presses.
  - **Caveats:** These are medians over experiments, mostly short lab lessons with immediate tests, many from one lab. Mayer lists boundary conditions: coherence effects are strongest for learners with low working memory capacity, for system-paced lessons and for highly interesting extras; signaling matters most when the learner might otherwise be lost. An earlier version of the same chapter carries different numbers for some principles.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Mayer2021]
- **Finding:** Independent meta-analyses confirm the direction with smaller sizes.
  - **Evidence:** Signaling (Schneider et al. 2018, 103 studies, 12,201 participants, secondary summaries): retention g = 0.53, transfer g = 0.33, cognitive load reduced g = 0.25, motivation g = 0.13, learning time up g = 0.30; color highlighting helped transfer and flashing hurt it; effective in every subject except mathematics. Segmenting (Rey et al. 2019, 56 investigations, 88 comparisons): "small to medium effects for retention and transfer", with lower cognitive load and longer learning time.
  - **Caveats:** Read through secondary summaries and an abstract.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Schneider2018] [Rey2019]
- **Finding:** Interesting but irrelevant additions lower learning.
  - **Evidence:** Sundararajan and Adesope 2020: 58 studies, 68 effects, more than 7,500 students; g = -0.33 (range -0.48 to -0.18). For learners with low prior knowledge -0.52 against -0.24 with prior knowledge. Worse for static details, details placed next to relevant diagrams, paper materials, and open-ended test questions.
  - **Caveats:** Most studies used lessons of 6 to 12 minutes in a lab. The authors raise a possible emotional benefit that the studies left unmeasured. Details that are interesting and relevant are outside the finding.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Sundararajan2020]
- **Finding:** Combining words with a matching graphic (dual coding) has moderate support.
  - **Evidence:** IES guide recommendation 3, moderate evidence. Weinstein et al. list dual coding among six strategies. Mayer's opening principle: people learn more deeply from words and graphics than from words alone. Deans for Impact: learning suffers when the two channels carry different content at the same time.
  - **Caveats:** Weinstein et al. keep dual coding separate from learning styles: the benefit applies to everyone.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Pashler2007] [Weinstein2018] [Mayer2021] [DeansForImpact2015]

### Retention and transfer

- **Finding:** Most studies test soon after teaching, and where later tests exist the methods that slow practice down come out ahead.
  - **Evidence:** In the Dunlosky study set, 74% of post-tests were within one day, 17% at 2 to 7 days, 3.3% at 8 days to a month, 0.4% at one to three months and 0.2% from 4 months to 7 years. Delayed results that exist: testing beat rereading at 2 days and 1 week after losing at 5 minutes (Roediger and Karpicke); the testing effect was larger at a day or more (Rowland, secondary summary); interleaved practice gave d = 0.83 one month later (Rohrer); mastery learning gave 0.71 SD about 8 weeks later (Kulik); Saga tutoring gains of 0.23 SD were present a year or two later (Guryan).
  - **Caveats:** Case comparison showed larger effects on immediate tests than delayed ones (Alfieri 2013). Secondary summaries of Nickow report all tutoring outcomes were measured within three months.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [DonoghueHattie2021] [RoedigerKarpicke2006] [Rowland2014] [Rohrer2020] [Kulik1990] [Guryan2023] [Alfieri2013]
- **Finding:** Effects of educational interventions commonly shrink after the intervention ends.
  - **Evidence:** Bailey et al. 2020 conclude that fade-out "is widespread, and often co-exists with persistence", that it is a real phenomenon and not a measurement artefact, and that persistence depends on the skills targeted and on what the later environment offers. One example in the review: effects of phonological awareness training fell by about half by a follow-up averaging 8 months later. They name three routes to lasting effects: skill building (simpler skills support later ones), sustaining environments, and institutional gateways. They add that memory research shows "savings": relearning something once mastered takes fewer trials.
  - **Caveats:** The review draws mostly on interventions with children. The skills most likely to last are ones that are changeable, fundamental, and would not have developed without the intervention.
  - **Grade:** A (review of experimental evidence)
  - **Sources:** [Bailey2020]
- **Finding:** "Transfer" covers nine separate dimensions, and a claim of far transfer has to say which ones were crossed.
  - **Evidence:** Barnett and Ceci 2002. Content, three dimensions: the learned skill (specific procedure, representation, or general principle), the performance change measured (speed, accuracy, or approach), and the memory demand (execute when prompted, recognize when to use, or recall unprompted). Context, six dimensions: knowledge domain, physical context, temporal context (time since training), functional context (academic or real-world purpose), social context (alone or with others), and modality. Their summary of a century of work: evidence for transfer "under some conditions is substantial, but critical conditions for many key questions are untested".
  - **Caveats:** A taxonomy, offered to settle a debate, with no effect sizes of its own.
  - **Grade:** D
  - **Sources:** [BarnettCeci2002]
- **Finding:** Training a general mental capacity does not carry over to unrelated skills.
  - **Evidence:** Sala et al. 2019, second-order meta-analysis: working-memory training improved memory tasks (near transfer, 99 effect sizes). For far transfer (reasoning, speed, language; 119 effect sizes) and for all programs together including video games, music, chess and exergames (233 effect sizes), effects were small or null, and "when placebo effects and publication bias were controlled for, the overall effect size and true variance equaled zero". Sala and Gobet 2019, as quoted in a secondary source: "school instruction and professional training should concentrate on domain-specific material".
  - **Caveats:** The evidence is about cognitive training programs, which is far transfer in its strongest sense. It leaves near transfer inside a subject untouched.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Sala2019] [SalaGobet2019]
- **Finding:** Transfer to new problems in the same domain can be taught, by comparison, by variation, by abstraction tied to examples, and by practice at choosing the method.
  - **Evidence:** Case comparison: d = 0.50 (CI 0.44 to 0.56) over 336 tests in 57 experiments, d = 0.60 at the level of experiments; asking for similarities and giving the principle after the comparison gave d = 1.18 over 29 tests. Testing: transfer d = 0.40, 0.58 with response congruency (Pan and Rickard). Interleaving trains the choice of strategy, which blocked practice hides (Rohrer; Weinstein). Bjork and Bjork list varied practice among the desirable difficulties. The IES guide recommends connecting abstract and concrete representations (moderate evidence). Deans for Impact: transfer needs knowledge of the problem's context and of its underlying structure, and examples alone make the shared structure hard to see. Anderson et al.: students transferred from the tutor to other settings "to the degree that they can map the tutor environment into the test environment".
  - **Caveats:** Worked examples alone produced skill that was specific to the example structure (Sweller and Cooper). Donoghue and Hattie found smaller effects for far than near transfer across all ten techniques. Productive failure favors transfer and conceptual outcomes and adds nothing for procedures.
  - **Grade:** A for comparison and testing, B for the rest
  - **Sources:** [Alfieri2013] [PanRickard2018] [Rohrer2020] [Weinstein2018] [BjorkBjork2011] [Pashler2007] [DeansForImpact2015] [Anderson1995] [SwellerCooper1985] [DonoghueHattie2021] [SinhaKapur2021]

### Motivation and persistence

- **Finding:** Self-determination theory holds that people stay engaged when three needs are met: competence, autonomy and relatedness. In student samples the more self-endorsed the motive, the better the outcomes.
  - **Evidence:** Ryan and Deci 2000 set out the three needs. Howard et al. 2021: 344 samples, 223,209 participants, mean age 16. Correlations with adaptive outcomes: amotivation -.24, external regulation -.01, introjected .17, identified .38, intrinsic .41. With effort: identified .51, intrinsic .54, external -.08. With intention to continue: identified .31, intrinsic .26, external -.02. With objectively measured achievement: intrinsic .13, identified .11 (self-reported grades: .32 and .29). External regulation went with lower well-being.
  - **Caveats:** Correlational, school and university students, a third of samples from physical education. The link to objective achievement is small.
  - **Grade:** A (meta-analysis of correlations), D for the theory paper
  - **Sources:** [RyanDeci2000] [Howard2021]
- **Finding:** Tangible rewards that people expect for taking part reduce their later free-choice engagement, and informative positive feedback raises it.
  - **Evidence:** Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999, 128 experiments. Free-choice persistence: all rewards d = -0.24 (101 studies), all tangible rewards -0.34 (92 studies), engagement-contingent -0.40, completion-contingent -0.36, performance-contingent -0.28. Positive feedback: +0.33 on free-choice behavior and +0.31 on self-reported interest.
  - **Caveats:** Tasks were ones people found interesting to begin with. Tangible rewards hurt children more than college students, and verbal rewards helped children less.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Deci1999]
- **Finding:** The ARCS model (attention, relevance, confidence, satisfaction) is a design checklist with supportive but weak-quality evidence.
  - **Evidence:** Keller 1987 presents the four conditions and a design process, field tested in two in-service teacher programs, and says it "warrants more controlled studies". Goksu and Islam Bolat 2021: 38 controlled studies, 110 effect sizes, 8,690 students; achievement ES = 0.74, motivation ES = 0.43.
  - **Caveats:** The meta-analysts warn that their result averages high-quality and low-quality studies, state that it does "not represent the strength of the evidence", and recommend a replication that weighs study quality. A secondary summary notes that most of the motivation studies built ARCS into computer-based lessons.
  - **Grade:** B for the meta-analysis, D for the model
  - **Sources:** [Keller1987] [Goksu2021]
- **Finding:** Specific, difficult goals raise performance over "do your best", and less so on complex tasks.
  - **Evidence:** Locke and Latham 2002, summarizing 35 years: goal difficulty d = .52 to .82; specific difficult goals against do-your-best d = .42 to .80; on the most complex tasks .41 against .77 on the simplest. On complex tasks the effect depends on finding a workable strategy.
  - **Caveats:** Mostly work and lab tasks. The numbers are the authors' summary of earlier meta-analyses.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [LockeLatham2002]
- **Finding:** Gamification shows small positive effects, and only the effect on learning outcomes holds up in the better studies.
  - **Evidence:** Sailer and Homner 2020, 38 publications and 40 experiments: cognitive outcomes g = .49 (CI 0.30 to 0.69, 19 studies, N = 1,686), motivational g = .36 (16 studies, N = 2,246), behavioral g = .25 (9 studies, N = 951). Game fiction and competition combined with collaboration moderated behavioral outcomes.
  - **Caveats:** Motivational and behavioral effects were unstable in the high-rigor subset. Studies ran from 2013 to 2017 with small samples.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [SailerHomner2020]
- **Finding:** Growth mindset interventions have small average effects that shrink further in the best-designed studies.
  - **Evidence:** Sisk et al. 2018: mindset and achievement correlate r = .10 (273 effect sizes, N = 365,915); interventions d = 0.08 (43 effect sizes, N = 57,155); d = 0.34 for low-income students on seven effect sizes; the correlation is r = .02 in adults (detail numbers first taken from a secondary summary and confirmed in the paper's full text in the cleanup pass on 2026-11-22). Macnamara and Burgoyne 2023: 63 studies, N = 97,672, d = 0.05 (CI 0.02 to 0.09), not significant after correction for publication bias; 0.04 in the 13 studies that showed mindsets changed; 0.02 in the 6 highest-quality studies; authors with a financial stake reported larger effects. Yeager et al. 2019: 12,490 ninth graders in 65 schools, a session of under one hour; lower-achieving students gained 0.10 grade points (standardized 0.11).
  - **Caveats:** Yeager et al. preregistered the lower-achieving subgroup and found effects where peer norms supported the message. The populations are adolescents.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Sisk2018] [MacnamaraBurgoyne2023] [Yeager2019]
- **Finding:** Asking learners to write about how the material is useful to them helps those who expect to do poorly, and the effect is small and conditional at scale.
  - **Evidence:** Hulleman and Harackiewicz 2009: randomized within classrooms, 262 ninth-grade science students (136 relevance, 126 control), a mean of 4.7 short essays over a semester; students with low success expectations gained interest and "nearly two thirds of a letter grade". Hulleman et al. 2010: two randomized experiments (lab and college classroom) found more perceived utility and interest, mainly for low performers. Kizilcec et al. 2020: 269,169 learners in 247 online courses; value-relevance writing raised completion for learners in less-developed countries by 2.79 and 2.74 percentage points in courses with a completion gap, and lowered it by 1.62 and 1.71 points in courses without one.
  - **Caveats:** In the 2009 trial the control group wrote summaries, so the comparison is relevance writing against summary writing. At scale the effect was "an order-of-magnitude smaller" than in the earlier small studies.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [HullemanHarackiewicz2009] [Hulleman2010] [Kizilcec2020]
- **Finding:** Most people who start a free online course do not finish, and light-touch nudges have not changed that.
  - **Evidence:** Jordan 2014: the average MOOC enrolled about 43,000 and 6.5% completed (completion data for 42 courses); completion fell as course length rose. Reich and Ruiperez-Valiente 2019: 565 course runs, 12.67 million registrations, 5.63 million learners at Harvard and MIT; 52% of registrants never entered the course; second-year return fell from 38% to 7% across cohorts; completion did not improve in six years (3.13% of all participants in 2017-18 and 46% of paying "verified" learners, as reported by Inside Higher Ed from the paper's figure). Kizilcec et al. 2020: a planning prompt had raised completion from 14% to 18% in a pilot of 2,053; at scale it raised engagement for 1 to 2 weeks and left final completion unchanged. Kulik et al. 1990: self-paced mastery courses lowered completion in college.
  - **Caveats:** MOOC registrants include many with no plan to finish. Paying learners completed at 46% against 3.13% for all participants.
  - **Grade:** A
  - **Sources:** [Jordan2014] [Reich2019] [Kizilcec2020] [Kulik1990]
- **Finding:** Streak mechanics raise short-term return rates in one large consumer product, by the vendor's own tests.
  - **Evidence:** Duolingo engineering blog (vendor report): learners with a 7-day streak were 2.4 times more likely to return the next day; letting one lesson extend the streak (separated from the daily goal) raised day-14 retention by 3.3%, daily active learners by 1%, and the share of new learners on a streak by 19%; a new streak animation raised day-7 retention of new learners by 1.7%; allowing two "streak freezes" raised daily active learners by 0.38%. A product manager interview summary adds that a third freeze added nothing.
  - **Caveats:** Vendor A/B tests, relative changes, outcome is app usage and not learning. The same post notes fewer learners reached their daily goal once the streak no longer required it, and that losing a streak can demotivate. The 2.4 figure is a correlation.
  - **Grade:** C
  - **Sources:** [Duolingo2020] [Duolingo2022] [DuolingoInterview]
- **Finding:** Several teaching choices covered earlier double as persistence levers.
  - **Evidence:** Active learning cut failure from 33.8% to 21.8% (Freeman). Mastery learning improved attitudes toward content and instruction (Kulik). Conversational wording carries a median d = 1.00 for learning in Mayer's tests, which he attributes to social presence. Classroom quizzing worked equally at low and high stakes (g = 0.477 and 0.441), and Yang et al. count motivation among three explanations for the classroom effect.
  - **Caveats:** Only the failure-rate figure measures persistence directly.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Freeman2014] [Kulik1990] [Mayer2021] [Yang2021]

### Myths to flag

- **Finding:** The experiments needed to justify matching instruction to a learner's preferred "style" have mostly never been run, and the few sound ones point the other way.
  - **Evidence:** Pashler et al. 2008 set the test: sort learners by style, randomly assign teaching methods, give everyone the same test, and show that the best method differs by style. They found people do state preferences and do differ in aptitudes, and "virtually no evidence" of the required interaction; of the few studies with a proper design, several contradicted the matching idea. Their conclusion: "no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice". Weinstein et al. and Deans for Impact repeat the verdict.
  - **Caveats:** The authors add that many versions of the idea have simply never been tested properly.
  - **Grade:** A (systematic review)
  - **Sources:** [Pashler2008] [Weinstein2018] [DeansForImpact2015]
- **Finding:** The "learning pyramid" percentages (10% of what we read up to 90% of what we teach) trace back to assertion, and the organization credited with them cannot locate a source study.
  - **Evidence:** Letrud and Hernes 2018 traced versions of the pyramid back more than 160 years and conclude the models "did not originate from empirical research" and conflict with the standard picture of memory. A practitioner summary adds: Dale's 1946 "cone of experience" carried no numbers; the percentages first appear in a 1967 trade magazine article with no method; the National Training Laboratories told researchers they "can no longer locate the source".
  - **Caveats:** The history detail is from a secondary practitioner page. The underlying point that doing beats reading has real support elsewhere in this file, at very different sizes.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [LetrudHernes2018] [DaleConeSummary]
- **Finding:** The claim that attention collapses 10 to 15 minutes into a lecture traces to a paper about note-taking.
  - **Evidence:** Bradbury 2016: teaching guides all cite one 1978 review whose subject is note-taking; studies that did try to measure attention have serious method problems; "the available primary data do not support the concept of a 10- to 15-min attention limit"; the largest source of variation in attention is the teacher. Wilson and Korn 2007 reached the same conclusion (via secondary summary). A clicker study of chemistry students found attention alternating in short cycles, with lapses of a minute or less most common, and fewer lapses after quiz questions and demonstrations (abstract).
  - **Caveats:** Attention does vary and lapse; the myth is the fixed number. Mayer's segmenting principle rests on learner pacing, not on a clock.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Bradbury2016] [WilsonKorn2007] [ClickerAttentionStudy]
- **Finding:** Two more popular numbers need the same care: "10,000 hours" and "two sigma".
  - **Evidence:** The 10,000 figure is an extrapolation to age 20 for the best group in one retrospective study of 30 violin students (practitioner summary); across domains practice hours explain 24% of variance at most (corrected figure, 26% in the 2014 paper). Two sigma is covered under tutoring above.
  - **Caveats:** Both started from real data and were stretched in retelling.
  - **Grade:** B
  - **Sources:** [Ericsson1993] [TenThousandHoursSummary] [Macnamara2014] [vonHippel2024]

## Disagreements and open questions

- **Which quiz format teaches most.** Rowland (lab) finds recall formats beat recognition, which fits the idea that effortful retrieval is what helps. Adesope finds multiple choice (0.70) ahead of short answer (0.48). Yang (classrooms) finds multiple choice works (0.567) and free recall does worst (0.238). The likely link is success rate: hard formats with low success and no feedback teach little. None of the three tested that link directly. [Rowland2014] [Adesope2017] [Yang2021]
- **Whether feedback is needed.** Rowland (0.73 against 0.39) and Yang (0.537 against 0.374) say feedback adds a lot. Adesope finds 0.63 against 0.60. Rowland's own split by success rate suggests feedback matters most when the learner gets many items wrong. [Rowland2014] [Yang2021] [Adesope2017]
- **One test or many.** Adesope finds one practice test (0.70) ahead of two or more (0.51). Yang finds the effect rising with the length of quizzing across a course, and Latimier finds repeated retrieval works better spaced than massed. [Adesope2017] [Yang2021] [Latimier2021]
- **Retrieval practice with complex material.** Van Gog and Sweller say the testing effect fades or vanishes for material with many interacting elements, and that worked examples are the better tool there. Karpicke and Aue say complexity was never measured, and the cited studies show small positive effects. Classroom data show quizzing helps problem-solving outcomes (0.453), and the transfer meta-analysis finds the weakest transfer for worked-example problems. One reading that fits all four: recalling facts about a procedure is not practice at the procedure, while solving problems is. [VanGogSweller2015] [KarpickeAue2015] [Yang2021] [PanRickard2018] [RetrievalThesis]
- **Self-explanation prompts on worked examples.** The self-explanation meta-analysis finds prompts help across task types (0.55). The worked-example meta-analysis finds that examples with prompts did worse than examples alone. The second is a between-study comparison, the first often lacks control of time. Open. [Bisra2018] [Barbieri2023]
- **Tell first or let them try first.** Unassisted discovery loses clearly (-0.38). Problem solving followed by instruction wins for conceptual and transfer outcomes (0.36, or 0.25 in randomized studies) and ties for procedures. Both sides agree instruction has to arrive; the dispute is the order, and the answer differs by outcome type and age. The productive failure estimate of 0.87 "after accounting for publication bias" is contested. [Alfieri2011] [Kirschner2006] [HmeloSilver2007] [SinhaKapur2021]
- **Desirable difficulty against high success.** Rosenshine reports an optimal success rate of about 80% during instruction. The desirable difficulties account favors conditions that lower performance during practice. Rowland's data put a floor under it: below about half correct with no feedback, retrieval practice taught nothing measurable. Where the best point lies between 50% and 80% for a given learner is open in these sources. [Rosenshine2012] [BjorkBjork2011] [Rowland2014]
- **How big mastery learning is.** 0.52 SD (Kulik) against 0.04 SD on standardized tests with time held equal (Slavin). The two differ by which tests count and whether extra time is allowed. [Kulik1990] [Slavin1987]
- **Two sigma.** Bloom's figure stands 2.5 to 12 times above every later pooled estimate of tutoring (0.79, 0.37, 0.288, 0.16 at scale). von Hippel attributes the gap to narrow tests, short duration, bundled mastery quizzes and trained tutors. [Bloom1984] [vonHippel2024] [VanLehn2011] [Nickow2024] [Kraft2024]
- **How good tutoring systems are.** Median 0.66 SD (Kulik and Fletcher) against 0.01 to 0.09 for K-12 mathematics (Steenbergen-Hu and Cooper 2013). Inside one meta-analysis the effect is 0.73 on local tests and 0.13 on standardized ones, and 0.78 in small studies against 0.30 in large ones. Whether the low numbers reflect misaligned tests or the high numbers reflect small-study bias is unresolved. [KulikFletcher2016] [SteenbergenHu2013] [Ma2014]
- **The spacing rule of thumb.** The same experiment is summarized as 5 to 10%, 10 to 20%, and 20 to 40% falling to 5 to 10% of the retention interval. For retention of a few weeks the reported best gaps were a quarter to a third of the delay (11 days for 35, 21 days for 70). [Cepeda2008] [Carpenter2012] [RohrerPashler2010]
- **How much of the interleaving effect is spacing.** Interleaved assignments also spread each skill over time. The classroom trial equated the final review and the test delay and still cannot separate the two. [Rohrer2020] [BrunmairRichter2019]
- **Whether the doer effect is causal.** The within-unit analysis rules out a general diligence trait. The authors themselves say it stops short of proof. A randomized test of adding practice at the point of reading was not among the sources I found. [Koedinger2016] [VanCampenhout2023]
- **Equal learning rates.** Koedinger et al. find students nearly equal in learning rate under good practice conditions. That sits uneasily with everyday experience and with the small share of variance that practice hours explain in education (5% after the 2018 corrigendum, 4% in the 2014 paper). The two can both hold if prior knowledge drives most differences, which is what the PNAS data show. The 27 datasets are mathematics, science and language practice scored right or wrong inside online systems, so open-ended work such as writing code stays an open question. [Koedinger2023] [Macnamara2014]
- **Mayer's medians against independent estimates.** Signaling is 0.70 in Mayer's tests and 0.53 (retention) or 0.33 (transfer) in an independent meta-analysis. [Mayer2021] [Schneider2018]
- **Growth mindset.** A preregistered national trial finds a small real effect for lower-achieving adolescents. A meta-analysis that grades study quality finds about zero in the best studies. Both agree the average effect is small. [Yeager2019] [MacnamaraBurgoyne2023] [Sisk2018]
- **Small motivation studies shrink at scale.** Planning prompts went from a 29% relative gain in a pilot to zero at scale; value-relevance went to "an order-of-magnitude smaller". [Kizilcec2020]
- **Adults are thin in the evidence.** In the Dunlosky study set 65% of participants were undergraduates and 2% other adults. Tutoring trials are almost all with children, half of them first graders. The adult data here are college ITS studies (0.32 to 0.37; 0.75 postsecondary), health professionals in simulation training (ES 1.17 for mastery against non-mastery), the LISP and programming tutors (one third of the time), and goal-setting research at work. A press summary of Macnamara et al. names computer programming among the professions where practice hours explained under 1% of performance differences (1% after the 2018 corrigendum, still not significant). [DonoghueHattie2021] [Nickow2024] [SteenbergenHu2014] [KulikFletcher2016] [Cook2013] [Anderson1995] [LockeLatham2002] [Macnamara2014]
- **The retention horizon is short.** Three quarters of technique studies test within a day. The longest delays in this file are one month (interleaving), about 8 weeks (mastery learning), one year (the spacing experiment) and one to two years (Saga tutoring). [DonoghueHattie2021] [Rohrer2020] [Kulik1990] [Cepeda2008] [Guryan2023]
- **Subject coverage.** Worked examples, interleaving and productive failure evidence is mostly mathematics and science. Programming appears only in the Cognitive Tutor work. [Barbieri2023] [Rohrer2020] [SinhaKapur2021] [Anderson1995] [CorbettTrask2000]

## What the sources themselves recommend

These are the sources' own prescriptions, each attributed. They are recorded as evidence of what the authors conclude.

**On practice and review**

- **Dunlosky et al.:** use practice testing and distributed practice; use practice testing "instead of rereading". [Dunlosky2013]
- **IES practice guide (Pashler et al. 2007):** review key content "after a delay of several weeks to several months"; alternate worked solutions with problems to solve; combine graphics with verbal descriptions; connect abstract and concrete representations; use quizzes to re-expose students to key content; ask deep explanatory questions. [Pashler2007]
- **Carpenter et al. 2012:** start lessons with a brief review of concepts learned several weeks earlier; put questions on earlier material into homework; make exams and quizzes cumulative. [Carpenter2012]
- **Carpenter et al. 2022:** give enough practice inside each session, and leave enough time between sessions that the material is "still familiar but not fresh in mind". [Carpenter2022]
- **Rohrer and Pashler 2010:** for very long retention, revisit material at least a year after first exposure. [RohrerPashler2010]
- **Latimier et al.:** space out retrieval practice on the same content; there is no need to make the intervals expand. [Latimier2021]
- **Yang et al.:** give corrective feedback after class quizzes, quiz after class more than before it, match quiz and exam formats, and keep quizzing through the whole course. [Yang2021]
- **Pan and Rickard:** to get transfer from testing, combine three things: final tasks that call for the practiced responses, retrieval practice with elaboration (such as explaining or feedback with explanations), and high success on the practice tests. [PanRickard2018]
- **Brunmair and Richter:** use interleaving for categories that are hard to tell apart; be cautious with expository texts and words. [BrunmairRichter2019]
- **Rohrer et al. 2020:** arrange mathematics assignments so that "no 2 consecutive problems require the same strategy". [Rohrer2020]
- **Bjork and Bjork:** vary the conditions of practice, interleave, space, and use tests as learning events, and make sure the learner has the background to respond successfully. [BjorkBjork2011]
- **Soderstrom and Bjork:** judge instruction by long-term retention and transfer, since performance during training misleads teachers and learners alike. [SoderstromBjork2015]
- **Ericsson:** set a well-defined goal just beyond current performance, give feedback, allow repetition and refinement, and keep raising the standard so the skill does not settle into automatic routine. [Ericsson2008]
- **MacSuga-Gage and Simonsen:** raise the rate of opportunities to respond. [MacSugaGage2015]

**On explaining and guiding**

- **Rosenshine:** review first, teach in small steps with practice after each, ask many questions, model, guide practice, check understanding, aim for a high success rate, scaffold, require independent practice, review weekly and monthly; reteach when checks show errors. [Rosenshine2012]
- **Kirschner, Sweller and Clark:** give novices strong guidance; reduce it as prior knowledge grows. [Kirschner2006]
- **Alfieri et al. 2011:** add feedback, worked examples, scaffolding and elicited explanations to any discovery task. [Alfieri2011]
- **Barbieri et al.:** use correct worked examples for first learning or practice; pairing them with self-explanation prompts "may not be a fruitful design modification". [Barbieri2023]
- **Bisra et al.:** prompt self-explanation; explore computer-generated prompts; report time on task in studies. [Bisra2018]
- **Chi and Wylie:** design activities that move learners from passive toward constructive and interactive engagement. [ChiWylie2014]
- **Sinha and Kapur:** use problem solving before instruction for conceptual understanding and transfer, built to productive failure design principles; teach first for younger children and for domain-general skills. [SinhaKapur2021]
- **Alfieri et al. 2013:** ask learners to find similarities between cases and give the principle after the comparison. [Alfieri2013]
- **Deans for Impact:** define key terms before the lesson; use worked examples with an explanation for each step; give immediate feedback during targeted skill practice and delayed feedback once students are fairly proficient. [DeansForImpact2015]
- **Mayer:** remove extraneous material, signal the structure, place words next to graphics, segment into learner-paced parts, pre-train key terms, use conversational wording, and prompt generative activity. [Mayer2021]
- **Sundararajan (author's summary):** keep engaging details and make them relevant to the content; novices are hurt most by irrelevant ones. [Sundararajan2020]

**On mastery and tutoring**

- **Bloom; Guskey:** formative test, feedback, corrective work in a different form, then a parallel retest; enrichment for those who pass. [Bloom1984] [Guskey2007]
- **Slavin:** evaluate mastery programs on broad measures and with instructional time held equal. [Slavin1987]
- **Anderson et al.:** give immediate feedback in "short and directed error messages"; present the tutor as a tool for learning, not as an imitation of a human tutor; build instruction around a model of the skill's component rules. [Anderson1995]
- **VanLehn:** feedback and hints at the level of each step capture most of the benefit of tutoring; finer interaction adds little. [VanLehn2011]
- **Kulik and Fletcher:** align the outcome test with what the system teaches, and attend to implementation quality when reading evaluations. [KulikFletcher2016]
- **Nickow et al.; Kraft et al.:** tutoring works best with teacher or paraprofessional tutors, three or more sessions a week, during the school day, at ratios of 3 to 1 or lower, with at least 15 hours in total and a provided curriculum; expect about 0.15 to 0.2 SD at scale. [Nickow2024] [Kraft2024]
- **von Hippel:** the benefit of tutoring depends on how much individual instruction and feedback students get, how much they practise, and the type of test. [vonHippel2024]
- **Koedinger et al. 2023:** conclude that lectures and readings alone leave the typical student below mastery (about 65% correct) and that practice with feedback is needed after them: about seven more opportunities per skill for the typical student and about thirteen for a student in the lower quarter of prior knowledge. [Koedinger2023]
- **Koedinger et al. 2015; Van Campenhout et al.:** conclude that formative practice at the point of learning produces larger gains than more reading or video. [Koedinger2015] [VanCampenhout2023]

**On transfer and durability**

- **Barnett and Ceci:** state which dimensions of content and context a transfer task crosses before calling it far transfer. [BarnettCeci2002]
- **Sala and Gobet:** teach domain-specific material; do not expect general cognitive training to carry over. [SalaGobet2019] [Sala2019]
- **Bailey et al.:** aim at skills that are changeable, fundamental and would not develop anyway; plan for an environment that sustains them. [Bailey2020]

**On motivation**

- **Ryan and Deci; Howard et al.:** support competence, autonomy and relatedness; help learners see the personal value of the work, since identified regulation is the motive most tied to persistence. [RyanDeci2000] [Howard2021]
- **Deci, Koestner and Ryan:** expected tangible rewards for engaging or finishing undermine later interest; informative positive feedback supports it. [Deci1999]
- **Keller:** design for attention, relevance, confidence and satisfaction, and sustain attention without overstimulating. [Keller1987]
- **Locke and Latham:** set specific, difficult goals and give feedback on progress toward them. [LockeLatham2002]
- **Hulleman and Harackiewicz:** have students write about how the material connects to their own lives. [HullemanHarackiewicz2009]
- **Sailer and Homner:** if using gamification, game fiction and competition combined with collaboration showed the best behavioral results. [SailerHomner2020]
- **Macnamara and Burgoyne:** test mindset interventions with active controls, manipulation checks, blinding and preregistration before relying on them. [MacnamaraBurgoyne2023]
- **Kizilcec et al.:** no single light-touch intervention lifts completion everywhere; test interventions in the setting where they will be used. [Kizilcec2020]
- **Duolingo (vendor):** make the streak easy to keep (one lesson) and allow a little slack (two freezes, not three). [Duolingo2020] [Duolingo2022] [DuolingoInterview]

**On myths**

- **Pashler et al. 2008:** spend resources on practices with strong evidence in place of learning-style assessment. [Pashler2008]
- **Bradbury:** do not set lesson length by a 10 to 15 minute attention limit; what the teacher does matters more. [Bradbury2016]
