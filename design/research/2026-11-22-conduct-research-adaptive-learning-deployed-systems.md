# Stream 9 - Deployed systems teardown: what real adaptive learning systems do, and what results they publish

## How I checked things

- **Date of the work:** 2026-11-22.
- **Scope:** vision question Q15 (which techniques deployed systems use and what results they publish) and the part of Q16 about how deployed systems gather their own evidence. The theory of knowledge tracing, mastery criteria, help and feedback sits in streams 3, 4 and 5. This file records what each named system does and what it has published.
- **Tools:** web search and web fetch. The search tool returns a short machine-written synthesis plus quoted page text, and for many pages it saves the full text. I took numbers only from quoted page text or saved full text. Where a number sat only in the synthesis line, the entry says "search summary, unconfirmed".
- **What I fetched:** 130 entries are in the source list. About a dozen of them group two to five pages from one vendor's help center or blog under one key. For each entry the list says what I read: "full text" (the whole document was available and I read it through), "full text (key sections)" (the whole text was available and I read the method, results and rule passages I quote), "search excerpt" (I saw only the passages the search tool quoted), "abstract only", or "secondary summary at URL".
- **Read in depth (the ones that matter most):** Fletcher and Morrison 2014 (the IDA report on the DARPA Digital Tutor), the What Works Clearinghouse 2016 report on Cognitive Tutor, Koedinger et al. 1997, Fancsali et al. 2021 and Ritter and Fancsali 2016 on MATHia, Falmagne et al. 2006 and the ALEKS student guide, Fang et al. 2019, Matayoshi et al. 2018, 2020 and 2025, Roschelle et al. 2016, the WestEd 2024 report on the ASSISTments replication, Settles and Meeder 2016, the Khan Academy help pages, Oreopoulos et al. 2026, Holt 2024, the Math Academy Way (Skycak), Kulik and Fletcher 2016, VanLehn et al. 2005 and 2007, the DataShop export and import format pages.
- **Grades:** A = meta-analysis or randomized controlled trial. B = controlled or quasi-experimental study, or a careful analysis of log data with a comparison. C = practitioner or deployed-system report, including a vendor's own documentation of how its product works. D = expert opinion or theory with no direct test.
- **Who ran it:** each result is tagged "independent" (evaluator separate from the developer, such as RAND, SRI, WestEd, IDA or a university group with no stake), "developer" (authors work for or built the system), or "vendor" (marketing or help-center material).
- **What stayed unconfirmed:**
  - Pane et al. 2014: I read the abstract, the RAND research brief, the addendum abstract and the What Works Clearinghouse write-up. The journal text itself sits behind a paywall. The exact first-year point estimates are missing from this file for that reason.
  - Pane et al. 2015 (Continued Progress): the RAND page gives the design and the direction of the result. The effect sizes are unconfirmed. One secondary blog gives 0.41 in mathematics and 0.29 in reading, and the lead in my brief gave 0.27 and 0.19. I saw neither pair on a RAND page.
  - Anderson et al. 1995 (Cognitive Tutors: Lessons Learned): abstract only. The LISP tutor figures of "30 percent faster" and "30 to 43 percent higher gains" come from a secondary wiki page.
  - The ASSISTments reassessment study: the 74 percent against 67 percent post-test figures and the 83.4 against 77.2 percent figures for personalized schedules appeared only in the search summary. The designs and the 0.27 effect size are confirmed.
  - Hays, Glick and Lane 2019 (Amplifire): the 48 and 32 percent reductions are in the abstract. The sample of 3,712 nurses and the rates 1.09 and 0.56 per 1,000 line-days appeared only in the search summary. Citation check: all three figures are in the paper's own text.
  - Cui, Xue and Thai 2018 (Squirrel AI): design, sample and mastery thresholds confirmed in the text. The headline effect sizes g = .68 and g = .49 appeared only in the search summary. Citation check: both are in the arXiv text, one for each of the paper's two experiments.
  - Maye et al. 2026 (spaced repetition meta-analysis in medical education): abstract only, and the pooled effect of 0.78 appeared only in the search summary. Citation check: the abstract gives a standardised mean difference of 0.78 (95 percent CI 0.56 to 0.99).
  - David Hu's 2011 Khan Academy post: seen only as an excerpt on the Khan Academy blog. The streak-of-10 history is recorded in the stream 3 file as secondary, and I did not improve on that.
  - Knewton alta: the Johns Hopkins analysis is listed on a Wiley resources page by title only. Its findings are unread.
  - Smart Sparrow's later history (sale of the platform in 2020) appeared only in my own search term, so it is left out.
  - Author lists marked "authors not captured" in the source list were missing from the page text I saw. Citation check: all but one of these were filled in from Crossref, arXiv or the document's first page. The AERA 2014 paper [ARRS2014] is still without named authors.
- **Not found at all:** a randomized trial of any adaptive system teaching working software engineers a programming tool, an independent outcome study of Execute Program, Exercism, Codecademy, Math Academy or Synthesis Tutor, and a published per-lesson authoring cost for ALEKS, Khan Academy or Duolingo.
- **Injection check:** every fetched page read as ordinary content. Zero pages tried to give instructions to a reader agent.

## Top findings

1. **Independent trials at scale find small effects, and they arrive late.** Cognitive Tutor Algebra I: a first-year effect indistinguishable from zero and 0.20 SD in year two for high schools (147 schools, RAND). ASSISTments: g = 0.18 on a standardized test (2,850 students, SRI) and g = 0.10 one year after use ended (5,991 students, WestEd). Khan Academy with weekly teacher coaching: 0.12 to 0.22 SD (University of Toronto and partners, an academic team whose lead author sat unpaid on Khan Academy's research advisory committee from 2021 to 2024). ALEKS across 15 studies: g = 0.08 fixed-effect, 0.10 random-effects and not significant. Grade A. [Pane2014] [RANDBrief2013] [Roschelle2016] [FengWestEd2024] [Oreopoulos2026] [Fang2019]
2. **The size of a published effect tracks who ran the study and which test was used.** Across 50 tutoring-system evaluations the average effect was 0.73 on locally developed tests and 0.13 on standardized tests. In What Works Clearinghouse data, developer-commissioned studies averaged 0.31 and independent studies 0.14 (unconfirmed). Within the same program, developer-commissioned studies came out about 1.7 times larger. Grade A. [KulikFletcher2016] [Wolf2020]
3. **The largest published result for adults is the DARPA Digital Tutor, and it is an outlier with 12 learners per group.** After 16 weeks, Navy recruits beat graduates of a 35-week course by 3.54 SD on a knowledge test and 7.98 SD on team troubleshooting, and beat technicians with 9.6 years of experience by 3.11 and 4.19 SD. Groups were selected, the tests were locally developed, and a later meta-analysis treated both Digital Tutor results as outliers. The one module built from lectures (security) came out level with the comparison groups. Grade B. The assessor was IDA, a federally funded research center that acted as third-party evaluator under a Defense Department contract. The program itself was funded by DARPA. [Fletcher2014] [KulikFletcher2016]
4. **The Digital Tutor was built by teaching the course with humans first.** About half the funding went to recruiting 24 expert tutors by audition and running a full 16-week human-tutored course, with every session recorded. Content authors (usually the tutors) and content engineers then turned the recordings into the tutor. Its rules: ask far more than tell, rarely give a direct hint, never state a misconception, verify understanding of any explanation before moving on, and call a human mentor when the dialogue fails. Grade C. [Fletcher2014]
5. **Deployed mastery rules are simple counters far more often than probabilities.** ASSISTments: three right in a row. ALEKS: a point counter to 3 or 5 where each right answer straight after another right answer counts double and a wrong answer subtracts one. Khan Academy: 70 percent for Familiar, 100 percent for Proficient, then a correct answer on a later mixed test for Mastered. IXL: a 0 to 100 SmartScore with 80 as proficient. Math Academy: pass each knowledge point within 2 to 5 questions. MATHia is the exception with Bayesian knowledge tracing at 0.95 for every skill in a workspace. Grade C. [ASSISTmentsSB] [Matayoshi2025] [KhanHelp] [IXLSmartScore] [MathAcademyWay] [Fancsali2021]
6. **Several systems split mastery into two steps: "learned now" and "confirmed later without help".** ALEKS marks a topic Learned in practice and Mastered only when it is answered on a later Knowledge Check. Khan Academy grants Mastered only from Proficient plus a correct answer on a unit test, course challenge or mastery challenge. ASSISTments retests at 7, 14, 28 and 56 days. Execute Program unlocks a lesson only after its prerequisites pass a review one day later. Grade C. [ALEKSGuide] [KhanHelp] [PASS2015] [Bernhardt2020]
7. **Every mature system caps struggle with a hard limit and then changes course.** MATHia stops a workspace at a maximum number of problems (often 25), moves the student on without mastery, and alerts the teacher: this happens in about 12 percent of sections, and the median workspace loses 4.3 percent of students this way. ASSISTments stops a Skill Builder at 10 problems per day. ALEKS ends a learning attempt after five wrong answers in a row and suggests another topic. Math Academy fails the lesson, switches to other topics, and after a second failure at the same point assigns review of the key prerequisite. Knewton alta steps back up to three prerequisite "hops". Grade C. [RitterFancsali2016] [Fancsali2021] [ASSISTmentsSB] [Matayoshi2025] [MathAcademyWay] [KnewtonAlta]
8. **Help is charged against mastery, at different prices.** ASSISTments marks a hinted problem wrong, or takes 0.33 off the score per hint, at the teacher's choice. MATHia lowers a skill estimate only when a hint gives away the answer, and treats errors as evidence against mastery. ALEKS charges nothing for viewing an explanation and withholds the double credit on the next right answer. Grade C. [Adjei2021] [ETRIALS2025] [MATHiaSupport] [Matayoshi2025]
9. **Placement by an adaptive test is standard, and testing out is cheap.** ALEKS opens with about 30 questions that place the student among tens of thousands of knowledge states. Khan Academy's 30-question course challenge levels up every skill answered right. Duolingo has a placement test and a per-unit jump-ahead test. Math Academy and i-Ready start with an adaptive diagnostic, and Squirrel AI gives a pretest before each lesson and teaches only what was missed. The Digital Tutor took a different route for adults: it held time fixed and gave fast learners harder and less assisted problems. Grade C. [ALEKSGuide] [Falmagne2006] [KhanHelp] [KhanVideo] [DuoPlacement] [MathAcademyWay] [iReady] [Cui2018] [Fletcher2014]
10. **Forgetting after in-session mastery is large and fast, and later related learning protects against it.** ALEKS measures retention with one random unscored "extra problem" per progress assessment. For topics at the edge of what the student knows, the correct rate falls steeply over the first days and levels near 0.6. For topics with five or more layers of later material built on them it stays near 0.8 (8.35 million extra problems from 6.7 million students). Grade B, developer. [Cosyn2021] [Matayoshi2018] [Matayoshi2020]
11. **Scheduled retests after mastery raise long-term retention.** In a within-student experiment (97 eighth graders, 32 skills), skills that got automatic reassessment and relearning beat mastery-only skills on an end-of-year test, most for weak students and hard skills. A personalized schedule beat the fixed 7-14-28-56 schedule for medium-knowledge students (effect size 0.27). Both studies were run by the ASSISTments group itself. Workplace logs from 10,514 employees show the best gap between reviews grows with the retention interval. Grade A and B. [ARRS2014] [PASS2015] [Kim2019]
12. **Duolingo's best-known number measures engagement against the full version of its own half-life regression model.** Half-life regression beat the Leitner system on prediction error by 45 percent or more, yet in the user experiment against Leitner it moved daily retention by +0.3 percent and cut practice sessions by 7.3 percent. The quoted 12 percent gain came from a trimmed model against the full model (3.3 million users, two weeks). The outcome in every published Duolingo product experiment I found is user retention or activity. Its test-score evidence comes from separate efficacy studies of self-selected course finishers. Grade A for the experiments, developer. [Settles2016] [Jiang2021] [DuoReview]
13. **Usage is the binding constraint in schools.** 4.7 percent of students met Khan Academy's 30 minutes a week in its 99-district study, and about 9 percent in its 2022-23 study. DreamBox students averaged 35 to 44 minutes against a recommended 60 to 90. ASSISTments students logged 14 hours against an expected 18 to 24. In the Khan Academy trial, gains appeared only in classrooms averaging 35 minutes a week or more, and middle-school classes at 7.8 minutes a week gained nothing. A three-year panel of over 200,000 students, co-authored by two Khan Academy employees, estimates 0.031 SD at 11 minutes a week and 0.085 SD at 30. Grade A and B. [Holt2024] [KhanEfficacy2024] [CEPR2016] [Hechinger2016] [Roschelle2016] [Oreopoulos2026] [KhanPNAS]
14. **Teachers override mastery, and the override hurts the weakest students.** In one district (49 schools, 280 classes, about 11,000 students) the median class violated Cognitive Tutor's mastery sequence 21 percent of the time (unconfirmed, the district size and the 21 percent were seen only in a search summary), mostly by moving students ahead to keep the class together. Violators made more errors and the gap grew over the year. Grade B, developer. [Ritter2016]
15. **Hand-authoring is the main cost, and published ratios are steep.** The historical estimate for an intelligent tutor is 200 to 300 hours of development per hour of instruction. Example-tracing tools cut large projects to roughly 50 to 100 hours per hour. A Smart Sparrow adaptive tutorial took 15 to 40 hours to build. Cognitive Tutor Algebra I cost schools $97.18 per student per year against $27.88 for the comparison curricula. Grade C. [Aleven2009] [CTATSlides2011] [BESTNetwork] [Daugherty2012]
16. **Model-generated content with human selection is now in production, and live generation is kept inside authored frames.** Duolingo built its first 100 courses in about 12 years and 148 more in under a year. Its designers plan the lesson and fill a prompt template, the model writes candidates, and for batch-generated content "the learner only sees the top X percent of output". Live features use scenarios, opening messages and direction written by people. Synthesis Tutor's engineers state that "no user ever sees live/unchecked LLM output". Grade C, vendor. [DuoLLM2023] [Duo148] [DuoUCFL2025] [DuoMax2023] [UnderstandReview]
17. **Crowdsourced hints from vetted teachers improved the next problem in a randomized test.** In ASSISTments, students randomly given a teacher-written hint or explanation (against being shown the answer) were more likely to get the next problem right on the first try and asked for less help on it. Randomization was 9 to 1 at the problem level. The published pilot covered 1,787 problems, and the project page counts 6,044 problems randomized by October 2021 in data it had yet to analyze. Grade A, developer. [Patikorn2020] [HeffernanTA]
18. **Pre-LLM dialogue tutors helped most when the material was above the student's level.** AutoTutor averaged about 0.8 SD over reading static text for the same time (developer review). In seven experiments run by the tutors' own developers, tutorial dialogue beat a well-written text only when novices studied content written for intermediates. Andes, which flags each step red or green and gives help only on request, reached 0.61 SD on instructor exams and 0.25 SD on a multiple-choice final at the US Naval Academy, in an evaluation by its developers with the course instructors. Grade A and B. [Nye2014] [VanLehn2007] [VanLehn2005]
19. **Confidence-based workplace systems rest on vendor and vendor-coauthored evidence.** Area9 reports that learners are "15 to 40 percent unconsciously incompetent" and claims up to 50 percent less learning time. The best study is a matched comparison of 177 physician users and 177 non-users (10.6 points higher pass likelihood, with users defined as those who finished at least 75 percent). Amplifire reports infection-rate drops of 48 and 32 percent after nurse training, in a before-and-after design. Grade B and C. [Area9] [Healy2020] [Hays2019] [AmplifireCases]
20. **Programming-specific platforms publish their mechanics and very little outcome data.** The best case for the LISP tutor and its sibling geometry and algebra tutors, by their developers' own account, was equal proficiency in one third of the time. SQL-Tutor checks a solution against constraints and tells the student which principle was violated, leaving the repair to the student. Execute Program, Exercism and Codecademy describe prerequisite graphs, spaced review and automated checks, and their public material stops at mechanics. Grade B for the tutors, C for the platforms. [Anderson1995] [Mitrovic2012] [Bernhardt2020] [ExercismDocs] [Codecademy]
21. **Deployed systems test their own rules inside the product.** Duolingo ships hundreds of A/B tests each week and reports about half succeed, and it ran its student model in "shadow mode" before using it. ALEKS randomly assigned 5 percent of topics to each of two mastery thresholds from April 2023 to November 2024. ASSISTments has produced more than 33 published randomized experiments through E-TRIALS. Carnegie Learning built the open-source UpGrade platform for MATHia. LearnLab ran over 360 in vivo experiments. Grade C. [DuoCiti2026] [DuoSpectrum] [Matayoshi2025] [ETRIALSsite] [UpGrade2020] [LearnLab]
22. **A random, unscored probe question is the cheapest validity check in use.** ALEKS adds one extra problem to each assessment and compares the answer with what the student model predicted (correlation .7 to .85). Khan Academy judged a new proficiency rule by the chance of getting the next problem right just after proficiency was granted. Math Academy checks lesson pass rates against later quiz results. Grade C. [Falmagne2006] [KhanHu] [MathAcademyWay]
23. **The DataShop transaction format is the field's common log.** Required columns are Anon Student Id, Session Id, Time, a Level column, Problem Name, and at least one of Step Name, Selection or Action. The import page also says a file without Problem View must give Problem Start Time. The useful optional ones are Outcome (CORRECT, INCORRECT or HINT), Input, Feedback Text, Help Level, Total Num Hints, Attempt At Step, Condition Name, KC and Event Type. The full list is in the evidence section. Grade C. [DataShopImport] [DataShopExport]
24. **Adaptive tutors work where a machine can mark the answer.** Reich's review limits the genre to mathematics, computer science and early language learning, and names four standing problems: schools bend new tools to old routines, the well-prepared benefit most, routine assessment narrows what is taught, and data and experiments can harm. Grade D. [Reich2020] [Reich2020LAS]

## Findings by system

### 1. Cognitive Tutor and MATHia (Carnegie Learning)

- **What it is:** a mathematics tutor for middle and high school, sold as part of a blended curriculum. The recommended split is about 60 percent of class time on teacher-led work with a worktext and 40 percent in the software, or two days a week in software and three days with the text. Hundreds of thousands of students use it each year. [Fancsali2021] [Ritter2016]
- **Sequencing:** content is ordered as modules, units and workspaces. A student has one position in the sequence and moves to the next workspace on mastery. Inside a "mastery workspace" the system holds a large pool of multi-step problems and, after each one, picks the next problem to exercise skills the student has yet to master. "Concept builder" workspaces are a fixed sequence of questions. Teachers can unlock modules and move students by hand. [MATHiaDataShop] [Fancsali2021] [RitterFancsali2016]
- **Student model:** each step of a problem maps to a knowledge component (skill). For each skill the system keeps a Bayesian knowledge tracing estimate with four parameters: already known, learn rate, guess and slip. The student sees the estimates as the Skillometer, a ring per skill that fills toward mastery. The vendor describes the ring as "a predictor of the probability that you will be able to demonstrate that skill again in the future", in contrast to a percent correct. [Fancsali2021] [MATHiaSupport]
- **Mastery criterion:** a skill is mastered when its estimate passes 0.95. A workspace is complete when every skill attached to it is mastered. [Fancsali2021] [MATHiaDataShop]
- **Stuck:**
  - Each step offers hints on request, generally in three levels: level 1 "goal setting" restates what the step asks for, level 2 "teach" explains how to do it, level 3 "bottom out" gives the answer. [MATHiaDataShop]
  - Known error patterns trigger a "just-in-time" message tied to that error (for example a known misconception, or typing a number that appears in the problem but is wrong). [MATHiaDataShop]
  - A right answer raises the skill estimate and a wrong one lowers it. A hint lowers it only when the hint gives away the answer to the step. [MATHiaSupport]
  - Each workspace has a maximum number of problems, "often 25". A student who reaches it without mastering every skill is moved to the next workspace anyway. Carnegie Learning calls this a "promotion", flags it, and reports it to the teacher. The stated reasoning: if a student cannot master the material in the tutor in reasonable time, "the tutor's mode of instruction for this topic is not resonating with the student", and the teacher should present another approach. [RitterFancsali2016] [Fancsali2021]
  - How often: students are promoted from "about 12 percent of sections". "Section" is the older name for "workspace" (footnote 1 on page 1 of the 2019-2020 DataShop documentation [MATHiaDataShop]). A 2020 paper with the same developers gives a like figure with its base: in the 2018-19 school year students failed to master the workspace in approximately 424,000 of approximately 3.78 million workspace completions, about 11.2 percent (Fancsali et al. 2020, which the adaptation-policies notes hold under the key Fancsali2020). Across 308 workspaces in 2018-19 the median workspace had 4.3 percent of students fail to master all skills (first quartile 0.7 percent, third quartile 12.1 percent, maximum 77.7 percent). The developers use this rate to pick which workspaces to redesign. [RitterFancsali2016] [Fancsali2021]
- **Ahead:** a student whose estimates pass 0.95 quickly sees fewer problems. Placement in the sources I read is a manual act: the teacher sets the student's position. [Fancsali2021] [RitterFancsali2016]
- **Review:** the sources I read describe mastery within a workspace and say little about scheduled review of earlier skills.
- **Content:** each tutor rests on a hand-built cognitive model (production rules for every correct and common incorrect step), hint texts for each step, and a skill map that developers refine over years using log data. Some skill models are "the result of years of iterative refinements". The classic estimate is 200 to 300 hours of development per hour of instruction. With example-tracing tools (CTAT), large projects ran at about 50 to 100 hours per hour, small ones were worse than the historical estimate (single projects ranged from 48:1 to 720:1), and the authors put the overall saving at 4 to 8 times once cheaper non-programmer authors are counted. [Fancsali2021] [Aleven2009] [CTATSlides2011]
- **Cost to schools:** $97.18 per student per year for Cognitive Tutor Algebra I against $27.88 for the comparison curricula, a gap of about $69. The parts: textbooks replaced each year ($21.55 against $11.28), software ($29.92 against $14.04), technology, and teacher training ($15.52 against $0.94). [Daugherty2012]
- **Published results:**
  - Koedinger et al. 1997 (developer, quasi-experimental, grade B): 470 ninth graders in 20 classes at three Pittsburgh high schools in 1993-94 used a new curriculum plus the tutor for about 25 of 180 class periods. Against 120 students in 5 matched comparison classes they scored 0.3 SD higher on the Iowa Algebra Aptitude Test, 0.3 SD higher on an SAT subset (not significant), 0.7 SD higher on a problem-situation test and 1.2 SD higher on a representations test. The authors' summary is "15 percent better" on standardized tests and "100 percent better" on tests aimed at the new curriculum. Curriculum and tutor were delivered together, so the study cannot separate them. [Koedinger1997]
  - Pane et al. 2014 (independent, RAND, randomized by school, grade A): 147 schools (73 high schools, 74 middle schools) in 51 districts in seven states, about 18,700 high-school and 6,800 middle-school students, two cohorts. Year one: posttest scores did not differ from control. Year two: about 0.20 SD for high schools, significant (0.21 in the addendum's alternative analysis), which moves the median student to the 58th percentile. Middle schools showed a similar size that was not significant. The outcome was a commercial standardized algebra test. [Pane2014] [RANDBrief2013] [PaneAddendum2014] [IESPane]
  - What Works Clearinghouse 2016 (independent review, grade A): six studies of Cognitive Tutor Algebra I met standards, with 12,840 students. Rating for algebra: "mixed effects", average improvement index +4 percentile points, range -7 to +19 across five studies and 12,182 students. One study was significantly positive (the 1993-94 Pittsburgh study, +19), one was "substantively important" and positive (+15, not significant), and three were indeterminate, including Pane 2014 high schools (+3 by the Clearinghouse's calculation, not significant after its adjustment) and a Maui study (-7). General mathematics: "no discernible effects" (one study, 658 students). Cognitive Tutor Geometry: "potentially negative effects", -8 percentile points (one randomized study, 669 students). [WWC2016]
  - Other trials quoted in a later review: 0.29 SD in a four-school randomized study (Morgan and Ritter 2002, a vendor report published by Carnegie Learning) and -0.19 SD for geometry in Baltimore (Pane et al. 2010). Secondary summary in [Oreopoulos2026].
  - Mastery in practice (developer, observational, grade B): in one large district (49 middle schools, 280 classes, about 11,000 students) the median class violated the mastery sequence 21 percent of the time (unconfirmed: the district size and the 21 percent were seen only in a search summary, and the published abstract confirms only the direction of the result). Classes that violated it made more errors, the error gap grew over the year, and lower-performing students were hit hardest. The reasons the authors list: teachers doubt mastery learning, trust their own judgment over the tutor's, or face pacing guides and benchmark exams that assume the whole class is on one topic. [Ritter2016] [RitterFancsali2016]
  - Vendor usage guidance: successful students "tend to spend 40 to 50 hours on a course, complete 1 to 2 workspaces per hour, and master 80 percent of the workspaces they encounter". [MATHiaAPLSE]
- **Criticisms and failures:** the first-year result was null in the largest trial. The geometry product tested negative. The What Works Clearinghouse rating is "mixed". The product costs about three and a half times the comparison curricula. Mastery pacing collides with whole-class pacing, and teachers resolve the collision by overriding the tutor. Promotion without mastery is routine in some workspaces. [Pane2014] [WWC2016] [Daugherty2012] [Ritter2016]

### 2. ALEKS (McGraw Hill)

- **What it is:** an assessment and learning system for mathematics and chemistry built on knowledge space theory (Doignon and Falmagne 1985, not fetched). Used in schools and colleges, and as a college placement test.
- **Student model:** a "knowledge state" is the full set of problem types a student can solve. Only some sets are feasible, because some problems presuppose others. For one beginning algebra structure of 88 problem types there are about 60,000 feasible states. Two short lists describe a state completely: the "inner fringe" (the most advanced problems the student can do) and the "outer fringe" (the problems the student is ready to learn). Together they average 10 to 15 problems. The structure comes from querying experts about which problems presuppose which, then checking against student data. [Falmagne2006]
- **Initial assessment:** about 30 questions. Students are not told whether each answer is right while it runs. Each question is chosen so that, on current evidence, the student has about a 50 percent chance of knowing it. A right answer raises the likelihood of every state that contains the problem and a wrong answer lowers it. The student can answer "I don't know", which shifts the likelihoods more strongly and shortens the test. The test stops when the uncertainty (entropy) over states is low and every remaining question is very likely or very unlikely to be answered right. In the paper's worked example, 24 questions picked one state out of 57,147. [ALEKSGuide] [Falmagne2006]
- **Sequencing:** after the assessment the student works in Learning Mode on topics from the "Ready to Learn" set (the outer fringe). The topic carousel is sorted from the easiest ready topic to the hardest by default, and the student may pick any of them or re-sort by content area. [ALEKSGuide]
- **Mastery criterion in Learning Mode (exact rule):**
  - Each topic opens with one worked example. Then the student gets practice instances. Three actions are possible: a right answer, a wrong answer, or opening the Explanation page.
  - The topic score starts at 0. A right answer adds 1. A right answer straight after another right answer adds 2. A wrong answer subtracts 1, with a floor of 0, and a second wrong try at the same question subtracts nothing more. Opening the Explanation changes nothing by itself, and the next right answer then adds only 1.
  - The student gets a new instance after a right answer, after an Explanation, or after two wrong answers in a row.
  - A topic the initial assessment classed as "unknown" needs a score of 5. A topic classed as "uncertain" needs a score of 3.
  - The developers describe these rules as having "evolved to their current form over time based on empirical evidence". [Matayoshi2025] [ALEKSGuide]
- **Learned against Mastered:** reaching the score makes a topic "Learned". It becomes "Mastered" when the student answers it correctly, without help, on a later Knowledge Check. [ALEKSKC] [ALEKSGuide]
- **Stuck:** the Explanation page shows a worked solution to the current instance at any time. Five wrong answers in a row count as a failed learning attempt, and "the student is gently prompted to try another topic". The theory's answer to difficulty is to review in the inner fringe. Teachers see reports. [Matayoshi2025] [Falmagne2006]
- **Ahead:** the initial Knowledge Check credits every topic it infers the student knows, so a strong student starts deep in the course. A right answer in a row counts double, so a student who knows a topic leaves it after three right answers (1 + 2 + 2 = 5) or two (1 + 2 = 3) at the lower threshold (my arithmetic from the rule). [Matayoshi2025]
- **Review:**
  - A Progress Knowledge Check of about 20 to 25 questions fires after the student has learned at least 20 topics and spent at least 5 hours in Learning Mode since the last check. The student has 24 hours to start it and is offered a review mode first.
  - Learned topics answered right on the check become Mastered. Learned topics missed are tagged "Needs More Practice" and are loaded at the front of the carousel. Students "may gain or lose topics", and the guide tells them this is normal.
  - Review mode sorts topics hardest first, "so topics the student is least likely to retain in an upcoming Knowledge Check are displayed first". [ALEKSGuide] [ALEKSKC]
- **Content:** a "problem type" is an abstract template and a student sees randomly drawn instances of it. Experts supply the prerequisite structure. The structure is monitored continuously: each assessment includes one extra problem that plays no part in choosing the state, and the correlation between the predicted and the observed answer to it "hovers between .7 and .85". Authoring cost is unpublished. [Falmagne2006]
- **Published results:**
  - Fang et al. 2019 (independent meta-analysis, grade A): 15 studies from 2005 to 2015, 24 independent samples, sample sizes 86 to 888, randomized and quasi-experimental designs. Of 24 effect sizes, 12 were positive and 12 negative. Overall g = 0.08, 95 percent CI [0.02, 0.15], p = .02 under a fixed-effect model, and g = 0.10, CI [-0.08, 0.27], p = .28 under random effects. The authors' reading: "as good, but not better than, traditional classroom teaching". Effects were larger for one semester of use (g = 0.17) than for a school year (g = -0.02) under the fixed-effect model. School level, role (main or supplementary instruction) and test type made no reliable difference. [Fang2019]
  - Forgetting (developer, log analysis, grade B): from 286,345 progress assessments, the correct rate on a previously learned topic falls fastest over the first few days and flattens between one and two weeks. From 8,352,006 extra problems answered by 6,701,233 students (2016 to 2019), retention depends on how deep the topic sits in the student's state: topics on the inner fringe (nothing learned on top of them) level off near 0.6, the next layer near 0.67, and topics five or more layers deep stay near 0.8. [Matayoshi2018] [Cosyn2021]
  - Retrieval practice (developer, log analysis of almost 4 million students, grade B): a delay before a topic is tested goes with better retention, and "the learning of closely related content is associated with an even higher rate of retention" than being assessed on the topic itself. [Matayoshi2020]
  - Mastery thresholds (developer, randomized inside the product, grade A): from April 2023 to November 2024, 5 percent of topic starts were randomly given the high threshold (5) and 5 percent the low threshold (3). The high threshold added about two learning actions, mostly right answers. The stream 3 file holds the retention numbers from the full text: about 0.02 higher retention on a base near 0.6 after several weeks, at a cost of 7.4 against 5.4 actions. [Matayoshi2025]
- **Criticisms and failures:** the pooled effect is close to zero against ordinary teaching. Students lose credited topics at each Knowledge Check, which the vendor has to explain to them. The model is tied to domains where problems can be machine-checked and ordered by prerequisite. [Fang2019] [ALEKSKC]

### 3. ASSISTments (Worcester Polytechnic Institute, now a nonprofit)

- **What it is:** a free online homework platform for middle-school mathematics. Teachers assign problems from open curricula or from "Skill Builders". Students get immediate right-or-wrong feedback and help on request, and teachers get a report by student and by problem with common wrong answers. More than 500,000 students and 30,000 teachers use it each year. The design goal is to assist the teacher, who decides what to assign. [Roschelle2016] [ETRIALS2025]
- **Sequencing:** the teacher picks the assignment. Inside a Skill Builder the problems for one skill arrive in random order from a pool of 20 to 100. [ASSISTmentsSB]
- **Student model:** a streak counter per assigned skill, plus a full log (problem, correctness, type of help, number of hints, seconds spent). [Adjei2021]
- **Mastery criterion (exact rule):** three problems right in a row. The number is set per problem set and the teacher can change it. On mastery the student is taken to their report and the assignment is done. [ASSISTmentsSB] [Adjei2021]
- **How help counts:** if a student asks for help, the system either marks the problem incorrect or gives partial credit by the number of help steps used, at the teacher's choice. Under the continuous score a problem is worth 1.0, 0.67, 0.33 or 0, losing 0.33 for each hint. In the reassessment studies "a student can no longer receive credit for a problem after they have used a hint". [Adjei2021] [ETRIALS2025] [WPIReport]
- **Stuck:**
  - Hints come "in increasing order of specificity", and the last one usually gives the answer (the bottom-out hint).
  - Scaffolding questions break the original problem into smaller steps answered in order, the last of which answers the main problem.
  - A student sees at most 10 problems of a Skill Builder in one day. Without three in a row by then, the system asks them "to take a break on the skill until the next day", when more problems open through the same link. The vendor gives the reason: "allowing for teacher intervention".
  - The teacher's report shows who is struggling and on which problems. [Adjei2021] [ASSISTmentsSB]
- **Ahead:** a student who knows the skill is done after three problems. Teachers choose what to assign, so acceleration is the teacher's call.
- **Review (ARRS, the Automatic Reassessment and Relearning System):** after mastery the skill is retested on an expanding schedule: 7 days later, then 14 days after passing that, then 28, then 56. Each retest is one problem without hints. A miss sends the student back to relearn the skill (three in a row again) before retaking the same level. The published study used 7, 14, 30 and 60 days. The Maine trial describes retention checks "at 1- and 2-week intervals". [PASS2015] [ARRS2014] [Roschelle2016]
- **Content:** WPI staff wrote the Skill Builders (more than 300 topics). Most other problems come from open curricula such as Illustrative Mathematics and EngageNY. Hints and explanations were first written by staff, then crowdsourced through TeacherASSIST: any teacher can write help for a problem, and help from vetted "starred" teachers (five in 2018, nine in 2019) is shown to all students. By the project's own count this covered 6,044 problems with 15,964 pieces of help. [Roschelle2016] [Patikorn2020] [HeffernanTA]
- **Published results:**
  - Roschelle et al. 2016 (independent evaluator SRI, school-randomized, grade A): 43 schools in Maine, 2,850 seventh graders (1,621 treatment, 1,229 control), delayed-treatment control doing homework as usual. Teachers had a year to learn the tool before the measured cohort. Treatment students scored 8.84 scale points higher on the TerraNova test, t(20) = 2.992, p = .007, Hedges's g = 0.18. For students at or below the median on prior achievement g = .29, above the median g = .12 (both are in the article text, but a 2017 corrigendum exists that reportedly concerns the subgroup estimates and I could not open it, so these two values are unconfirmed against the corrected record). Use averaged 14 hours and 967 problems over the year, against an expected 18 to 24 hours. Attrition was 10.1 percent overall and 5.6 points differential. The intervention was the tool plus teacher professional development, and the authors say they cannot yet name which part did the work. [Roschelle2016]
  - Feng, Huang and Collins 2023 and the WestEd 2024 report (independent, school-randomized replication, grade A): 63 schools in 41 North Carolina districts (32 treatment, 31 control), seventh grade in 2019-20. The pandemic cancelled the end-of-year test, so the outcome is the state grade 8 test one year after use ended: 5,991 students, 535.13 against 534.33, p = .011, g = 0.10. 21.45 percent of treatment students reached proficiency against 15.84 percent. The effect was larger for non-White students (differential impact g = 0.14) and Hispanic students (differential impact g = 0.13). About a third of students took an alternative course and had no grade 8 score (attrition 33.97 percent, differential 0.32 points). [Feng2023] [FengWestEd2024]
  - ARRS (developer, within-student randomization of skills, grade A, small): 97 eighth graders in one suburban school, 32 skills split into two sets, each student getting ARRS on one set and mastery-only on the other. End-of-year scores were higher on the reassessed skills, most for students with low pretest scores and for harder skills. Search summary, unconfirmed: 74 against 67 percent. [ARRS2014]
  - Personalized schedules (developer, grade B): a schedule fitted to the student's mastery speed beat the fixed 7-14-28-56 schedule for all three knowledge groups, reliably only for medium-knowledge students (effect size 0.27). [PASS2015]
  - TeacherASSIST (developer, randomized at the problem level, grade A): crowdsourced help against showing the answer, assigned 9 to 1. In the repeated experiment (January to September 2019), intention-to-treat: more next problems right on the first try (corrected p = 0.04), less help requested on the next problem (p < 0.01), fewer attempts (p = 0.02). The pilot showed the same direction with only the help-seeking result significant. The authors call the effect small. [Patikorn2020]
  - Earlier small study quoted in a review: 28 fifth graders did one assignment in ASSISTments and one on paper, and scored 0.61 SD higher after the ASSISTments one (Mendicino, Razzaq and Heffernan 2009, a developer study co-authored by the platform's founder). Secondary summary in [Oreopoulos2026].
- **Research platform (E-TRIALS):** researchers define conditions without code. Three study types: a single support against the current "best so far" help, a comparison of two or more kinds of help held across several problem sets, and a "problem varied" study where the researcher supplies content and recruits participants. Students are randomized one by one when a teacher assigns the content. More than 33 published randomized experiments have come out of it, 10 of them without the founder as an author, and one methods paper pools 84 A/B tests. Each study returns eight standard data files. [ETRIALSsite] [ETRIALS2025] [AuxData2023]
- **Criticisms and failures:** effects are small in absolute terms and shrank from 0.18 to 0.10 between the efficacy trial and the replication, though the second was measured a year later. The three-in-a-row rule passes many students who then miss transfer items (stream 3). The 10-problem daily cap exists because some students never reach three in a row, the "wheel-spinning" case studied on this platform (stream 4). [Roschelle2016] [FengWestEd2024] [Adjei2021]

### 4. Duolingo

- **What it is:** a consumer language-learning app, with newer courses in mathematics and music. Its adaptivity is aimed at keeping people practicing daily.
- **Sequencing:** a fixed path of units, each made of lessons. Within a lesson, the Session Generator picks exercises from a pool, using Birdbrain's prediction of whether this learner will get each exercise right. The stated aim is exercises "at just the right difficulty level". The target success rate is unpublished in the sources I read. [Birdbrain] [DuoSpectrum]
- **Student model:**
  - Half-life regression (2016): for each word, recall probability is p = 2^(-delta/h), where delta is the time since last practice and h is the word's half-life in the learner's memory. The half-life is estimated as h = 2^(theta dot x) from features such as counts of past right and wrong answers. It ranks words for practice sessions and fills the "strength meter". [Settles2016]
  - Birdbrain (2020 onward): a logistic regression in the style of item response theory. The chance of a right answer depends on one number for the learner's ability and one for the exercise's difficulty, and difficulty is the sum of feature difficulties (exercise type, words used). After every answer both numbers move by one step of stochastic gradient descent, which the authors describe as "a generalization of the Elo rating system". It was first run in "shadow mode", predicting without acting, and tuned against real answers before it drove lessons. The system handles about a billion exercises a day. [DuoSpectrum] [Birdbrain]
- **Mastery criterion:** completing the lessons of a unit moves the learner on. In the older skill tree a learner could "place out of a level by taking a short test; if no more than three exercises are answered incorrectly, the learner is automatically placed in the next level up". [DuoWhitepaper2021]
- **Stuck:** the session adapts by serving easier exercises. The paid tier adds "Explain My Answer", a chat that explains why an answer was right or wrong. [DuoMax2023]
- **Ahead:** an adaptive placement test for new learners, and a jump-ahead test that marks a whole unit complete. [DuoPlacement]
- **Review and retention mechanics, with published numbers (all developer A/B tests, relative changes):**
  - Half-life regression against the Leitner system (six weeks, just under 1 million users): daily retention +0.3 percent for any activity, +0.3 percent for lessons, -7.3 percent for practice sessions. The authors shipped it anyway, reading the drop as fewer pointless practice sessions.
  - A trimmed model without per-word features against the full model (two weeks, 3.3 million users): +12.0 percent any activity, +1.7 percent lessons, +9.5 percent practice. This is the "12 percent" in the abstract. Offline, half-life regression cut prediction error by 45 percent or more against baselines. [Settles2016]
  - Streak wager (bet in-game currency on keeping a 7-day streak): day-7 retention +14 percent, with significant gains at day 1 and day 14.
  - Weekend amulet (keep the streak over a weekend off): 4 percent more likely to return a week later, 5 percent less likely to lose the streak. The team's lesson: "by giving learners the option to take a break, they're actually more likely to do more in the long run". [DuoStreaks]
  - Streak separated from the daily goal (one lesson extends the streak): day-14 retention +3.3 percent, daily active learners +1 percent, share of daily learners on a streak +10.5 percent in 20 days (+19 percent for new learners). Fewer learners reached their daily goal. [DuoStreaks]
  - Two streak freezes in place of one: daily active learners +0.38 percent. [DuoStreaks]
  - Over four years the growth team raised the current-user retention rate by 21 percent, which it credits with a 4.5-fold rise in daily active users. A 10-day streak marks a sharp fall in drop-off, which the author calls partly selection. [Mazal2023]
- **Content:**
  - Until 2023 every exercise was "written, reviewed, edited, and translated by human experts".
  - Since then: a Learning Designer plans the theme, grammar, vocabulary and exercise types. The tool fills a prompt template (fixed rules for the exercise type, such as two answer options and under 75 characters, plus variable rules for level and grammar focus). The model writes candidates. The designer picks and edits. [DuoLLM2023]
  - Scale: "Developing our first 100 courses took about 12 years, and now, in about a year, we're able to create and launch nearly 150 new courses." The 148 courses reuse one base course across interface languages ("shared content"). [Duo148]
  - A 2025 talk by Duolingo staff separates two modes. Asynchronous generation: many candidates, chained prompts, "learner only sees the top X percent of output", designer as curator. Synchronous generation: the learner sees exactly what the model writes, prompts are elaborate, designer as coach. The problems designers fix include mixed languages, word-for-word translations and odd questions. [DuoUCFL2025]
  - Live features (Roleplay): "Humans write the scenarios", the opening message, and where the model should take the conversation, and staff review generated explanations for accuracy and tone. [DuoMax2023]
- **Published efficacy results:**
  - Vesselinov and Grego 2012 (funded by Duolingo, data and analysis by the outside team, single group before and after, grade C): about eight weeks, adults learning Spanish. Average gain 91.4 points on a college placement test, 8.1 points per hour of study (95 percent CI 5.6 to 10.7). Estimate: 34 hours (26 to 49) to cover a first college semester. [Vesselinov2012]
  - Jiang, Rollinson and Plonsky 2021 (Duolingo researchers with one outside author, peer-reviewed, grade C): 225 adults who had finished the beginner course with Duolingo as their "only learning tool" reached Intermediate Low in reading and Novice High in listening on ACTFL tests, comparable to fourth-semester university students. French reading was higher than the university sample (d = 0.72). Speaking and writing were left out. [Jiang2021]
  - Critique of these studies: participants were finishers who then volunteered twice. In one study 8,367 finishers were contacted, 813 answered, 499 were eligible and 195 started the test. "Only tool" excluded classes and other apps and left reading, listening and conversation unscreened. [DuoReview]
- **How it tests:** "Every single week we run hundreds of A/B tests", "about half of them work", and it takes about a month from idea to product. Three teams of about 100 people each own growth, revenue and teaching. [DuoCiti2026]
- **Criticisms and failures:** the published experiments optimize return visits. The move from the skill tree to the path removed learner-driven spaced review, a common user complaint. Efficacy claims rest on self-selected finishers. [Settles2016] [DuoPlacement] [DuoReview]

### 5. Khan Academy

- **What it is:** a free library of videos, articles and practice exercises organized into courses, units and lessons, with a mastery system on top and, since 2023, the Khanmigo chat tutor (covered in stream 5).
- **Sequencing:** course order is the default path. The learner or teacher can jump anywhere. After each activity a card shows which skills went up or down and may recommend lessons. [KhanHelp]
- **Student model:** one of five levels per skill: Not started, Attempted, Familiar, Proficient, Mastered. Each skill is worth 100 mastery points: 50 at Familiar, 80 at Proficient, 100 at Mastered. Course mastery is the share of skills at Proficient or Mastered. [KhanHelp]
- **Mastery criterion (exact rules from the help center):**
  - Attempted: under 70 percent on an exercise, or a miss on a mixed-skill assessment from Not started or Familiar.
  - Familiar: 70 to 99 percent on an exercise (an older page says 70 to 85 percent), or a right answer on a mixed-skill assessment from Attempted or Not started, or a miss from Proficient.
  - Proficient: 100 percent on an exercise, or a right answer on a mixed-skill assessment from Familiar, or a miss from Mastered.
  - Mastered: already Proficient, then a right answer on this skill on a mixed-skill assessment (unit test, course challenge or mastery challenge).
  - Special cases on the older page: 100 percent on the first attempt at a skill jumps two levels to Proficient, and a Mastered skill drops two levels to Familiar on a score under 70 percent.
  - Sizes: a practice exercise has 4 to 7 questions, a quiz at least 5, a unit test at least 9, a course challenge 30.
  - Answers on harder skills also move the levels of their prerequisite skills. [KhanHelp]
- **Stuck:** each exercise has step-by-step hints and linked videos and articles. The Attempted level exists to flag "that students may be struggling with a skill". Teachers see a skills report. [KhanHelp]
- **Ahead:** the course challenge is the test-out tool. It samples 30 questions from the whole course in 30 to 45 minutes and levels up each skill answered right. The founder's advice for students who know the material: "if you get 70 percent or better, take it again and again" (unconfirmed, the video transcript could not be fetched in the citation check), then use unit tests for the units left. It is not pass or fail. [KhanHelp] [KhanVideo]
- **Review:** Mastery Challenges unlock when the learner has 3 skills at Familiar, 1 at Proficient, and more than 12 hours have passed since the last one. Each has 6 questions on 3 skills, 2 per skill, in mixed order. Both right: the skill goes up a level. Both wrong: down a level. One of each: unchanged. Skills are picked by "the time elapsed since the learner last reviewed the skill, and their level of skill mastery". [KhanHelp]
- **History of the rule:**
  - Early version: a streak of right answers in a row (10 in a row per the stream 3 file, secondary).
  - 2011: a logistic regression on the practice history of the exercise replaced the streak. The A/B test reported "fewer problems done per proficiency" with the chance of getting the next problem right just after proficiency "roughly the same for both groups". [KhanHu]
  - 2013: a model per exercise with features compressed from performance on all other exercises (about 800 features squeezed to 100), tested by a randomized experiment that granted proficiency sooner. [KhanML]
  - 2014 ("Missions"): a practiced skill got a "mastery card" in a mixed challenge after a delay, "typically 16 hours". A right answer moved it up a level, and the engine could promote a strong student straight to Mastered. The delay between promotions was the spaced repetition. [Faus2014]
  - 2018: the current course and unit mastery levels. [Khan2018PR]
- **Content:** staff content creators write videos, articles and exercises (stated in vendor material, authoring process and cost unpublished in what I read).
- **Published results:**
  - Vendor studies (observational, grade C): about 350,000 students in grades 3 to 8 in 2022-23. Using Khan Academy 30 or more minutes a week (18 or more hours a year) went with about 20 percent greater-than-expected growth on MAP Growth, effect size .36. About 9 percent of students reached that dose. Students at 9 to 18 hours (15 percent of the sample) showed about 7 percent more growth. An earlier 99-district study reported 0.26 SD for students at the recommended dose, who were 4.7 percent of the sample. [KhanEfficacy2024] [Holt2024]
  - Oreopoulos, Gibbs, Jensen and Price 2026 (independent academic team, two field experiments, grade A; the paper declares no conflicts, and a 2026 correction to [KhanPNAS] discloses that the lead author sat unpaid on Khan Academy's informal research advisory committee from 2021 to 2024): the treatment is Khan Academy plus a weekly coach for the teacher and a mastery "roadmap". Arlington, Texas: 224 teachers randomized by school-grade for one year. Grades 3 to 6: +0.171 SD on the end-of-year test, +0.122 with demographic controls. Grades 7 to 8: point estimates negative and not significant. Practice time explains the split: elementary classes averaged 34.7 minutes a week and middle-school classes 7.8. Gains appear from about 35 minutes a week. Nashville: 47 teachers, a one-week review with topic randomized within student: +0.22 SD overall, a gain indistinguishable from zero in classes under 5 minutes of average practice, +0.24 SD at 25 to 50 minutes, +0.27 SD at 50 to 100 minutes. Treatment-on-the-treated estimates run 0.18 to 0.43. [Oreopoulos2026]
  - Panel study in PNAS (Eames et al. 2026, developer co-authored: three academic authors and two Khan Academy employees who supplied the data, grade B): three years of administrative data on more than 200,000 students in districts with Khan Academy's MAP Accelerator, using changes in classroom practice time within teacher and school. 6.6 hours a year (about 11 minutes a week) gives +0.031 SD. Gains are roughly linear, reaching +0.085 SD at 30 minutes a week. "Higher-achieving students benefit most, in part because they spend more time" and get through more skills. [KhanPNAS]
- **The "5 percent problem" (Holt 2024, grade D for the argument, built on the studies above):** studies report gains for "students who used the program as recommended": Khan Academy 0.26 SD (a study by three Khan Academy researchers), DreamBox 0.20 SD (the 2016 Harvard study), i-Ready 0.22 SD (a 2019 study, who ran it is unstated in the article), IXL 0.14 SD (reported by IXL). The share who do so is "usually buried in a footnote", and for Khan Academy it was 4.7 percent. A study of Zearn in Washington, DC found the heaviest users were more likely to be white or Asian and from high-income areas. Holt's analogy: a drug that 95 of 100 patients fail to take as prescribed. He adds that the 5 percent might have done as well with any practice program. [Holt2024]
- **Criticisms and failures:** low dose in ordinary use, benefits concentrated among already strong students, and a mastery level that students can raise by retaking a course challenge until questions fall their way. [Holt2024] [KhanPNAS] [KhanVideo]

### 6. Other K-12 and higher-education systems, briefly

#### Knewton and alta (now Wiley)

- **Sequencing and student model:** subject experts build a knowledge graph of learning objectives joined by prerequisite arrows and align each piece of content to an objective. A proficiency model keeps an estimate per objective and updates it on every interaction. The engine recommends the next item toward the assignment's target objectives. [KnewtonAlta]
- **Mastery criterion:** in the platform's interface a goal is complete when each target objective passes `min_predicted_mastery` (the knowledge state at which the model deems the student proficient) and the student has answered at least `min_work_per_target` distinct questions. An optional `max_work_on_goal` ends the goal after a set number of questions regardless. The thresholds are set by the product that embeds the engine. [KnewtonAlta]
- **Stuck:** "refreshers". When a student struggles on a target objective, alta gives a couple of practice questions on the nearest prerequisite. If those go well the student returns to the target at once. If they go badly, alta delivers instruction and more practice on that prerequisite, and can go to more distant prerequisites, up to three "hops" from the target. In Fall 2021 data (more than 40 million interactions sampled across four course families), 10 to 15 percent of student assignments had any refresher, about 80 percent of students met one at least once, and only about 20 percent of refresher cases went past one hop. Most students who got a refresher still finished the assignment. The vendor states plainly that this does not prove the refresher caused the recovery. [KnewtonAlta]
- **Published results:** vendor usage statistics. A Johns Hopkins analysis is listed by title on the vendor's resource page and I did not read it.
- **Why Knewton's claims drew criticism:** in 2015 the founder described the product as "a robot tutor in the sky that can semi-read your mind and figure out what your strengths and weaknesses are, down to the percentile", able to find content "proven most effective for people just like you, and give that to you every single time". Michael Feldstein answered: "he is selling snake oil", and explained that the only efficacy studies he had seen were of Pearson products and "do not differentiate between Knewton features and Pearson-native features", and that the system "has a very narrow bandwidth in terms of what they can observe about the student relative to what a human teacher can observe". His standard for a vendor: "Explain what the damned thing does in clear, concrete, and straightforward language." The company was sold to Wiley in 2019, described in the press as "sold for parts". Grade D. [NPRKnewton2015] [Feldstein2015] [IHEKnewton2019]

#### DreamBox

- **Published result (independent, Harvard CEPR 2016, quasi-experimental, grade B):** nearly 3,000 students in grades 3 to 5 in Howard County public schools and the Rocketship charter network (the count is the vendor guide's, and the press account says more than 3,000). Key findings in CEPR's words: "Most students did not reach the recommended levels of usage", use was driven "largely [by] teacher- and school-level practices, as opposed to student preferences", students who spent more time saw larger gains, students "who followed the DreamBox lesson recommendations, as opposed to going back and repeating content, saw faster gains", and "the evidence for the causal impact of DreamBox on student achievement is encouraging but mixed". A press account puts the gain at 1.5 to 3 percentile points for students who completed some lessons, with average use of 35 minutes a week in Howard County and 44 at Rocketship against a recommended 60 to 90. [CEPR2016] [Hechinger2016]
- **Vendor framing:** the vendor's summary turns the linear usage estimate into "7.5 points more growth" at 60 minutes a week, a dose that the studied students did not reach. [DreamBoxVendor]

#### IXL (SmartScore)

- **Mastery criterion:** a SmartScore from 0 to 100 per skill. It is "not a percent correct score". Inputs named by the vendor: number of questions done, right and wrong answers, question difficulty, with recent answers weighted more and runs of right answers "rewarded highly". 80 is "proficiency", 90 "excellence", 100 "mastery". The algorithm is proprietary.
- **How the score moves:** from 0 to 70, large gains for right answers and small penalties for wrong ones. From 71 to 90, harder questions. From 90 (the "Challenge Zone"), right answers add 1 to 2 points and wrong answers subtract 3 to 8, and a student may need "as many as 10 questions correctly in a row" to reach 100. A wrong answer brings a step-by-step explanation and easier questions.
- **Guidance:** 80 or 90 "may be the perfect stopping point", and 100 is framed as extra credit. The vendor recommends two skills to proficiency per week. [IXLSmartScore]
- **Published results:** a randomized study of 25 teachers in grades 3 to 5 in Michigan, run by the Johns Hopkins Center for Research and Reform in Education (Copeland et al. 2023), found +0.13 SD (secondary summary in [Oreopoulos2026]). The vendor reported 0.14 SD for students who used the program as designed (secondary summary in [Holt2024]).

#### i-Ready (Curriculum Associates)

- **How it works:** an adaptive diagnostic three times a year places the student overall and by domain. The system builds a lesson sequence from that placement. Teachers can reorder it or add lessons. A new diagnostic re-places the student. The vendor's targets are 30 to 49 minutes per subject per week and at least 70 percent of lessons passed. [iReady]
- **Published results:** the vendor's own study meets only the lowest evidence tier it applies for ("ESSA Level 3", correlational). An outside review site reports one quasi-experiment, run by the vendor itself (Holzman and Duncan 2022, Curriculum Associates Research Report No. 07), with matched groups of fifth graders in six Massachusetts districts in 2020-21: ES +0.12 in reading (2,132 students) and +0.23 in mathematics. Treatment was defined as completing at least one lesson in the first quarter, a self-selected group. [iReady]

#### Squirrel AI (Yixue, China)

- **How it works:** a fine-grained map of knowledge points, with knowledge space theory and information theory used "to precisely diagnose the student's knowledge state with as few questions as possible". Each lesson starts with a diagnostic pretest, and the learning phase covers only the knowledge points not mastered. [Cui2018]
- **Mastery criterion:** an ability score above 0.75 on the knowledge point in the mathematics product and above 0.85 in the English product. [Cui2018]
- **Published result (developer-authored, randomized, grade A by design, weak in practice):** 203 middle-school students were randomized to the system or to classes taught by teachers with 8 to 18 years of experience. 90 of 101 finished in the system group and 73 of 102 in the teacher group, so attrition was 11 against 28 percent (my arithmetic). Earlier studies by the same group report g = 0.48 and g = 0.32. The headline effect is Hedges's g = .68 on gain scores, and the paper's second experiment gives g = .49 (both confirmed in the arXiv text during the citation check). A secondary review notes the study ran for about three days during a vacation and judges it "not credible evidence". [Cui2018] [AvantiDocs]

#### Math Academy

All of this is the company's own description, from its book "The Math Academy Way". Grade C throughout.

- **Knowledge graph:** thousands of topics from fourth grade to university mathematics, joined by prerequisite links. Each knowledge point inside a topic also links to its "key prerequisites", the earlier topics it uses most directly. A course is a region of the graph.
- **Ahead:** a new student takes an adaptive diagnostic that finds the "knowledge frontier", the boundary between what they know and what they are ready to learn. The diagnostic also tests lower-grade foundations and fills gaps while the student works on course topics that do not depend on them.
- **Sequencing:** new lessons always come from the frontier. The student works a mix of lessons, reviews and quizzes.
- **Lesson shape and mastery criterion:** a lesson is a short series of "knowledge points" of rising difficulty. Each has a worked example and then questions like it. The student must "answer sufficiently many questions correctly (with sufficiently few mistakes) in each successive knowledge point". A student who cannot show mastery of a knowledge point "within 2 to 5 questions" fails the lesson.
- **Stuck:** after a failed lesson the system pauses that path and gives the student unrelated topics, then asks for a second attempt later. The stated reason is that a rest lets the first exposure consolidate. "On average, students pass their first attempt 95 percent of the time, and within two attempts 99 percent of the time." A second failure at the same knowledge point triggers "remedial reviews on the key prerequisites". A missed quiz question triggers a remedial review of that topic at once. Lessons with low pass rates are rewritten with more scaffolding or split.
- **Review:** spaced repetition per topic with a twist the company calls Fractional Implicit Repetition. Passing a review of an advanced topic gives partial credit to the review schedules of its prerequisites, and failing a review of a prerequisite penalizes the topics built on it. Reviews mix topics (interleaving). Quizzes arrive about every 150 minutes of work. [MathAcademyWay] [Hecker2025] [SkycakPodcast]
- **Effort signals:** work is counted in XP, about one per minute for a focused student. Rushing and guessing earn negative XP. The company treats failing more than one lesson in ten, or earning half the expected XP per minute, as signs of a problem in how the student is working.
- **Validation:** "We also track review and quiz performance. There is less priming for review tasks, and no priming for quizzes", so a lesson that passes students who then fail quiz items is flagged as shallow. [MathAcademyWay]
- **Published results:** vendor claims and a description of an in-school accelerated program for students selected at the 90th percentile. Every figure I found comes from the company.

#### Smart Sparrow

- **How it works:** an authoring platform. The instructor lays out screens with text, simulations and inputs, then writes rules. A "trap state" is a rule that recognizes a specific anticipated mistake and fires its own feedback or sends the student down a remedial or bypass path. "Each intelligent trap-state must be programmed beforehand, meaning that unanticipated wrong answers will receive a generic response." Each submission logs the timestamp, the answers and the trap states fired, and instructors use those logs each term to add traps for common wrong answers they had missed. [SmartSparrow] [Horodyskyj2018]
- **Authoring cost:** in a pathology project, each adaptive tutorial took 15 to 40 hours to build, and needed "diagnostic expertise and understanding of potential misconceptions" to storyboard the feedback. [BESTNetwork]
- **Published results:** two randomized crossover trials in pathology education reported learning "at least equal in effectiveness to conventional methods" and better diagnostic accuracy for medical students on one task. Grade A by design, small, run by the project team. [BESTNetwork]

### 7. Adult, workplace and military systems

#### DARPA Digital Tutor (Navy information technology training)

This is the closest published case to TeachPad's first students: adults learning a technical craft, taught one to one by a machine built from expert tutors.

- **Goal:** compress years of on-the-job experience into the 16 weeks the Navy allots for initial IT training, with the same entry requirements. The objectives went "well beyond the entry-level or journeyman-level abilities" of the standard course. [Fletcher2014]
- **How it was built:**
  - Objectives came from reference material plus interviews and observation of Fleet technicians named as experts by managers and peers, with a focus on "the key experiences, problems, and insights that had contributed to their expertise".
  - Tutors were chosen for both subject expertise and one-to-one tutoring skill, by half-hour auditions with a representative student. Twenty-four were selected.
  - "About half its funding was used" to recruit them and run a full human-tutored 16-week course first. Students often got different tutors on the same subtopic for different perspectives.
  - All sessions were audio recorded. A Content Author (usually the tutor who taught it) worked with a Content Engineer who knew the software, under a Course Architect responsible for the flow of the whole course.
  - Development was iterative: build a segment, test it with learners, revise until it met its target. [Fletcher2014]
- **Architecture:** an Inference Engine turns low-level data on the learner's actions into a picture of the strategy and path the learner is using and what they understand or misunderstand. An Instruction Engine decides what the learner should work on next and which technique to use, aiming at what was "missed or misunderstood, or understood at an insufficiently deep level", and decides when to ask the learner to reflect. A Conversation Module holds the dialogue in natural language without attempting full understanding. A Recommender decides when to call a human mentor. [Fletcher2014]
- **Sequencing and pacing:** each subtopic (for example Group Policy, Internet Protocol, Active Directory) had about a week. Problems grew "from a few minutes and a few steps to open-ended 30 to 40 minute problems" on real Fleet systems. Once the learner "demonstrates sufficient understanding of the material presented and can explain and apply it successfully", the tutor moves up to a higher level of abstraction or across to a related topic. [Fletcher2014]
- **Ahead:** time was held constant and achievement allowed to vary. "Fast-paced learners who reached targeted levels of learning early were given more difficult problems, problems that dealt with related subtopics that were not otherwise presented in the time available, problems calling for higher levels of understanding and abstraction, or challenge problems with minimal (if any) tutorial assistance." [Fletcher2014]
- **Stuck and teaching tactics (the report's list):**
  - Prompt for antecedents, explanations, consequences or implications of answers.
  - Question answers "both right and wrong", and ask learners what they have learned so far.
  - Probe vague or incomplete explanations.
  - On an impasse or a misconception, review knowledge already acquired "by asking why something did or did not happen".
  - "Rarely providing a correct answer or a direct hint."
  - "Never articulating a misconception."
  - Require logical, causal or goal-oriented reasoning when reviewing the learner's steps.
  - Refocus the dialogue when answers show a concept that should have been mastered is missing.
  - Let problem solving continue until the learner finds their own careless error in a concept already mastered.
  - Verify understanding of any explanatory material before proceeding.
  - Tell typing errors from real misconceptions.
  - Use different approaches by problem complexity: supply knowledge for simple problems, prompt the learner to break complicated problems into parts, and prompt for "reflective explanations" on complex ones. [Fletcher2014]
- **Assessment inside the course:** "frequent, substantive tests" of facts and concepts, including explanatory sentences and short paragraphs. [Fletcher2014]
- **Humans in the loop:** six hours a day with the tutor, then a two-hour study hall of about five students led by a Navy instructor, covering hardware, study skills early on, and Navy culture. [Fletcher2014]
- **Published results (IDA as third-party assessor under a Defense Department contract, DARPA-funded program, groups selected and not randomized, 12 per group, locally developed tests, grade B):**
  - Final assessment: 12 Digital Tutor graduates (16 weeks), 12 graduates of a 35-week classroom and lab course (ITTC), 12 Fleet technicians averaging 9.6 years of experience and chosen as strong performers.
  - Written knowledge test (4 hours): 3.54 SD over the classroom graduates and 3.11 SD over the Fleet technicians, both p < .0001.
  - Team troubleshooting over two and a half days: 7.98 SD over classroom graduates and 4.19 SD over Fleet technicians. Digital Tutor teams solved about twice as many problems as Fleet teams, with fewer harmful actions (-1.85, -1.63) and fewer unnecessary steps (-2.26, -2.10).
  - Oral review board: 1.07 and 0.89. Network design exercise: 0.74 (not significant) and 1.52 in the executive summary table. The report disagrees with itself here: its body text and Table 14 give 0.33 and 0.63, both not significant.
  - Security exercise: no significant difference (-0.97 and -0.03). The report's explanation: "An appropriate human tutor was not available for the Security component", and much of it was delivered as lecture.
  - Against the human-tutored version of the course: digital tutoring was significantly better on troubleshooting and better but not significantly so on knowledge.
  - A total reading score explained about 25 percent of knowledge-test variance for classroom students and none for Digital Tutor students.
  - Earlier assessment of the human-tutored course after 10 weeks: effect size 2.30 on a written test against 20 graduates of the standard self-paced course.
  - Veterans follow-up: 97 mostly unemployed veterans completed an 18-week civilian version with zero academic dropouts. Six months later all but one who sought IT work had been hired, at a median salary of $73,000.
  - Claimed return: net present value to the Fleet "would exceed $300M annually" if fully used. Development cost per hour of instruction is unstated in what I read. [Fletcher2014] [FletcherDTIC]
- **How the field treats it:** Kulik and Fletcher's meta-analysis of 50 evaluations found the two Digital Tutor evaluations (average effects 1.97 and 3.18) were outliers, "the only ones in the data set", and capped them before averaging by putting the value at the 95th percentile in place of each. The same review found tutoring-system effects of 0.73 on local tests and 0.13 on standardized tests. The journal text sits behind a paywall. The outlier capping, the 1.97 and 3.18 and the quoted words were confirmed in the cleanup pass on 2026-11-22 in a full-text reprint of the article (IDA Document NS D-8391, Results, "Overall Effects", "Primary Analysis", p. 53), and the 0.73 and 0.13 were confirmed on secondary pages only. [KulikFletcher2016]
- **Caveats in the report itself:** small groups, comparison groups whose adequacy "remains subject to discussion", and differences in content and objectives between the courses. A prior adult precedent it cites: Sherlock, where 20 to 25 hours of tutored troubleshooting practice matched about four years of on-the-job experience on rare diagnostic tasks. [Fletcher2014]

#### Area9 Rhapsode (and NEJM Knowledge+)

- **How it works:** every answer comes with a confidence rating. Combining right or wrong with sure or unsure sorts each learning objective into four states: unconscious incompetence (sure and wrong), conscious incompetence, conscious competence and automaticity. The engine also uses response time. It picks the next probe or content from that state, skips material the learner answers correctly and confidently, and schedules refreshers. A sample from one telecom course shows three learners reaching "100 percent proficient" in 8, 20 and 34 minutes. [Area9]
- **Vendor claims:** learners are "typically 15 to 40 percent unconsciously incompetent in any given subject" (20 to 40 percent in one study at "a global technology company"). Up to 50 percent less time than a traditional 45-minute e-learning course. Up to 90 percent less retention effort. These come from brochures. [Area9]
- **Published results:**
  - Healy et al. 2020 (authors include employees of the publisher and of the certifying board, matched comparison, grade B): 177 physicians who completed at least 75 percent of NEJM Knowledge+ before a board recertification exam (2014 to 2017) against 177 matched non-users. Users had a 10.6 point higher likelihood of passing (95 percent CI 5.37 to 15.8, p < .001), 10.7 points higher likelihood of a top-quartile score, and 10.8 points lower likelihood of a bottom-quartile score. A vendor summary gives the raw pass rates as 95.5 against 85.9 percent. Users are defined by finishing most of the product, so persistence is confounded with use. [Healy2020] [PopulusSummary]
  - Healy et al. 2018 (survey, grade C): of 4,400 users surveyed, 836 responded. A subgroup of 153 reported passing at 95 percent against a national 89 percent. Self-reported, low response. [Healy2018]

#### Amplifire

- **How it works:** the learner answers first, in several formats. Clicking an answer once means partial confidence, twice means certain, and "I don't know yet" is an option. Right-or-wrong feedback is immediate. Corrective feedback (an explanation, sometimes a worked example, naming the kind of error) comes after a delay that the system sets from the learner's estimated ability, the item's difficulty and how long the learner took. A fully confident right answer gets no corrective feedback, on the grounds that it adds nothing. A partly confident right answer does get it. The system also sends behavior messages such as "make sure to read the question carefully" when an answer arrives faster than the question could be read. Items repeat until the learner is confident and correct on all of them. [Hays2019]
- **The core idea:** "confidently held misinformation", being sure and wrong, is the state the product hunts. In one nursing deployment it logged 25,129 such instances and 29,838 instances of uncertainty among 3,707 nurses. The least-informed nurses spent about half an hour in the module and the best-informed about 12 minutes. [AmplifireCases]
- **Published results (authors are Amplifire employees plus one academic, before-and-after designs, grade B at best):** a 48 percent reduction in central-line bloodstream infections and a 32 percent reduction in catheter-associated urinary infections after training several thousand nurses in one health system. Vendor case studies quote 29 percent over 26 months across 23 hospitals against the prior three years, and 79 percent in one pilot hospital against stable rates in the system's other hospitals. Infection rates have many causes and none of these designs is randomized. [Hays2019] [AmplifireCases]

#### Axonify

- **How it works:** frontline staff do one short session per shift. The vendor's "sweet spot" is 3 to 5 minutes, and the 2018 benchmark report gives an average just under 6 minutes including company messages. A session asks about four questions chosen by "business priority, an employee's demonstrated levels of knowledge and weaknesses, as well as their level of confidence in that knowledge". Missed material returns. [Axonify]
- **Published result (independent academics using Axonify's logs, observational, grade B):** 10,514 employees at five companies (two retailers, two pharmaceutical sales teams, one college), about 220,000 data points, sessions of about three minutes with two to three questions and corrective feedback. A mixed model on the first three occurrences of each question found a significant interaction: "the optimal amount of spacing between repeated retrieval events increased as the retention interval increased", in line with laboratory work. The authors recall the rule of thumb that the gap between two study sessions should be about 10 to 20 percent of the retention interval. Timing was uncontrolled. [Kim2019]

#### Spaced-repetition tools (SuperMemo, Anki) and medical students

- **SM-2 (SuperMemo, the classic rule):** review a new item after 1 day, then 6 days, then after the previous interval times the item's ease factor. Ease starts at 2.5 and has a floor of 1.3. After each review the learner grades recall from 0 to 5, and the ease moves by 0.1 - (5 - q) * (0.08 + (5 - q) * 0.02). A grade under 3 restarts the item at 1 day without resetting ease. Items graded under 4 repeat the same day until they reach 4. [SM2]
- **Anki:** the default is a modified SM-2 with learner-set learning steps before the first long interval, four answer buttons, and failures during initial learning that leave ease untouched (to avoid "low interval hell"). The newer FSRS scheduler keeps three numbers per card: difficulty, stability (days for recall to fall from 100 to 90 percent) and retrievability. It schedules each card for the moment predicted recall falls to the "desired retention", 90 percent by default. The manual warns that workload climbs steeply above 90 percent and becomes "overwhelming" above 97. [AnkiDocs]
- **Evidence from medical students (surveys linked to exam scores, grade B and C):**
  - 72 students at one school: each additional 1,700 unique Anki cards seen went with one more point on USMLE Step 1, and each 445 practice questions with one more point, controlling for prior scores, grades and test anxiety. Cards from a commercial flashcard product showed no such relation. Self-reported and exploratory by the authors' own description. [Deng2015]
  - 201 students at another school (response rate 36.9 percent): Anki users averaged 241.10 against 235.51 for non-users (d = 0.38). Users who "always" or "most of the time" completed their reviews scored about 247 against 236 for those who did so "sometimes". [Lu2021]
  - A 2026 meta-analysis of spaced repetition in medical education reports a pooled benefit (standardised mean difference 0.78, 95 percent CI 0.56 to 0.99, from the abstract only). [Maye2026]
- **What these tools leave to the learner:** card writing, card quality and showing up. The evidence is about people who chose to keep reviewing.

#### Programming-specific tutors and platforms

- **LISP tutor and the cognitive tutors (Carnegie Mellon, 1983 on):** a production-rule model solves each problem alongside the student. A step on any correct path passes without comment. A step off the path gets a short message aimed at getting back on. Ten years of lessons: students learn in units the size of production rules, "the best tutorial interaction style was one in which the tutor provides immediate feedback, consisting of short and directed error messages", and the tutors "work better if they present themselves to students as nonhuman tools to assist learning rather than as emulations of human tutors". Results: "usually, but not always" significant gains, and in the best cases the same proficiency "in one third of the time". A secondary page gives the LISP mini-course result as exercises finished 30 percent faster with higher scores. Developer studies, grade B. [Anderson1995] [UBCWiki]
- **SQL-Tutor and constraint-based modeling (Mitrovic, University of Canterbury):**
  - Domain knowledge is a set of constraints, each with a relevance condition (when it applies) and a satisfaction condition (what must then be true). The tutor needs no model of how to solve the problem, only of what a correct solution must satisfy.
  - Short-term student model: the constraints the current solution satisfies and violates. Long-term model: a history of use per constraint, summarized over a window such as the last five attempts.
  - Feedback: the student asks for it when ready. It says the solution is wrong, points to the part that is wrong, and names the domain principle violated. "The error correction is left to the student to perform."
  - Problem selection: early versions picked problems around constraints the student violated often or had never met. A later version used a small Bayesian network per problem to predict how many constraints the student would violate, and chose problems that were neither too hard (too many messages at once) nor too easy.
  - Findings from the team's own studies: feedback that names the violated principle (the "Hint" and "All errors" levels) led to faster and more effective learning than other levels. Adding positive feedback let students finish the same tasks in half the time with the same learning gain. Adaptive tutorial dialogues beat non-adaptive ones with an effect size of 0.69 after about 100 minutes. The first evaluation in 1998 used volunteers without a control. Developer studies with university students, grade B. [Mitrovic2012] [Mitrovic2016]
- **Execute Program (commercial, working programmers learning TypeScript, SQL, regular expressions):**
  - Lessons are short text broken by live code examples the learner must complete: 4,821 paragraphs against 3,751 interactive examples across the catalog, "you'll rarely go a full minute without completing an interactive code example".
  - Lessons form a prerequisite graph: "A lesson won't unlock until you've finished the lessons that it depends on, and then successfully reviewed them after a one-day wait." The founder notes this lets a lesson skip re-explaining its prerequisites, which keeps lessons short.
  - Reviews return on exponentially growing intervals (the founder gives 1, 2, 4, 8 days and onward as the simple model and says the real schedule differs). A user reports an item retires after the fourth pass, around day 64.
  - Wrong answers in a review cost nothing as long as the learner gets there in the end. Only "give up", which shows the answer, shortens the interval. The rule changed in early 2020 because machine marking was failing answers over unimportant typos.
  - Pacing is capped by the reviews: about 30 minutes a day for about two weeks per course, with reviews under 10 percent of total time.
  - All of this is the vendor's and users' description. Grade C. [ExecuteProgram] [Bernhardt2020] [MatuschakEP] [Place2020]
- **Exercism (free, volunteer-run, for programmers learning a new language):** each language has a syllabus, a tree of concepts. A "concept exercise" teaches one or more concepts and unlocks when its prerequisite concepts are done. The design target: a seasoned developer in that language should need "no more than 5 to 10 minutes" per concept exercise, and the exercises assume the student already programs in another language. A submission that passes the tests goes to an Analyzer (rule-based comments on specific patterns, for example using a library call that defeats the point of a recursion exercise) and a Representer (normalizes the code so that one mentor comment can be reused on every equivalent solution). Human mentoring is optional and requested by the student. Grade C. [ExercismDocs]
- **Codecademy:** lesson, then quiz, then project. A "personalized practice" feature tracks "what you're learning, when you learn it, and how well you've been doing" and schedules review by spaced repetition. Career paths end sections with a two-part exam, 70 percent on both parts to pass, with one retake per 24 hours. Passing every exam earns a certificate. Grade C. [Codecademy]

### 8. Dialogue tutors before large language models

#### AutoTutor (University of Memphis)

- **Mechanism:** "expectation and misconception tailored" dialogue. Each main question is open-ended and comes with 2 to 10 expectations (the facets of a good answer) and 0 to 5 misconceptions (wrong ideas students often voice). Semantic matching (latent semantic analysis, regular expressions, word overlap) scores how well the student's words cover each expectation and whether they match a misconception. [Nye2014]
- **Help ladder for each uncovered expectation:** a pump ("what else?"), then a hint, then a prompt for a specific missing word or phrase, and finally an assertion that states the expectation "if a student cannot cover an expectation despite many hints and prompts". A detected misconception gets a correction. The tutor also gives short feedback, answers the student's definitional questions, and ends with a summary. [Nye2014]
- **Mastery criterion:** the dialogue on a question ends when all expectations are covered, by the student or, failing that, by the tutor's assertions.
- **Content:** every question needs its expectations, misconceptions, hints, prompts and summary written by hand.
- **Published results (the developers' own 17-year review, grade A for the underlying experiments):** "learning gains that are on average about 0.8 sigma above controls who read static instructional materials (e.g., textbooks) for an equivalent amount of time", across computer literacy, physics and critical thinking, and "on par with expert human tutors in computer mediated conversation". [Nye2014]

#### Why2-Atlas and Why2-AutoTutor (qualitative physics)

- **Design:** the student writes an essay answering a "why" question. The tutor finds flaws and missing points and runs a dialogue on each. Seven experiments held content constant across two kinds of human tutoring (spoken and typed), the two computer tutors, and three text conditions. The strongest text control, "canned text remediation", showed every student the same mini-lessons, made by rewriting the tutor's dialogue lines as plain statements. [VanLehn2007]
- **Result (grade A):** "When novices (students who had not taken college physics) studied content that was written for intermediates, then tutorial dialogue was reliably more beneficial than less interactive instruction, with large effect sizes. When novices studied material written for novices or intermediates studied material written for intermediates, then tutorial dialogue was not reliably more effective than the text-based control conditions." [VanLehn2007]

#### Andes (physics homework, US Naval Academy)

- **Mechanism:** the student solves ordinary physics problems on screen, entering vectors, variables and equations in any order. Each entry turns green or red at once. The design principle is to change the homework and leave the rest of the course alone. [VanLehn2005]
- **Stuck (the three kinds of help):**
  - An unrequested message only when the error is probably a slip (a blank field, an undefined variable from a typo, missing units).
  - "What's Wrong Help" when the student selects a red entry and asks.
  - "Next Step Help" when the student asks what to do next.
  - The reasoning: for careless mistakes give help unasked, and "for errors where some learning is possible" give help only on request, "to increase the chance that students will repair substantive errors without asking for help". [VanLehn2005]
- **Published results (developers with the course instructors, sections of volunteering instructors against other sections, grade B):** hour exams over five years: effect sizes 0.21, 0.92, 0.52, 0.44 and 0.60 by year, 0.61 overall (455 Andes students, 276 controls). The 2003 final exam, multiple choice and covering material beyond Andes: 0.25 overall after adjusting for major and grade average (89 against 823 students), 0.52 for non-science majors and not significant for engineers and scientists. The authors' reading: strong students learn equally well either way. [VanLehn2005]

### 9. Scripted model tutors

#### Synthesis Tutor (mathematics for ages 5 to 11)

- **What the vendor says:** "Synthesis uses AI where it helps, and a team of expert educators and neuroscientists designs the lessons and reviews every interaction for quality", and it is "not handed to a chatbot". Lessons use on-screen manipulatives, read text aloud, and check understanding with "micro-assessments throughout every lesson", and "a child only moves on once they have shown they have mastered the material". [Synthesis]
- **What outside observers saw:**
  - A 2023 note on the preview lesson describes "a scripted sequence of messages" with "some very simple branching structure": the tutor waits until the student puts a shared widget into a target state, sends an extra message when the widget is in a bad state or the student takes too long, answers numeric or multiple-choice input with a set response, and repeats the final exercise until it is right. The same note: "The authors say that the system is based on the DARPA Digital Tutor work, but I don't see those mechanics here at all, at least at the instructional design level." [MatuschakSynthesis]
  - A secondary review quotes the project's engineers on a public forum: "No user ever sees live/unchecked LLM output", and "It has manipulatives/widgets that the tutor works with and responds to, rather than just a chat bot". It also quotes testers of the 2023 demo: wrong inputs "just proceeded with the pre-programmed lesson", and the pace "moves at a crawl". [UnderstandReview]
- **Published results:** the vendor's results page cites the Digital Tutor percentiles (better than "99.9 percent of Navy technicians") and a partnership with that team. Its own outcome data are unpublished in what I found. [Synthesis] [UnderstandReview]

#### Other products that pair authored structure with a model

- **Duolingo Max:** people write the scenario, the first message and the direction of each Roleplay, and the model plays the character inside that frame. "Explain My Answer" is anchored to one exercise and one answer. Staff review samples of output, and learners can flag any message. [DuoMax2023]
- **Duolingo batch generation:** the model writes exercise candidates from a designer's template and only the best-ranked share reaches learners. [DuoLLM2023] [DuoUCFL2025]
- **Khanmigo and the Harvard physics tutor:** covered in stream 5. Both put a human-authored solution or hint set in front of the model for each problem.
- **The pre-LLM precedent:** AutoTutor, Why2-Atlas and the Digital Tutor were all scripted dialogue systems. Their scripts were expectations, misconceptions and recorded expert moves, and their language handling only had to choose among authored moves. [Nye2014] [VanLehn2007] [Fletcher2014]

### 10. Critiques of the whole category

- **Reich 2020, "Failure to Disrupt" (grade D, a synthesis):** learning-at-scale tools fall into three genres by who controls the path: the instructor, an algorithm, or peers. Algorithm-guided tutors are "theoretically compelling" and depend on automated assessment, "which only exist in three domains: mathematics, computer science, and early language acquisition". Even there, a machine can check whether code meets a spec and cannot judge design. Self-paced courses mostly serve learners who are "already very successful". Four standing dilemmas: "the curse of the familiar" (schools reshape new tools to fit old routines), "the edtech Matthew effect" (the advantaged benefit most), "the trap of routine assessment", and "the toxic power of data and experiments". His advice is a "tinkerer's stance": small improvements on a long history. [Reich2020] [Reich2020LAS]
- **RAND personalized learning studies (independent, matched comparison, grade B):**
  - 2015, "Continued Progress": 62 schools pursuing personalized learning showed more growth than matched peers over two years, with the greatest gains where schools grouped students by data, shared data with students, and had flexible space. Effect sizes unconfirmed (see "How I checked things"). [RAND2015]
  - 2017, "Informing Progress": 32 schools and about 5,500 students over one year: 0.09 in mathematics (significant) and 0.07 in reading (not significant), about 3 percentile points. The 2017 schools were mostly secondary and newer to the approach. [RAND2017Brief]
  - RAND's own caution: school-level effects ranged from +14 to -17 percentile points in mathematics and +16 to -19 in reading, seven schools (nearly a quarter) had significantly negative effects, and the design "leaves open the possibility that factors other than personalized learning might have caused the positive effects". [RANDPE314]
- **Who runs the study:** in What Works Clearinghouse data, evaluations run or paid for by the developer produced average effects of 0.31 against 0.14 for independent ones (unconfirmed), and came out about 1.7 times larger within the same program after controls. The authors found "some evidence that developers are more likely than independent researchers to 'bury' disappointing findings" and recommend preregistration. Grade A. [Wolf2020]
- **Which test is used:** in 50 evaluations of intelligent tutors the median effect was 0.66. Local tests: 0.73 (38 studies). Standardized tests: 0.13 (9 studies). Both: 0.45 (3 studies). Evaluations with unusual control groups or flawed implementation showed small effects. Grade A. [KulikFletcher2016]
- **Technology reviews:** of 31 randomized trials of computer-assisted learning in developed countries, 21 found significant effects, 16 of those in mathematics. A meta-analysis of 74 studies of educational technology in mathematics (56,886 students) found +0.15 overall: +0.18 for supplemental computer-assisted instruction, +0.08 for computer-managed learning and +0.07 for comprehensive programs. Oreopoulos et al. summarize the same review as 12 of 19 recent trials with substantial impact, from 0.14 to 0.56 SD, with an average of 0.18, and add that "quality of implementation is an important factor". Grade A. [Escueta2020] [CheungSlavin2013] [Oreopoulos2026]
- **Effects shrink with scale, time and low use:** Cognitive Tutor went from 0.3 to 1.2 SD in a developer study of three schools to 0.20 SD in year two of a 147-school trial. ASSISTments went from 0.61 SD in a one-week study of 28 students to 0.18 and 0.10 in statewide trials. ALEKS showed g = 0.17 over a semester and -0.02 over a year. Khan Academy shows 0.26 to 0.36 for the few students at full dose and 0.03 to 0.09 in panel estimates for ordinary classrooms. [Koedinger1997] [Pane2014] [Roschelle2016] [FengWestEd2024] [Fang2019] [KhanEfficacy2024] [KhanPNAS]
- **The dose problem:** covered under Khan Academy above. [Holt2024]

## How deployed systems gather evidence

### In-product experiments

- **Duolingo:** randomized A/B tests on every change, "hundreds" each week, about half of which improve their target metric. New student models run first in "shadow mode", where predictions are logged and compared with real answers before the model drives anything. The cost is real: to test a new model "we need to process every challenge response once per model". Metrics are day-1, day-7 and day-14 retention, daily active users, lessons completed, and for learning "how quickly learners advanced to more difficult material". [DuoCiti2026] [DuoSpectrum] [DuoEngineering] [DuoStreaks]
- **ALEKS:** randomization inside the mastery rule. From April 2023 to November 2024, 5 percent of topic starts got one threshold and 5 percent the other, while 90 percent ran as usual. The outcome was the correct rate on the random extra problem in later progress assessments. The same extra problem gives a standing check of the student model (predicted against observed answer). [Matayoshi2025] [Falmagne2006]
- **Khan Academy:** an A/B test of a new proficiency model judged on three things: problems done per proficiency earned, new exercises attempted, and the chance of getting the next problem right just after proficiency was granted. The stream 3 file records that next-item correctness without help is still its primary metric. [KhanHu] [KhanML]
- **ASSISTments E-TRIALS:** outside researchers define conditions without code, students are randomized individually when a teacher assigns the content, and the default control is the platform's "best so far" help. Each study returns eight standard files: action logs (one row per click: start, resume, finish a problem, request help, submit an answer), problem logs (time on problem, attempts, discrete score 0 or 1, continuous score 0, 0.33, 0.67 or 1.0), assignment logs, prior logs (class and school context), same-skill prior logs, same-skill post logs, assignment settings and redo logs. The fixed format lets one analysis script serve many studies. [ETRIALS2025] [ETRIALSsite]
- **TeacherASSIST:** every problem with crowdsourced help is its own small experiment, with a 9 to 1 split between help and answer-only, and the outcome measured on the next problem: right on first try, help requested, number of attempts. [Patikorn2020]
- **Carnegie Learning UpGrade:** an open-source A/B service separate from the tutor. The tutor asks it which condition a student is in at each "decision point". It supports assignment by individual or by group (class, teacher, school), rules for keeping a student or a class consistent, rules for what happens after the experiment ends, and automatic exclusion of students who met the content before the experiment began. It stores only anonymized identifiers. The design problem it answers: in self-paced software, students in one class reach the experimental content at different times. [UpGrade2020] [MurphyRitter2022]
- **Math Academy:** tracks pass rate per lesson and per knowledge point, then checks whether students who passed a rewritten lesson still succeed on reviews and quizzes, where priming is lower. [MathAcademyWay]
- **DARPA Digital Tutor:** five staged assessments during development, each comparing the current version with standard training and with experienced technicians on job-sample tasks. [Fletcher2014]

### In vivo experiments (LearnLab)

- LearnLab at Carnegie Mellon and Pittsburgh ran "over 360" experiments inside real courses. Its definition of in vivo: no recruiting or scheduling of participants, "longer, more realistic duration (many hours or days rather than many minutes)", and "data from students engaged in genuine academic" work. [LearnLab]
- The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction framework gives the unit of analysis: a knowledge component, a learning event, an instructional event and an assessment event. [KLI2012]

### Learning-curve analysis

- A learning curve plots error rate (or time) on a knowledge component against the number of opportunities the student has had to use it. "A good learning curve reveals improvement in student performance as opportunity count increases."
- Reading the curve:
  - Starts high and ends high: students finished without mastering the skill.
  - Starts low and stays low over many opportunities: the skill is easy and students are over-practicing.
  - Flat, rising or "too wiggly": the skill is probably badly defined (it lumps different things together) and worth splitting or refining.
- DataShop fits the Additive Factors Model: the chance that student i gets step j right depends on the student's proficiency, the difficulty of each knowledge component on the step, and that component's learning rate times the student's prior opportunities on it. Alternative skill maps are compared by AIC, BIC and cross-validation. The model has one parameter per student and two per knowledge component, so it is fitted on pooled data from a population of students. [DataShopLC] [Koedinger2012EDM]
- Carnegie Learning uses two workspace-level signals to choose what to redesign: the share of students who hit the problem cap without mastery, and how well its knowledge tracing predicts answers in that workspace. [Fancsali2021]

Outcome values beyond the standard three: `JIT` (an error that matched a tracked pattern and got a just-in-time message), `FREEBIE_JIT` (the same, without lowering the skill estimate), `ERROR` (an untracked error), `INITIAL_HINT` and `HINT_LEVEL_CHANGE`. Help Level runs 0 (no hint), 1 (goal setting), 2 (teach), 3 (bottom out). [MATHiaDataShop]

#### ASSISTments logs

Problem identifier, correctness, type of help sought, number of hints requested, time in seconds, attempt count, discrete score and continuous score per problem, plus one row per click in the action log. [Adjei2021] [ETRIALS2025]

#### xAPI (Experience API, ADL)

A statement is a JSON object. Required: `actor` (who), `verb` (an identifier for the action, with a display name), `object` (the activity). Recommended: `id`. Optional: `result`, `context`, `timestamp`, `authority`, `attachments`. The store adds `stored`. The `result` object holds `score` (`scaled` from -1 to 1, `raw`, `min`, `max`), `success` (true or false), `completion` (true or false), `response` (a string) and `duration`, plus `extensions`. The verb vocabulary is open, so two products can log the same event differently unless they agree on a profile. [xAPI]

#### Caliper Analytics 1.2 (1EdTech, formerly IMS Global)

An event requires `id` (a UUID), `type`, `actor`, `action`, `object` and `eventTime` (UTC, ISO 8601 with milliseconds). Optional: `profile`, `generated`, `target`, `edApp`, `session` and `extensions` among others. Event types are fixed by "metric profiles". The relevant ones: `AssessmentItemEvent` with actions Started, Skipped and Completed (a completed item can generate a `Response`), and `GradeEvent` with action Graded on an `Attempt`, generating a `Score` with `scoreGiven` and `maxScore`. Caliper's vocabulary is closed and school-shaped, where xAPI's is open. [Caliper]

#### What the three formats share and where they differ

- All three record who, what item, what happened and when.
- Only the DataShop format has first-class fields for the step within a problem, the hint level, the number of hints available, the knowledge component, the attempt number and the experimental condition. Those are the fields that knowledge tracing, learning curves and A/B analysis read. xAPI and Caliper can hold them only as extensions.

## Disagreements and open questions

- **Is the Digital Tutor result real or an artifact?** For: an outside assessor, hands-on job-sample tests, the same pattern across five assessments, and a veterans follow-up with employment outcomes. Against: 12 selected learners per group, tests built around the course's own objectives, comparison courses with different goals, and the general finding that local tests give effects about five times those on standardized tests (0.73 against 0.13). The meta-analysis co-written by the report's first author capped the result as an outlier. The one segment delivered as lectures came out level with the comparison groups, which fits the claim that the captured tutoring did the work, and equally fits a test aligned to the tutored content. [Fletcher2014] [KulikFletcher2016]
- **Counter or probability for mastery?** MATHia uses a fitted probability per skill. ASSISTments, ALEKS, Khan Academy and Math Academy use counters, and ALEKS tested its counter thresholds by randomizing them. The stream 3 file reports that counters and knowledge tracing declare mastery at nearly the same moment. The deployed record adds one point: the systems with counters also run a delayed confirmation step, and MATHia in the sources I read relies on the in-session estimate alone. [Fancsali2021] [Matayoshi2025] [KhanHelp]
- **Should a hint count against mastery?** ASSISTments: yes, fully or by a third per hint. MATHia: only if the hint gives the answer. ALEKS: the explanation is free and the next answer earns less. Andes: help is free and deliberately left for the student to request. The Digital Tutor rarely gives hints at all. Each choice has a published system behind it and none has a head-to-head test in what I read. [Adjei2021] [MATHiaSupport] [Matayoshi2025] [VanLehn2005] [Fletcher2014]
- **What to do when a student keeps failing?** Four different answers are in production: move on and tell a human (MATHia), stop for the day (ASSISTments), switch topic and come back (ALEKS, Math Academy), step back to a prerequisite (alta at once, Math Academy on the second failure). Math Academy's own figure, 99 percent passing within two attempts after a pause, suggests a rest alone often suffices. Alta's figure, 80 percent of refresher cases needing only one hop, suggests the nearest prerequisite is usually enough. Both are vendor numbers on selected populations. [RitterFancsali2016] [ASSISTmentsSB] [Matayoshi2025] [MathAcademyWay] [KnewtonAlta]
- **Hold time fixed or hold mastery fixed?** School systems hold mastery fixed and let time vary, then meet the pacing problem and teacher overrides. The Digital Tutor held time fixed and let achievement vary upward. The school studies show the first approach leaves weak students stranded when the calendar wins. The second has one published instance. [Ritter2016] [Fletcher2014]
- **Does dialogue add anything over good text?** AutoTutor's review says about 0.8 SD over reading. The seven Why2 experiments say dialogue wins only when the text is above the student's level. The two can both hold if AutoTutor's text controls were less well matched to the students. [Nye2014] [VanLehn2007]
- **Engagement against learning.** Duolingo's team reports that Birdbrain raised both, after years of trading one for the other, and the size of the learning gain is unpublished. Its streak work shows that easing a rule (one lesson keeps the streak) raised return rates and lowered the share meeting their daily goal. Stream 4 found a 95 percent success target gave the best short-term retention of users and the weakest learning. [DuoSpectrum] [DuoStreaks]
- **Retention of content: how much does the product need to schedule?** ALEKS data say later learning that builds on a topic protects it (0.8 against 0.6). ASSISTments data say explicit retests help. Math Academy combines the two by giving prerequisite topics partial review credit when a later topic is reviewed. The size of that credit is unpublished. [Cosyn2021] [ARRS2014] [Hecker2025]
- **Why did ALEKS do better over a semester than over a year?** The meta-analysis reports it and offers no tested explanation. A novelty effect, falling use, and weaker designs in short studies are all open. [Fang2019]
- **Open: adults.** Apart from the Digital Tutor, the workplace evidence is logs and before-and-after comparisons. A randomized trial of an adaptive system with working professionals and a skill test as the outcome is still to be found.
- **Open: authoring cost with models.** The published ratios (200 to 300 hours per hour, 50 to 100 with tools, 15 to 40 hours per tutorial) predate model drafting. Duolingo's "12 years for 100 courses, one year for 148" is the only deployed before-and-after figure I found, and it mixes model drafting with reuse of one base course. [Aleven2009] [BESTNetwork] [Duo148]
- **Open: what a scripted model tutor's script must hold.** Synthesis publishes that its lessons are scripted and checked. The Digital Tutor report lists the tactics and the three engines. Neither publishes the script format. [Synthesis] [Fletcher2014]

## What the sources themselves recommend

Each line is a practice a source prescribes, with the source. These are the sources' positions.

- **Capture expert tutors, then build.** "The fundamental strategy for development was first to capture for computer delivery the practice of individuals who were expert both in a relevant area of IT and in one-on-one tutoring." Choose tutors for tutoring skill as well as subject expertise, by audition. [Fletcher2014]
- **Analyze the job before writing content.** "No activity is more essential to the success of a training program than determining its objectives." Interview acknowledged experts for the experiences and problems that made them expert. [Fletcher2014]
- **Ask, and hold the answer.** Prompt for explanations and implications, question right answers as well as wrong ones, rarely give a direct hint, never state the misconception, and verify understanding of explanatory material before proceeding. [Fletcher2014]
- **Build in segments and test each one.** "Building the Tutor segment by segment, testing each empirically, and then revising until a satisfactory level of effectiveness was achieved." [Fletcher2014]
- **Keep a person on call.** A recommender should decide when the dialogue needs a human mentor. [Fletcher2014]
- **Give short, immediate, directed error messages, and present the tutor as a tool.** "The tutors appear to work better if they present themselves to students as nonhuman tools to assist learning rather than as emulations of human tutors." [Anderson1995]
- **Flag errors at once and leave substantive help for the student to request.** Give unrequested help only for slips. [VanLehn2005]
- **Name the violated principle and leave the repair to the student.** Add positive feedback as well as error feedback. Pick problems that will produce a manageable number of feedback messages. [Mitrovic2012] [Mitrovic2016]
- **Cap unproductive practice and route the student elsewhere.** "Setting a max number of problems ensures that students do not endlessly struggle unproductively." When the cap is hit, "an alternate instructional approach is preferable". [Fancsali2021] [RitterFancsali2016]
- **Use failure rates per unit of content to decide what to rewrite.** [Fancsali2021] [MathAcademyWay]
- **Raise pass rates by adding scaffolding, and check that later quiz performance holds.** [MathAcademyWay]
- **After a failure, pause and return later. After a second failure at the same point, review the specific prerequisite that point uses.** [MathAcademyWay]
- **Assess first, and place the student at the edge of what they know.** [Falmagne2006] [MathAcademyWay]
- **Check the student model continuously with an extra, unscored question.** [Falmagne2006]
- **Reassess after mastery on an expanding schedule, and relearn on a miss.** [PASS2015] [ARRS2014]
- **Target more review "over the first several days after the item is learned".** [Matayoshi2018]
- **Space reviews in proportion to how long the knowledge must last.** The gap between sessions should be roughly 10 to 20 percent of the retention interval. [Kim2019]
- **Let the learner pick a recall target and accept the workload it implies.** Anki's default is 90 percent, and its manual advises keeping the target under 97. [AnkiDocs]
- **Lower the bar for coming back.** "Lowering the barriers to building a consistent daily habit is more important to successfully learning a language than how much you learn each day, at least early on." Give learners a way to take a break without losing progress. [DuoStreaks]
- **Run a new student model in shadow mode before it drives lessons.** [DuoSpectrum]
- **A/B test every change, and expect about half to fail.** [DuoCiti2026]
- **Keep people in the authoring loop for generated content.** The designer plans the lesson, the model drafts, the designer selects and edits. For batch content show learners only the best share of output. For live content write the scenario and the opening. [DuoLLM2023] [DuoUCFL2025] [DuoMax2023]
- **Do not show learners unchecked model output.** [UnderstandReview] [Synthesis]
- **Crowdsource help from vetted practitioners and test each piece against the next problem.** [Patikorn2020]
- **Assist the teacher, and give them a report of who struggled on what.** [Roschelle2016]
- **Give a new program more than one year before judging it.** "Benefits of PL may take some time to emerge." The Cognitive Tutor effect appeared in year two. [RAND2017Brief] [RANDBrief2013]
- **Train and coach the people who run the program, and protect practice time.** Gains appear at about 35 minutes a week or more, with teachers who buy in, plan, and monitor progress. [Oreopoulos2026]
- **Report the share of students who reach the recommended dose.** [Holt2024]
- **Use standardized or independent outcome measures, preregister, and note who paid for the study.** [KulikFletcher2016] [Wolf2020]
- **Explain the product in plain terms and publish reproducible evidence.** "Explain what the damned thing does in clear, concrete, and straightforward language, with real-world examples when possible." [Feldstein2015]
- **Take a tinkerer's stance.** Expect incremental gains, in domains where answers can be checked by machine. [Reich2020LAS]
- **Refine the skill map from learning curves.** A flat, rising or jagged curve means the skill is badly defined. [DataShopLC]
- **Log to a fixed, shared format so one analysis serves many studies.** [ETRIALS2025] [DataShopImport]
- **In self-paced software, tie experiments to decision points in the content, track prior exposure, and decide in advance how to keep a student's experience consistent.** [UpGrade2020] [MurphyRitter2022]
