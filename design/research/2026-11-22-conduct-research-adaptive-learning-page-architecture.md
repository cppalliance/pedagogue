## Top findings

1. **In the five LLM tutor trials read for this question, a human fixed the problems, the answers and the order beforehand, and the model generated only the help around them.** This covers these five trials and stops short of "every trial": three studies in this file had the model write the questions or the outline as well and still report gains (AutoPBL in finding 5, Learn Your Way in finding 4, the German primary-school trial in finding 24), and two of the five show their gain on near measures only. Harvard physics (N = 194, +0.63 SD): each prompt held an instructor's step-by-step answer, and the platform took over the order of parts because "a system prompt could not reliably provide enough structure". Turkish high school (about 1,000 students): teacher-written solutions and common mistakes in every prompt of the guarded tutor, which raised practice scores and left the later unassisted exam level with the control group. Math hints (N = 274): the model wrote the hints ahead of time, one author screened them, and they were stored. UK classrooms (N = 165 and N = 1,179): expert-written misconception explanations in the prompt and a human tutor approving each message, with gains on the next question and a mid-trial quiz and no gain over the static package on the standardized test. Grade A for the trials, B for the pattern. [Kestin2025] [Bastani2025] [Pardos2024] [LearnLMEedi2025] [LearnLMEedi2026]
2. **Fixed expert-written remediation matched live AI tutoring on a standardized test, and lost to it on the immediate retry.** Over 10 weeks (N = 1,179), AI tutoring differed from the platform's static package (curated videos, fluency exercises, an expert-written hint per misconception) by -0.01 SD [-0.15, +0.14] and +0.02 SD [-0.12, +0.16] on Star Maths. On the very next attempt at the same question, live tutoring reached 93.0% (AI, supervised) and 91.2% (human) against 65.4% for the static hint (N = 165). Grade A. [LearnLMEedi2026] [LearnLMEedi2025]
3. **Model drafts that were generated ahead, checked and stored taught as well as human-written hints in the one trial that tested it. Unchecked drafts have error rates between 5% and 40%.** In that trial (N = 274 online adults, a test repeated right after practice) gains with stored model hints and with human tutor hints did not differ (p = 0.416). 32% of raw ChatGPT hints on 75 math problems were wrong, 0 to 13% after ten-way voting, and a check took 37.6 seconds per hint. 5.2% of 291 tutorial blocks generated at runtime held errors. 40% of 20 model-written medical multiple choice questions had a major error, including 15% with the wrong option keyed and 15% with two correct options. Teachers judged 70% of model-written stems and keys valid and 37% of the distractors with their feedback. Grade A to B. [Pardos2024] [AutoPBL2025] [Thoracic2025] [HEDGE2024]
4. **The one randomized trial of an AI-generated textbook found a gain, and it cannot be credited to generation as such.** Learn Your Way: 77% against 68% on an immediate test and 78% against 67% three to seven days later (N = 60, p = 0.03 each on one-sided tests), against a PDF reader. The source chapter and both tests were human-written. The generated layer added embedded questions and quizzes, and every treatment student used the quizzes. A second trial (N = 214) of a model-written explanation against textbook examples found equal test scores (p = .750) with better interest and lower reported load. Grade A, small samples. [LearnYourWay2025] [LearnYourWay2026] [PhysicsAI2025]
5. **A hybrid close to option D has one direct test.** AutoPBL fixes a two-level outline in advance, generates one bite-sized block at a time from it, and puts a checkpoint question after every block. 29 beginners scored higher on quizzes than with a static tutorial plus a chatbot (p = 0.02 and 0.03), spent 85 minutes against 58, and cost $2 to $3 of model calls per session. 94.8% of blocks were error-free, and the authors plan to add human review and to cut waiting time. Grade B. [AutoPBL2025]
6. **Fully pre-authored courseware with embedded practice matches or beats lecture courses in less time, at a high build cost.** OLI statistics: a semester's material in 8 weeks against 15, with an 18-point gain on a statistics literacy test against 3 points (22 against 42 students). Six-campus trial (N = 605): pass rates 80% against 76%, not significant (figures as the What Works Clearinghouse review gives them), in about 25% less student time. A course of this kind cost roughly $500,000 to $1,000,000 to build (secondary source). Grade A for outcomes, C for cost. [Lovett2008] [Bowen2014] [WWCBowen2014] [Rhoads2013]
7. **A textbook with less text and more embedded activity beats a static one, most of all for the least prepared.** Randomized, one lesson, N = 136: 16% more improvement (p = .016) and 64% more in the lowest quartile. A minimal-text version of a lesson gave 2.4 points of improvement against 1.1 (N = 168). A few course points lifted activity completion from about 50% to over 90%. One author (Vahid) co-founded the company that sells the book, and his co-authors list an affiliation with it. Grade A to B. [Edgcomb2014] [Edgcomb2015] [EdgcombVahid2016]
8. **Questions set into the text help on what they ask about and on related material, and their exact position matters less than their presence and feedback.** Hamaker's review of 61 experiments: strong effect on repeated questions, weaker on related ones, and inserted against end-of-text questions did not differ for unrelated material (.01 against -.02). One 2022 experiment (N = 76) favored questions placed right after the relevant passage (d = 0.59 five days later). One textbook-chapter study found placement made no difference and answering twice beat answering once. Grade A to B. [Hamaker1986] [Rubio2022] [UnerRoediger2018]
9. **Machine-generated practice questions inside human-written textbooks behave like human-written ones at scale, and the vendor chose rule-based generation over a language model for reliability.** Same students, same course (N = 109): comparable engagement, difficulty and persistence. At scale: fill-in-the-blank 61.4% first-try correct and 62.1% persistence over 11.9 million sessions, matching 80.3% and 72.8% over 4.0 million, and 1.94 thumbs-down per 1,000 sessions. A monitoring service removes weak questions after release. Grade B, vendor research. [VanCampenhout2021] [VanCampenhout2025]
10. **In adaptive textbooks of the 1990s, adapting what is on the page helped comprehension, and adapting links helped only readers who left the linear path.** Adaptive stretchtext gave faster and better comprehension. Adaptive link annotation gave no test gain for 25 novices, about 80% of whose moves were Continue and Back. Beginners stayed longer when given a single NEXT button, and experienced learners stayed longer with annotated links. Grade B, small and old studies. [DeBra1999] [Brusilovsky2003] [WeberBrusilovsky2001]
11. **Structured lesson materials raise learning, and word-for-word scripting adds little beyond structure.** Structured pedagogy programs: 0.23 SD in language and 0.14 SD in mathematics. Across 19 projects, teacher guides added 6.1 correct words per minute, and each 10 points more on a scripting index went with 1.4 fewer (12% of variance, stated as non-causal). Direct Instruction scripts are field-tested and revised twice before release. Paraprofessional tutoring programs reached 0.40 SD, with results that the J-PAL review by the same team calls "substantially more consistent" than teacher tutoring, and the authors could not code how structured each program was. Grade A to B. [Snilstveit2016] [Piper2018] [Stockard2018] [Nickow2020] [JPALTutoring]
12. **Models are reliable at one tool call and much less reliable over a long, stateful sequence.** One model in the Berkeley benchmark scored 77 to 94% on single-turn calls, 62.5% on basic multi-turn tasks, 37.5% when a parameter was missing and 6.0% when the needed function was missing. Tool selection fell from about 98% with 10 tools to about 88% with 100. Grade B. [BFCL2025] [HumanMCP2025] [LongFuncEval2025]
13. **Schema enforcement fixes the shape of the output, and its cost to content depends on where the reasoning goes.** Across eight schema suites, constrained decoding kept 69 to 100% of outputs schema-valid where the bare model kept 13 to 90%. One study found format restrictions lowered reasoning accuracy, and a rebuttal found parity once the reasoning field came before the answer. Grade B. [JSONSchemaBench2025] [Tam2024] [StructuredRebuttal]
14. **Free-form generated interfaces are preferred to markdown, take a minute or two, and still trail human experts.** Raters preferred the generated page to markdown 82.8% of the time and preferred an expert-built page to the generated one 50.0% against 35.3%. Output errors were 0% on the three newest models and 29 to 60% on two older ones. A second group got its best results by generating a structured plan (flows and state machines) before any code. Preference was measured, learning was not. Grade B. [GenUIGoogle2026] [GenUIStanford2025]
15. **The same prompt gives different output on different runs, even at temperature zero.** Five models, eight tasks, ten runs each: accuracy moved by up to 15 points between runs, and identical raw text across all ten runs was rare. Grade B. [Atil2024]
16. **Live generation costs seconds and money per page, and a stored page replays at once.** Vendor traffic from September to November 2026: median 1.9 to 7.7 seconds to the first token, 8.9 to 61 seconds at the 95th percentile, 51 to 290 tokens per second, $0.50 to $50 per million output tokens. In experiments, a wait above 4 seconds lowered ratings of a spoken agent, and a 2-second text reply was rated less thoughtful than a 9 or 20 second one with no change in behavior (N = 240). The 0.1, 1 and 10 second limits trace to a 1968 paper that Nielsen himself described as lacking empirical evidence. Grade B to C. [Requesty2026] [VRDelay2025] [LatencyCHI2026] [Nielsen1993] [NielsenUsenet1991]
17. **Authoring by example cut tutor-building cost four to eight times, and a parameterized template pays for itself from the third instance.** Example-tracing tutors: 50 to 100 hours per hour of instruction against 200 to 300. ASSISTments: 28 to 40. A variabilized template took 38.8 minutes to build against 20.18 for one copy, then 7.76 minutes per instance. An AutoTutor script is a main question, 2 to 10 expectations, 0 to 5 misconceptions and a hint ladder. Grade B to C. [Aleven2016] [Razzaq2009] [WPIVariabilization] [Nye2014]
18. **Every mature content format separates blocks from the engine, keeps the answer and the scoring rule inside the item, and validates structure. The text-first ones are chosen for diffs, forks and long life.** QTI is XML with a schema. H5P and OLI Torus are JSON. PreTeXt is XML that its own guide says can need "more characters of markup than of content". Runestone ended new work on its reStructuredText format in 2026 in favor of PreTeXt. LiaScript stays readable "with every editor". The evidence is practitioner rationale. Grade C to D. [QTI3] [H5P] [Torus] [PreTeXt] [Runestone2026] [LiaScript]
19. **A block that fits every lesson teaches less in any one of them.** Wiley's reusability paradox: the context inside a learning object raises its teaching value and lowers the number of places it fits. Grade D. [Wiley2004] [Carpentries2015]
20. **Page design moves learning by a small to medium amount, and the effects are confirmed by independent meta-analyses.** Signaling g = 0.53 for retention and 0.33 for transfer. Segmenting d = 0.32 and 0.36. Text next to its picture g = 0.63. Interesting but irrelevant detail g = -0.33, and -0.52 for low prior knowledge. Conversational wording d = 0.30 and 0.54, fading in lessons longer than 35 minutes. An on-screen agent g = 0.19 to 0.20. Grade A. [Schneider2018] [Rey2019] [SchroederCenkci2018] [Sundararajan2020] [SundarAFT2020] [Ginns2013] [Schroeder2013] [CastroAlonso2021]
21. **One long scrolling page costs comprehension for some readers, and one section per page helped.** Reading on screens trails paper by g = -0.21 (54 studies), -0.25 where scrolling was needed and -0.13 where it was not (the difference was not significant). In one experiment (N = 40) a 13-section text read as separate pages beat the same text as one scroll, most for readers with low working memory. Grade A to B. [Delgado2018] [SanchezWiley2009]
22. **A progress display helps only when early progress looks fast.** Across 32 survey experiments a steady progress bar did not cut drop-off, a fast-then-slow bar cut it and a slow-then-fast bar raised it. Coffee-card buyers sped up about 20% as they neared the reward, and a card with two free stamps out of ten was completed by 34% against 19% for an eight-stamp card. These are surveys and loyalty cards. Grade A for surveys, B for transfer to lessons. [Villar2013] [Kivetz2006] [NunesDreze2006]
23. **Two sources side by side raise mental load, and in one study attention to a video lesson with a chatbot beside it fell off faster over time.** Side-by-side video and application raised pupil-measured load over toggling (N = 42). With a chatbot beside a video lesson, gaze on the lesson area was higher overall and fell faster over time (n = 151). The measured area was the video plus the chatbot for that group, so the study leaves open whether the chat pulled attention away from the lesson. Readers preferred AI notes in the margin of the text to a side chat (65% ranked it first) with equal comprehension. Matching colors between text and diagram raised retention (82.42 against 69.78) and transfer (63.85 against 47.88) in one study (N = 52). Grade B. [Kueker2024] [ConvAIEye2024] [MarginNotes2025] [Ozcelik2009]
24. **Tying material to a learner's interests helps, and the best-known results used pre-written variants picked per student.** Algebra tutor (N = 145): 53.66% against 43.2% correct on the hardest skill, still ahead four units later. Meta-analysis (34 publications): g = 0.48 retention, 0.36 transfer, 0.55 interest. Live LLM personalization has one positive trial in primary school (N = 114) and null accuracy results elsewhere. Grade A. [Walkington2013] [Lin2024] [GAIContext2025] [TPWP] [Karerwa2026]
25. **Deployed products keep a human between the model and the learner for lesson content.** Duolingo: the model writes ten exercises from a template, a designer keeps three and edits them. Khan Academy: the tutor is most accurate where it reads human-written hints first. Synthesis: lessons are scripted and reviewed. The US Army's tutoring framework advises automating authoring only up to the level where "the human still approves all decisions". Grade C. [DuolingoLLM] [KhanMath2025] [KhanJansepar2023] [SynthesisAbout] [Ososky2017]

## Findings by topic

### 1. Adaptive hypermedia and intelligent textbooks

- **Finding 1.1: The field settled early on two things a system can adapt: what is on the page, and which links the reader is steered toward.**
  - **Evidence:** Brusilovsky's 1996 review names adaptive presentation (the content of a page changes with the reader's knowledge) and adaptive navigation support (direct guidance through a "next" button, sorting, hiding and annotating links). The 2001 update lists the working techniques for canned text: inserting or removing fragments, stretchtext (a fragment that expands or collapses in place), altering fragments, sorting fragments and dimming fragments. In all of them a human wrote every fragment and the system chose which to show.
  - **Caveats:** These are taxonomies. They describe what was built.
  - **Grade:** D (taxonomy).
  - **Sources:** [Brusilovsky1996] [Brusilovsky2001] [DeBra1999]
- **Finding 1.2: Adapting the content of the page improved comprehension in the one thorough early test.**
  - **Evidence:** MetaDoc (Boyle and Encarnacion 1994) showed novices extra explanations and experts low-level detail through stretchtext. Against a plain hypertext version, readers of the adaptive version answered comprehension questions significantly faster and with significantly better comprehension. Search and navigation tasks showed equal correctness, nodes visited and operations.
  - **Caveats:** One system, a UNIX manual, reported here through two review papers. Sample size and effect sizes were not in the passages I read.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [DeBra1999] [Brusilovsky1996]
- **Finding 1.3: Link annotation helped learners who navigate freely and did nothing measurable for novices, who follow the linear path.**
  - **Evidence:** ISIS-Tutor (26 computer science freshmen, three versions): significantly fewer navigation steps and fewer repeated task attempts with adaptive annotation, and a time saving that did not reach significance. InterBook (25 teacher-education students, random split): equal test means, with about 80% of navigation steps made with the Continue and Back buttons or in-text hot words, none of which were annotated. A repeat with every link annotated again found equal scores. ELM-ART (random assignment of web visitors to four versions): people with no programming experience stayed longer when a NEXT button guided them, and people who knew another language stayed longer with annotated links. A separate 2009 experiment found direct guidance better than an unsupported version (p < 0.01) and a non-adaptive version (p < 0.05).
  - **Caveats:** Samples of 25 to 26. "Stayed longer" is a proxy for learning. Brusilovsky's own conclusion: "adaptive annotation is not a silver bullet".
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Brusilovsky2003] [WeberBrusilovsky2001] [DirectGuidance2009]
- **Finding 1.4: The 2022 history of intelligent textbooks names expert knowledge engineering as the reason the first generation did not scale, and the 2025 workshop is mostly language-model work measured on text quality.**
  - **Evidence:** "The reliance on expensive knowledge engineering made these approaches hard to scale" (domain experts listed concepts and tagged every fragment). Later generations moved to shared ontologies, then to automatic extraction and mining of reading data. At the 2025 iTextbooks workshop: a live simplifier in an e-reader lowered reading grade level by 7.37 on average across 54,371 student requests, with 94.5% of outputs above the authors' meaning-preservation threshold, generated at temperature 0 with no check after generation, and with comprehension left for future work. An LLM-enhanced interactive text in a computer science class was compared with a static digital text using 72 matched pairs, with a post-test advantage reported for higher-performing students. A side-by-side chat and PDF reader was preferred by 29 of 30 graduate students, mainly because the book let them verify the model.
  - **Caveats:** The workshop papers are short, and two of the three report usage or preference. The matched-pairs study is quasi-experimental and I read only its abstract and setup.
  - **Grade:** C to B.
  - **Sources:** [Brusilovsky2022] [iTextbooks2025] [Simplifier2025] [Crossley2025] [MuDoC2025]

### 2. Courseware built from pre-authored pages with embedded practice

- **Finding 2.1: OLI courses are built objective-first by a team, with a skills map tying every activity to a skill.**
  - **Evidence:** Each concept starts with one or more observable learning objectives, then short exposition (text, images, simulations, 3 to 5 minute videos, worked examples), with interactive tasks "interspersed with the exposition", hints on request and feedback that "targets common student misconceptions". A typical course has about 30 to 50 learning objectives and 100 to 1,000 skills. A skill can serve more than one objective, and each activity, question or question part can be tagged with one or more skills. The stated working rule: "Objectives are decomposed as little as possible during first development of the model; reductionism for its own sake is to be avoided", and decomposition stops at the point where the skill is "actionable". Teams combine faculty content experts, learning scientists, interface specialists and software engineers.
  - **Caveats:** Descriptions by the developers. The skills map is refined later from student data, which a new course lacks.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [Bier2014] [StraderThille2012] [ThilleSmith2010]
- **Finding 2.2: A fully pre-authored interactive course taught a semester of statistics in about half the time with equal or better results.**
  - **Evidence:** Carnegie Mellon, spring 2007. From 68 volunteers, 22 were randomly chosen for the OLI course in hybrid mode (8 weeks, two 50-minute meetings a week) and 42 served as controls in the 15-week lecture course. On the CAOS statistics literacy test the OLI group gained 18 percentage points (t(20) = 6.9, p < .01) and the control group 3 points (not different from zero), and the difference was significant (t(46) = 4.0, p < .01). The authors summarize that OLI students "took half the time to learn as much or more". A retention study followed 7 months later for the OLI group and 5 months later for controls.
  - **Caveats:** Volunteers at a selective university, 22 treated students, and instructors used the system's reports to plan each meeting, so the result belongs to the course plus a teacher. Stream 1 notes that two What Works Clearinghouse pages print different post-test figures for this study.
  - **Grade:** A, single small study.
  - **Sources:** [Lovett2008]
- **Finding 2.3: At six public campuses the same course in hybrid form gave the same outcomes as the lecture course.**
  - **Evidence:** 605 students (313 hybrid, 292 traditional), randomized within campus. Pass rates 80% against 76% (effect size 0.10, not significant). Standardized test about one point higher and common final-exam questions about two points higher for hybrid, neither significant. The authors call these "fairly precisely estimated" zeros. Hybrid students spent about 1.7 fewer hours a week in total (about 25% less) and rated the course about 11% lower. Simulated instructor-pay savings were 36 to 57% against all-section teaching and 19% against lecture plus sections, which the authors call speculative.
  - **Caveats:** Hybrid, with one hour of class a week. Students liked it somewhat less.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Bowen2014] [WWCBowen2014]
- **Finding 2.4: Building a course of this kind was expensive.**
  - **Evidence:** A 2013 journal article, citing Walsh's book on open courseware, puts OLI at "anywhere from $500,000 to $1,000,000 to develop a single course", with early courses near $1,000,000 and later ones about $500,000. A 2012 news page gives the same range.
  - **Caveats:** Secondary sources. The figure includes platform software.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [Rhoads2013] [OpenCulture2012]
- **Finding 2.5: Interactive textbooks with short text and many embedded activities beat static text, and students do the activities when they count for a little credit.**
  - **Evidence:** Randomized lesson study in an introductory programming course (136 analyzed): pre to post improvement 6.4 of 11 against 5.5 (16% more, p = .016). In the lowest pre-test quartile the interactive lesson gave 7.4 of 11, which the authors report as 64% more improvement than the static lesson (p < .001). Students chose to spend 17.5 minutes against 9.4. Cross-semester comparison (1,945 students, 4 classes, 3 universities, same instructors): course grade up 0.28 on a 0 to 4 scale, lower quartile up 0.38, exam scores up 13.6%. Text length (168 analyzed of 307): a minimal-text lesson (1 to 2 sentences then examples) gave 2.4 points of improvement against 1.1 for normal text (6 to 12 sentences), p < .01, with slightly more time spent on the shorter text. Engagement: 5 to 10 course points out of 100 gave over 90% activity completion against about 50% with zero points (1,394 students, 8 classes). 80 to 90% of students answered in earnest and fewer than 3% gamed the system.
  - **Caveats:** Vahid co-founded zyBooks, and Edgcomb and Lysecky list an affiliation with it. The randomized studies are single lessons with immediate tests. The cross-semester study compares cohorts. In the text-length study, students from the last three days were excluded for rushing.
  - **Grade:** A for the single-lesson trials, B for the rest. Vendor-affiliated.
  - **Sources:** [Edgcomb2014] [Edgcomb2015] [EdgcombVahid2016] [Edgcomb2017]
- **Finding 2.6: In Runestone ebooks, practice that adapts within the problem was finished more often and taught as much as writing code in less time.**
  - **Evidence:** Randomized four-condition study with worked-example and practice pairs. Gain against an off-task control: d = 0.799 for adaptive Parsons problems (significant), d = 0.311 for fixed Parsons problems and d = 0.133 for writing code (both not significant against control). The adaptation removes distractors or merges blocks when the learner struggles, all from pre-authored problems.
  - **Caveats:** One session plus a one-week delayed test, in one course.
  - **Grade:** A, single study.
  - **Sources:** [Ericson2018]
- **Finding 2.7: OpenStax Tutor's public evidence is a pilot.**
  - **Evidence:** 207 high school students in a 2015 to 2016 pilot. The system asks for a short free-text answer and then a multiple-choice pick, and re-asks earlier topics about three weeks later. Giving a valid free-text answer predicted success on the later spaced question. A news page quotes a researcher saying more frequent retrieval was "worth about a half letter grade".
  - **Caveats:** Observational. My search for a controlled efficacy trial of OpenStax Tutor returned these two items.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [Waters2017] [InformationWeekOpenStax]
- **Finding 2.8: Adjunct questions raise learning of what they ask about, help related material less, and can lower learning of unrelated material when study time is fixed.**
  - **Evidence:** Hamaker (1986) reviewed 61 published experiments (50 used for factual questions and 21 for higher-order questions), all published 1966 to 1982, all without detailed feedback. Abstract: "Adjunct questions of all cognitive levels have a strong facilitative effect on repeated test questions and a weaker effect on test questions related to the adjunct questions. Unrelated test questions are affected negatively by factual prequestions and by factual postquestions when study time is controlled." Effects varied with text length, question density, question format and control-group performance. They did not vary with age, the delay before the test, access to the text while answering, or distance between question and passage. Higher-order questions helped more broadly than factual ones. Inserted postquestions against massed (end of text) postquestions on unrelated items: .01 against -.02, not significant.
  - **Caveats:** Published studies only, which the author says may inflate effects. Studies with feedback were excluded, and modern embedded quizzes give feedback. The effect metric is the difference between the question group and the control group in the proportion of test questions answered correctly.
  - **Grade:** A (meta-analysis, old).
  - **Sources:** [Hamaker1986]
- **Finding 2.9: Newer experiments on where to put the questions disagree.**
  - **Evidence:** 76 university freshmen read two science texts with ten questions and the text available. Questions inserted right after the relevant passage gave better learning five days later than the same questions after the whole text (6.22 against 5.08, d = 0.59, p = .011), with more initial reading time and more accurate elaborations. In a biology textbook chapter study with a test two days later, answering questions beat rereading, placement after each section or after the chapter made no difference, and answering twice beat once. In an e-textbook excerpt study (N = 390), elaborative feedback at the end of the text gave the best post-test scores and learning efficiency, ahead of feedback in the middle and at the end.
  - **Caveats:** Three single studies with different materials and delays. The second and third were read as abstracts.
  - **Grade:** A to B.
  - **Sources:** [Rubio2022] [UnerRoediger2018] [FeedbackPlacement2024]

### 3. Machine-generated content inside a human-written textbook

- **Finding 3.1: Automatically generated questions, placed in a human-written textbook, were used by students the same way as human-written ones.**
  - **Evidence:** First study: 109 students in one university communication course answered both kinds mixed together. A mixed-effects logistic regression found them similar on engagement, difficulty and persistence. The difference that mattered was the kind of thinking asked for: recognition questions (matching, multiple choice) were easier and kept students trying longer than recall questions (fill in the blank), "regardless of method of creation". At scale in the Bookshelf e-reader, from the feature's launch in January 2022 to a June cut-off (the year was cut off in my extract): matching 4,028,835 sessions, 80.3% first-try correct, 72.8% persistence after a miss; fill in the blank 11,912,905 sessions, 61.4% and 62.1%; multiple choice 205,774 sessions, 74.1% and 76.1%. Thumbs-down ratings ran at 1.94 per 1,000 sessions across 3,594,408 sessions. About 12% of fill-in-the-blank interactions had a non-genuine entry, and nearly half of those students kept going to the right answer.
  - **Caveats:** Vendor research. The measures are usage and difficulty. Learning outcomes rest on the separate "doer effect" literature covered in stream 2.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [VanCampenhout2021] [VanCampenhout2025] [VitalSourceAQG]
- **Finding 3.2: The generator behind those questions is rule-based, the textbook is the only source, and quality is policed after release.**
  - **Evidence:** The pipeline uses syntactic and semantic parsing with the spaCy library, and "the procedure of transformation is primarily rule-based". It was built without large language models because they "lacked sufficient reliability at the time" and because "their potential to introduce factual inaccuracies posed an ethical concern, especially given the vast number of questions being generated - making human oversight unfeasible at scale". A language model is now used for one job: feedback on open-ended answers, by comparing the answer with the textbook section. A "content improvement service" watches every interaction, estimates difficulty and reads thumbs-down ratings to "determine if questions need to be removed and replaced".
  - **Caveats:** Vendor description.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [VanCampenhout2025]
- **Finding 3.3: Template-based item generation gives predictable psychometrics. Language-model items reach "good first draft" and their weak spots are the answer key, the distractors and difficulty control.**
  - **Evidence:** Template model with GPT-3.5 writing only the sentences, reviewed by three experts and given to 1,607 eighth graders: similar difficulty across generated items, strong discrimination, working distractors. Radiology clerkship (115 students, five questions per source): difficulty index 0.58 for faculty questions and 0.46 for both template and ChatGPT-4o questions; every template question had acceptable or better discrimination; the authors call the result exploratory. Licensing-exam study: model and human items were similar on blueprint fit, relevance, factual errors and key quality, model stems were better, human distractors were better, asking the model for a difficulty level did not work, and "similar prompts frequently generated overlapping content". Review of 60 studies of language-model item generation: only 10 mention validity, and the authors conclude most "neglect measurement properties". Thoracic surgery: of 20 Claude-written questions with an answer key, 8 (40%) had major errors - 3 with two correct answers, 3 with the wrong option marked as the key, 2 medically inaccurate - while the expert-written exam had none (p = 0.001). Math teachers reviewing GPT-4 output judged 14 of 20 stem, key and explanation sets valid (70%) and 37% of the misconception, distractor and feedback sets valid. A study of 651 GPT-4 and 449 human programming questions found the model "capable of producing MCQs with clear language, a single correct choice, and high-quality distractors".
  - **Caveats:** Small item counts in the medical studies (20 and 15 items). Results differ by subject: programming and reading fared better than medicine and mathematics distractors.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [SayinGierl2024] [RadiologyAIG2025] [ItemWriting2025] [AIGReview2025] [Thoracic2025] [HEDGE2024] [Doughty2024]

### 4. Textbooks and pages generated by a model

- **Finding 4.1: Google's Learn Your Way generates a personalized, interactive layer over a human-written chapter, and beat a PDF of the same chapter.**
  - **Evidence:** Design: the source is a human-written textbook section (OpenStax, LibreTexts). Step one rewrites it to a reading grade and swaps selected passages for versions tied to an interest the learner picks from a short list, and highlights the swapped passages. Step two builds an "immersive text" with embedded multiple-choice questions, section quizzes of 5 to 10 questions with feedback, slides, an audio lesson, mnemonics and a mind map. Expert rating: ten source documents, three grade-and-interest settings each, three raters per component, agree, partly or disagree on criteria such as accuracy, coverage, cognitive load and active learning. The overall experience scored above 0.90 on every axis, and generated illustrations scored lowest. Trial: 60 students aged 15 to 18 near Chicago, screened to a similar reading level, randomized to Learn Your Way or Adobe Reader with the same chapter on adolescent brain development, up to 40 minutes. Immediate test 77% against 68% (p = 0.03). Retention test three to seven days later by the journal version (the preprint says it was sent three days after the session and the blog says 3 to 5 days), 58 of 60 completing, 78% against 67% (p = 0.03). Both p values come from one-sided tests in the journal version (p = 0.029 and 0.03). In the journal version: mean 9.2 of 12 (SD 2.2) against 8.2 (SD 2.0), and 4.7 of 6 against 4.0. 100% against 70% said the tool made them comfortable taking the test, and 93% against 67% would use it again.
  - **Caveats:** A preprint and a journal article from the developer. One chapter, one session, 30 students per arm. Both tests were written by a human expert. Every Learn Your Way student used the quizzes and the PDF group had none, so retrieval practice is confounded with generation and personalization. The authors say so: "a natural question is which of these contributes most to learning efficacy. Since the current study did not hone in on this, there might be some transformations that have impact while others do not." Students who used more modalities did not score reliably higher (r = 0.24, p = 0.206).
  - **Grade:** A, small, developer-run.
  - **Sources:** [LearnYourWay2025] [LearnYourWay2026] [GoogleLYWBlog2025]
- **Finding 4.2: A model-written explanation matched textbook examples on a test and felt better to students.**
  - **Evidence:** 214 sixth graders in Germany, randomized. Both groups read the textbook overview of proportional relationships. The control group then read the textbook's examples and the experimental group read an explanation generated beforehand by a custom chatbot from a fixed prompt. Both on paper, 15 minutes. Test scores did not differ (p = .750). The generated material gave higher positive emotion, situational interest and self-efficacy and lower reported cognitive load.
  - **Caveats:** One topic, 15 minutes, immediate test, and the generated text was produced once and printed, so every student saw the same checked text.
  - **Grade:** A, single study.
  - **Sources:** [PhysicsAI2025]
- **Finding 4.3: The closest published system to a hybrid skeleton is AutoPBL, and it worked better than a static tutorial with a chatbot.**
  - **Evidence:** Design: a "Project Designer" agent writes a two-level project outline before the lesson. That outline is shown as a sidebar table of contents. Content then arrives as bite-sized blocks, "each containing a single learning or doing task", and "once the learner finishes a block, the next one will be adaptively generated according to the learner's progress and response to the checkpoint question". Every block ends in a generated multiple-choice or short-answer checkpoint the learner must answer, with a 20-second timer before the question appears. A side chat acts as teaching assistant with quote-and-ask and preset modes, and gives rising levels of help. Quality check: two researchers reviewed 291 generated blocks and found 94.8% free of errors. The two error types were questions that misreferenced earlier blocks and marking an over-general answer as correct. Study: 29 machine-learning beginners, counterbalanced over two projects, against a static tutorial (itself generated by GPT-4o from the same content prompts) with a GPT-4o chatbot. First-session quiz scores were higher with AutoPBL on both projects (p = 0.02 and p = 0.03). Frustration was lower (p = 0.01). Net time was 85 minutes against 58, with block generation time removed from the count. Cost was "in the range of $2 to $3" per session, and the authors are "actively seeking ways to systematically reduce the LLM usage of AutoPBL to lower cost and waiting time". Participants raised trust in generated content as a concern and the authors plan human review. The 20-second timer was the least liked feature.
  - **Caveats:** 29 participants, immediate quiz, quiz effect sizes not reported in the passages I read, one domain, Chinese-language content. The outline itself was model-written, with no expert pass.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [AutoPBL2025]
- **Finding 4.4: One research group built the same tutor in a stored version and a live version, and described the trade in plain terms.**
  - **Evidence:** MWPTutor keeps a hand-designed tutoring state machine (pump, hint, prompt, assertion, in the AutoTutor tradition) and uses a language model to fill in the utterances for each state, either all at design time ("cached") or during the conversation ("live"). On the stored version: "one can reliably say that there would be no 'new' errors or hallucinations since the LLM is not used after the design stage", it runs offline, and stakeholders can "trust the tutor without necessarily trusting the LLM". Against it: "pre-generating means that all possible scenarios that can arise during the conversation need to be anticipated in advance, which is extremely hard to achieve", so the tutor "is likely to feel somewhat oblivious of the context". Their conclusion: "We do not believe that any of these options is better than the other, and the best option might be a combination of both." Their premise: "while LLMs with certain guardrails can take the place of subject experts, the overall pedagogical design still needs to be handcrafted for the best learning results." In their example a free GPT-4 tutor took 29 utterances without reaching the answer where the structured tutor took 6.
  - **Caveats:** Preprint. Evaluated on math word problem datasets with an AI-simulated student, as the paper's introduction states. The engagement cost of stored utterances is argued, not measured.
  - **Grade:** B for the system comparison, D for the trade-off statement.
  - **Sources:** [MWPTutor2024]
- **Finding 4.5: Deployed products generate ahead of time and put a person in front of the learner-facing content.**
  - **Evidence:** Duolingo: a learning designer plans theme, grammar, vocabulary and exercise types. A prompt template holds fixed rules per exercise type and variable slots filled partly by tooling. The model returns ten exercises "in a matter of seconds", the designer picks three and edits them for naturalness, and "our Spanish teaching experts always have the final say". Before this, "every single exercise ... was written, reviewed, edited, and translated by human experts". A separate model then picks which stored exercise each learner sees. Khan Academy: exercises, hints and articles are written by former teachers and reviewed inside and outside the organization. A manual review of tutor conversations found "when Khanmigo has access to human-generated exercises, steps, hints, and solutions prior to making a calculation or evaluation, Khanmigo's accuracy improves", and the architecture was changed so the tutor always fetches them first. An engineering director said of exercise tutoring: "It has the full written hints from our content team. And so that's actually where it does the best job", and that the open "tutor me" mode without a stored answer is "not quite as accurate". Synthesis: the company says educators design the lessons and review "every interaction", and an outside observer of a 2023 demo described "a scripted sequence of messages" with simple branches and a final exercise repeated until correct.
  - **Caveats:** Company blog posts, an interview, a marketing page and one observer's notes. Outcome data for these choices are not published with them.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [DuolingoLLM] [DuolingoExperts] [KhanMath2025] [KhanJansepar2023] [SynthesisAbout] [Matuschak2023]
- **Finding 4.6: A 2026 position paper argues that generation belongs at authoring time.**
  - **Evidence:** "Runtime adaptation is too late and too costly to be equitable at scale, and might lead to inaccurate learning content due to the inability to conduct verification at scale." The proposal: content as "cards" (bounded units that state a learning objective), with the model producing several representations of each card at design time "to be verified by the instructor before it reaches any learner", which "removed per-learner inference costs". The authors call the learning effect "an empirical question".
  - **Caveats:** Opinion, untested.
  - **Grade:** D.
  - **Sources:** [DesignTimeGenUI2026]

### 5. Head-to-head evidence on fixed content against live generation

- **Finding 5.1: What the student saw in each tutor trial, split by who wrote it.**
  - **Evidence:**
    - **Kestin et al. (Harvard physics, N = 194).** Human-written and fixed: the lesson's problems, the order of problem parts (enforced by the platform), step-by-step answers placed in each prompt, and pre-recorded instructor videos that introduced each activity (stated in the article itself). Generated live: the tutor's replies, held to "a few sentences or less" and "one step at a time". Result: post-test medians 4.5 against 3.5, effect 0.63 SD by linear regression (the authors call it an underestimate because of a ceiling), median 49 minutes against 60. 83% of students rated the explanations as good as or better than a human instructor's. The authors state why they pre-wrote: hallucinations pose "a significant challenge", "thus, we avoided relying solely on GPT-4 to generate solutions".
    - **Bastani et al. (Turkish high school, about 1,000 students).** Human-written: the teacher's lesson, the practice problems, and for the guarded tutor "one or more (correct) solutions to the practice problem, as well as common student mistakes and how to provide feedback". The authors: "This problem-specific construction is labor-intensive, but ensures that GPT-4 does not provide incorrect feedback to the student." Generated live: the chat. Result: the guarded tutor raised practice scores 127% and left the later unassisted exam level. The unguarded chat, with only the problem in its prompt, raised practice 48% and lowered the exam 17%.
    - **Pardos and Bhandari (N = 274, online adults).** Everything the learner saw was fixed before the session. Hints were generated by ChatGPT ahead of time, screened by the second author before the study, and stored. Six raters then rated the same hints to measure the error rate (agreement 0.86 to 0.93): on average 24 of 75 problems (32%) had hints disqualified for wrong work or answer. Ten-way self-consistency cut errors to 0% in elementary algebra, 2% in intermediate and college algebra and 13% in statistics. A check took 37.60 seconds per hint. Learning gains with stored model hints equaled gains with human tutor hints, and only the model-hint condition beat the no-help control.
    - **LearnLM and Eedi 2025 (N = 165).** Human-written: the diagnostic questions, the wrong-answer options tied to misconceptions, and "explanations for both the student and a teacher about the misconception", all given to the model. Generated live: each message, then approved, edited or rewritten by a human tutor before sending. Tutors approved 74.4% of 3,617 drafts unchanged, and a later audit found five factual errors (0.1%). Against the static pre-written hint, live tutoring fixed the mistake on the next try far more often: 93.0% [90.4, 95.3] with the supervised model, 91.2% [88.5, 93.6] with a human, 65.4% [63.8, 66.9] with the hint. On the first question of the next unit: 66.2% after the supervised model, 60.7% after a human tutor.
    - **LearnLM and Eedi 2026 (N = 1,179, 10 schools, 10 weeks).** Same fixed material. 94.7% of 94,385 drafts approved unchanged, mathematical errors in fewer than 3 of 1,000 messages. On in-platform measures and a mid-trial quiz, AI tutoring matched human tutoring and beat the static package. On the external Star Maths test it did not: -0.01 SD [-0.15, +0.14] and +0.02 SD [-0.12, +0.16] against the static package, and human tutoring -0.09 SD [-0.25, +0.07].
  - **Caveats:** None of these trials varied the amount of pre-authoring while holding the rest equal, apart from Bastani's two arms, which also differ in the hint-only instruction. The 2026 trial is a developer report awaiting peer review. Kestin's comparison is a bundle (videos, self-pacing, tutor) against a class.
  - **Grade:** A for each trial, B for reading them as a pattern.
  - **Sources:** [Kestin2025] [KestinSecondary] [KestinPrompt] [Bastani2025] [Pardos2024] [LearnLMEedi2025] [LearnLMEedi2026]
- **Finding 5.2: Across these five trials the split is stable. Humans fixed the task, the answer, the misconceptions and the sequence. The model produced the wording of help, in response to what the student said or, in the hint study, ahead of time.**
  - **Evidence:** See 5.1. In addition, Khan Academy reports the same split from its own review (4.5), and AutoPBL's errors were in the parts it generated at runtime without a stored reference: a question that pointed at the wrong earlier block and a marking judgment that accepted a vague answer (4.3).
  - **Caveats:** This is a reading across studies that were designed for other questions. It describes these five trials only: AutoPBL (4.3) and Learn Your Way (4.1) report gains with model-written questions, and the guarded tutor in Bastani's trial and the 2026 Eedi trial show their gains on near measures only (5.1).
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Kestin2025] [Bastani2025] [LearnLMEedi2025] [KhanMath2025] [AutoPBL2025]

### 6. Model reliability when assembling pages from components

- **Finding 6.1: Single tool calls are a solved problem for current models. Sequences with state are where accuracy falls.**
  - **Evidence:** Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard, ICML 2025 paper. For one model (GPT-4o, native function calling), as I read the results table, whose header came through garbled in my extract: single-turn categories 77.2 to 94.0%, multi-turn base 62.5%, multi-turn with a missing parameter 37.5%, with long context 58.0%, with the needed function missing 6.0%. The best memory score of any model was 12%. The authors: "while state-of-the-art LLMs excel at single-turn calls, memory, dynamic decision making, and long-horizon reasoning remain open challenges."
  - **Caveats:** Late-2024 models. The multi-turn tasks are API workflows such as vehicle control and file systems, which differ from writing a page block by block. Stream 5 reports related results for policy-following agents.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [BFCL2025]
- **Finding 6.2: Accuracy in choosing a tool falls as the tool list grows, slowly at first.**
  - **Evidence:** Tool retrieval benchmark, 500 queries per run: 98.2%, 92.4% and 88.2% at 10, 50 and 100 tools for GPT-4o-mini; 97.8%, 91.9%, 88.6% for Claude 3.5 Haiku; 98.4%, 93.6%, 88.2% for Gemini 2.0 Flash. A long-context function-calling study reports drops of 7% to 85% as the catalog grows, depending on model. A tool-selection study drawing on a registry of more than 4,400 servers found 13.62% selection accuracy when all candidate descriptions went into the prompt and 43.13% when a retriever chose the candidate first.
  - **Caveats:** The first study used small, fast models and capped at 100 tools. The last selected among whole servers, with one base model and 20 trials per method. A list of about 20 tools sits at the easy end of all three.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [HumanMCP2025] [LongFuncEval2025] [RAGMCP2025]
- **Finding 6.3: Constrained decoding makes the shape of a tool call reliable. A bare model drifts on harder schemas.**
  - **Evidence:** JSONSchemaBench, about 10,000 real schemas. Compliance rate (valid outputs among accepted schemas): with the Guidance engine 0.98, 0.96, 0.87 and 0.92 on four suites; with the model alone 0.90, 0.65, 0.38 and 0.56 on the same suites. On the other four suites in the same table Guidance reached 0.94, 1.00, 0.69 and 0.88 against 0.46, 0.40, 0.13 and 0.21 for the model alone, so the full range is 0.69 to 1.00 against 0.13 to 0.90. OpenAI's hosted structured output reached 0.92 to 1.00 compliance (Gemini's 0.88 to 1.00 on the two suites it supports) while accepting a small share of the harder schemas (OpenAI accepted 0.06 to 0.30 of them on seven of the eight suites). On downstream tasks constrained decoding scored about 3% above unconstrained output.
  - **Caveats:** Open-source engines were tested with a 1-billion-parameter model. A valid shape says nothing about whether the content is right (stream 5, finding 15).
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [JSONSchemaBench2025]
- **Finding 6.4: Forcing a format can cost reasoning quality when the answer field is written before the reasoning.**
  - **Evidence:** Tam et al. (EMNLP 2024 industry track): "we observe a significant decline in LLMs' reasoning abilities under format restrictions ... stricter format constraints generally lead to greater performance degradation in reasoning tasks". A two-step method, answer in prose and then convert, kept free-text performance. A rebuttal by a structured-generation vendor, as summarized on a third-party blog, re-ran three tasks with a reasoning-first schema and found structured output level or slightly ahead (0.78 against 0.77, 0.77 against 0.73, 0.44 against 0.41).
  - **Caveats:** The rebuttal comes from a company with a stake, read through a secondary page. Both sides agree on the mechanism: field order.
  - **Grade:** B, contested.
  - **Sources:** [Tam2024] [StructuredRebuttal]
- **Finding 6.5: Free-form interface generation is now robust on the newest models and slow. A structured intermediate plan improves it.**
  - **Evidence:** Google: 100 sampled prompts (8 excluded), two raters each, pairwise. Generated interface preferred over markdown 82.8% and over plain text 97.0%. Expert-built pages preferred over generated ones 50.0%, with the generated page preferred 35.3%. Output errors by backbone model on one prompt set: 0% for the three newest, 29% and 60% for two older ones. The system depends on tools, long system instructions and "a series of post-processors to correct common issues". "One primary limitation ... is the slow generation speed, which can often take a minute or two", and raters saw pre-cached results. Stanford: the model first writes an interaction-flow graph and finite state machines for each component, then the code, then refines. Human raters preferred the result to a chat reply in over 70% of cases.
  - **Caveats:** Both measure preference on general queries with speed removed. Neither measures learning or tests a fixed component library against free markup. My search for that direct comparison returned these two papers and practitioner posts.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [GenUIGoogle2026] [GenUIStanford2025]
- **Finding 6.6: Repeat runs differ.**
  - **Evidence:** Five models, eight tasks, ten runs each, temperature 0, top-p 1, fixed seed: "accuracy variations up to 15% across naturally occurring runs with a gap of best possible performance to worst possible performance up to 70%", and "none of the LLMs consistently delivers repeatable accuracy across all tasks, much less identical output strings". Agreement of the raw text across all ten runs was often far below agreement of the parsed answer. In item writing, "similar prompts frequently generated overlapping content" and large generated sets did not span the content area.
  - **Caveats:** 2024 models and benchmark questions. Stream 1 (finding 22) has the matching result for parallel quiz items: variants meant to be equal often differ in difficulty.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Atil2024] [ItemWriting2025]
- **Finding 6.7: What live generation costs in time and money.**
  - **Evidence:** A routing vendor's production traffic, September to November 2026: median time to first token 1.9 to 7.7 seconds across nine models and 8.9 to 61 seconds at the 95th percentile; 51 to 290 output tokens per second; list prices $0.50 to $50 per million output tokens. Its worked example: a 500-token answer takes roughly 5.3 seconds on one model and 11.1 on another. A benchmark site lists 38 to 350 seconds to the first answer token for ten frontier models in their highest reasoning settings. AutoPBL measured $2 to $3 per learning session. Google's generated pages took a minute or two.
  - **Caveats:** Vendor and aggregator data that I could not check independently. Prices and speeds change monthly. A replayed stored page costs a file read.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [Requesty2026] [ArtificialAnalysis2026] [AutoPBL2025] [GenUIGoogle2026]
- **Finding 6.8: How long people will wait.**
  - **Evidence:** Nielsen's limits: 0.1 second feels instant, 1 second keeps the flow of thought, 10 seconds keeps attention, and beyond that people want a progress display. Nielsen cites Miller (1968) and Card et al. (1991), and in a 1991 forum post said of Miller's paper that it "completely lacked both theoretical and empirical evidence". Spoken virtual agents (delays of 1.5, 4.0 and 6.5 seconds): "latency above 4 seconds degrades quality of experience", engagement fell between 1.5 and 6.5 seconds (p < 0.0001), and natural fillers improved the sense of response time without rescuing the other ratings. Text chat with GPT-4o (N = 240, first token after 2, 9 or 20 seconds): prompting behavior did not change, and people who waited 2 seconds rated the output "less thoughtful and useful" than those who waited 9 or 20. Long waits sometimes turned into frustration. A customer chatbot study found longer latency lowered satisfaction and a typing indicator softened it. A serving-systems paper defines quality of experience as a prompt first token plus a delivery speed that matches reading speed, since faster than that adds nothing.
  - **Caveats:** None of these measured learning, and none used a tutoring task. My search for waiting time against learning outcomes in an LLM tutor returned these general studies.
  - **Grade:** B for the experiments, D for the classic limits.
  - **Sources:** [Nielsen1993] [NielsenUsenet1991] [VRDelay2025] [LatencyCHI2026] [ChatbotLatency2025] [Andes2024]

### 7. Authoring tools and content formats

- **Finding 7.1: Tools that let a non-programmer author by example cut tutor-building time several-fold.**
  - **Evidence:** CTAT example-tracing tutors: "roughly a 1:50 to 1:100 ratio of instructional to development time, compared to earlier estimates of 1:200 to 1:300", described as 4 to 8 times as cost-effective. CTAT-built tutors had been used by about 44,000 students and made up 40% of the datasets in the DataShop repository. The authors reviewed 18 such tutors. ASSISTments Builder: 1:28 to 1:40 by one study, and an abstract states 40 hours per hour of instruction. Nine undergraduates built 121 items at 79 minutes each in their first term and 115 at 55 minutes in their second (p < 0.01), with each item giving about 2 minutes of student work. The timing left out planning, expert review and image making.
  - **Caveats:** The two ratios measure different things, as the CTAT authors note. Both come from the tool builders. Example-tracing covers the solution paths the author demonstrated.
  - **Grade:** B to C.
  - **Sources:** [Aleven2016] [Razzaq2009]
- **Finding 7.2: A parameterized template costs about twice a single item and pays back from the third instance.**
  - **Evidence:** ASSISTments "variabilized" items (a template with variables that yields many parallel instances): 38.8 minutes on average to build the first variabilized item against 20.18 minutes to copy and edit one, then 7.76 minutes per generated instance, a speedup of 2.6. Extending a template to five more instances took about five more minutes.
  - **Caveats:** A student project report with 23 copied items by eight people and 30 variabilized instances by three. Stream 1 (finding 22) shows parallel instances often differ in difficulty.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [WPIVariabilization]
- **Finding 7.3: An AutoTutor lesson is a human-written script with a fixed shape, and the dialogue engine improvises only within it.**
  - **Evidence:** Each topic has a main question, "2-10 expectations (good responses) and 0 to 5 misconceptions (responses that require remediation)", plus hints, prompts, corrections and a summary. The engine walks a fixed ladder per expectation: "pump, then a hint, then a prompt, and then simply asserting the answer", and stops when the expectation is covered. Learning gains averaged "about 0.8 sigma" above reading static material for the same time in the developers' reviews, and a later paper gives a range of 0.3 to 0.8. The same review cites "approximately 100 or more hours of authoring time for a single hour of instruction" for tutoring systems in general and presents the script authoring tool as the answer for authors with little technical skill.
  - **Caveats:** Developer reviews. Stream 2 places AutoTutor inside the wider tutoring-system literature, where effects on standardized tests are much smaller.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Nye2014] [AutoTutorConversations]
- **Finding 7.4: The US Army's GIFT framework moved from hand-edited XML to a visual editor so subject experts could author alone, and assessment logic stayed the hard part.**
  - **Evidence:** The tools "evolved the task of authoring a tutor from the direct manipulation of extensible markup language (XML) code to an object oriented visual interface". After a 2017 redesign, user feedback at Fort Benning showed "there was still much work to be done", and the team built a two-day training course, videos and example tutors. "The most challenging area of authoring remains to be the authoring of the assessment logic which occurs within simulations." On automation of authoring: "most ITS authoring tasks be automated no higher than level 5. This is the point at which the human still approves all decisions and/or suggestions made by the system."
  - **Caveats:** Program papers. No authoring-time measurements were in what I read.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [GIFT2018] [SottilareComplexity] [Ososky2017] [GIFTArchitecture2018]
- **Finding 7.5: What the interchange formats have in common.**
  - **Evidence:**
    - **QTI 3 (1EdTech).** XML. One file per item. The item holds "the assessment content ... correct and incorrect answers, scoring and response processing information, and other metadata". Validated against a published schema, with a certification process. Presentation uses HTML5 and CSS inside the item.
    - **H5P.** A zip with a `content.json` for one piece of content and the libraries that render it. Each content type declares its allowed fields in a `semantics.json`. The core validates and sanitizes content on save.
    - **Open edX XBlock.** A component API in the platform, also used as the "adapter layer" for outside formats such as H5P and LTI.
    - **Common Cartridge (1EdTech).** A package for a whole course or textbook, with "each discrete learning object" tagged with metadata.
    - **xAPI (IEEE 9274.1.1-2023).** A JSON statement of actor, verb and object, with optional result and context, sent to a record store. The store checks syntax only.
    - **OLI Torus.** Resources of five types: container, page, activity, objective, tag. "Activity instances are not directly embedded into pages, rather a reference to an instance is stored within pages", so one activity can serve several pages, and an "activity bank" lets the system pick a different activity per attempt by criteria. Each activity part holds its scoring rules and feedback. A course exports as a zip of JSON files with a hierarchy file.
  - **Caveats:** Specifications and documentation. They show what large systems converged on.
  - **Grade:** C.
  - **Sources:** [QTI3] [QTI3Impl] [H5P] [H5PTech] [XBlockWiki] [CommonCartridge] [xAPI] [Torus] [TorusIngest]
- **Finding 7.6: The text-first formats give the same reasons for plain text: any editor, version control, forks, long life. They differ on markup weight.**
  - **Evidence:** PreTeXt: semantic XML, "you explicitly specify the logical parts of your document and not how these parts should be displayed", one source to HTML, PDF, EPUB and Braille, and "revision control ... works well with PreTeXt since the source is just text files". Its guide admits "authoring in xml might seem cumbersome at first, since some content will require more characters of markup than of content". Runestone, the interactive computer science ebook platform, announced in April 2026 "the last release of Runestone that will support RST as an authoring language", six years after partnering with PreTeXt, so that its developer can "focus on the interactive features and not worry about the authoring language". LiaScript: a Markdown dialect with quizzes, runnable code and spoken text, built so that "even without the LiaScript interpreter the content is still readable and interpretable with every editor", with courses kept in git where "with branches and forks you can evolve multiple variants for different languages, contexts, and target groups". Its stated position: "a language-based approach, instead of a tooling-centered one, provides more flexibility, freedom of creativity, and sustainability".
  - **Caveats:** Project documentation and one blog post. The evidence for format choice is the makers' rationale. My search for a controlled comparison of authoring speed or error rate between markdown, XML and JSON returned tool documentation only. MyST and Quarto appeared in search summaries and I did not open their pages.
  - **Grade:** C to D.
  - **Sources:** [PreTeXt] [PreTeXtGuide] [Runestone2026] [LiaScript] [LiaScriptDocs]
- **Finding 7.7: The reusability paradox.**
  - **Evidence:** Wiley and colleagues (2004): "The less specific the internal context of the learning object, the more instructional contexts into which it will 'fit.' Conversely, the more specific the internal context of the object, the fewer instructional contexts into which it will 'fit.'" Their working grain size came from asking a subject expert "can you ever imagine teaching one of these topics without teaching the others?" until the answer was no. Wiley's later summary, quoted by The Carpentries: "the pedagogical effectiveness of a learning object and its potential for reuse are completely at odds with one another", striking a balance "generally results in materials that are neither particularly effective NOR particularly reusable", and his way out is an open license so that others may revise a deeply contextual resource for their own setting.
  - **Caveats:** An argument, with the grain-size rule drawn from one project. A critic (Downes) disputes the notion of "internal context".
  - **Grade:** D.
  - **Sources:** [Wiley2004] [Carpentries2015] [OERu] [Downes]

### 8. Page design evidence

- **Finding 8.1: Signals that show structure and importance help.**
  - **Evidence:** Schneider et al. (2018): 103 studies, 12,201 participants, 139 retention and 70 transfer measures. Retention g+ = 0.53 [0.42, 0.64], transfer g+ = 0.33 [0.22, 0.43]. Signaling also lowered cognitive load and raised motivation and relevant fixations. Prior knowledge did not moderate the effect. The handbook chapter lists what counts as a signal: introductory paragraphs, headings, numbering, pointer words, highlighting, color coding and graphic organizers, and notes that some studies find no effect.
  - **Caveats:** Mostly short lab lessons with immediate tests.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Schneider2018] [Fiorella2022]
- **Finding 8.2: Cutting a lesson into segments helps, costs time, and helps knowledgeable learners at least as much.**
  - **Evidence:** Rey et al. (2019): 56 investigations, 88 comparisons, N = 7,713. Retention d = 0.32, transfer d = 0.36, lower overall cognitive load, longer learning time. Learners with high prior knowledge gained more on retention than those with low or none. In Table 3 of the full text, system-paced segments gave 0.42 and 0.35, and learner-paced segments 0.19 (not significant) and 0.45.
  - **Caveats:** Multimedia lessons, near transfer, immediate tests.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Rey2019]
- **Finding 8.3: Related text and graphics belong next to each other.**
  - **Evidence:** Schroeder and Cenkci (2018): 58 comparisons, n = 2,426, g = 0.63 for integrated against separated layouts. By a clearinghouse summary: computer-based 0.50 (20 effects), worksheets 0.80 (28), static graphics 0.68 (48), interactive graphics 0.24 (5), and 0.74 when text and picture do not duplicate each other.
  - **Caveats:** Moderator values from a secondary page.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [SchroederCenkci2018] [TUMClearinghouse2019]
- **Finding 8.4: Interesting but irrelevant additions lower learning, twice as much for novices.**
  - **Evidence:** Sundararajan and Adesope (2020), 68 comparisons: g = -0.33, range -0.48 to -0.18; -0.52 for learners without prior knowledge against -0.24 with it. Larger on open-ended than multiple-choice tests. The first author notes most studies were lab lessons of "about 6 to 10 minutes" and "strongly recommend[s] excluding them from learning materials".
  - **Caveats:** Short lessons. A summary page lists conditions where the effect weakened: warned learners and strong prior knowledge.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Sundararajan2020] [SundarAFT2020]
- **Finding 8.5: Advance organizers and stated key ideas have small, old support.**
  - **Evidence:** Luiten, Ames and Ackerson (1980), 135 studies: organizers helped learning and retention. A secondary review gives their mean effects as 0.21 for learning and 0.26 for retention, larger for high-ability learners (0.23) than low (0.13). Stone (1983), 112 studies from 29 reports: positive, with a retention effect of 0.66 by a secondary account and no extra benefit for low-ability learners. Hattie's synthesis gives 0.41 by a secondary page. Headings, summaries and overviews are counted as signals in 8.1.
  - **Caveats:** Unweighted 1980s meta-analyses seen as abstracts and through secondary pages. My search for a modern pooled estimate for summaries or key-idea callouts as such returned the signaling meta-analysis.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Luiten1980] [Stone1983] [AdvOrgSecondary]
- **Finding 8.6: Color that links matching elements helps. Evidence for color as a category label is indirect.**
  - **Evidence:** Ozcelik et al. (2009), 52 undergraduates, random assignment: terms shown in the same color in text and diagram raised retention (82.42 against 69.78, eta squared .08) and transfer (63.85 against 47.88, eta squared .17), and eye tracking showed shorter search for matching terms. Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller (1999): color coding lowered rated load and raised multiple-choice scores (F(1,13) = 7.01) for a diagram with text. Color coding is one of the signal types in 8.1.
  - **Caveats:** Both studies use color to tie a word to a picture element. A scheme where box color marks purpose (information, personal, progress, quiz) is a signaling device by Mayer's definition, and I found it tested only as part of signaling in general. The 1999 experiment was small (its test statistic has 13 error degrees of freedom).
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Ozcelik2009] [Kalyuga1999] [Fiorella2022]
- **Finding 8.7: Reading from screens trails paper a little, more under time pressure and for expository text, and long scrolling pages are the suspect.**
  - **Evidence:** Delgado et al. (2018): 54 studies, 171,055 participants, Hedges' g = -0.21 [-0.28, -0.14] between participants and -0.21 within. The gap was larger under time limits, present for informational text and absent for narrative, and grew over the years 2000 to 2017. Where scrolling was needed g = -0.25 (p < .001), where it was not g = -0.13 (p = .06), and the moderator test was not significant. Sanchez and Wiley (2009): 40 students read a 2,700-word text on ice ages in 13 sections, as one scrolling page or as 13 pages with Next and Back. The paged version gave better essays (beta = .52, p < .01), and working memory capacity interacted with format (R squared change .07), with low-capacity readers hurt most by scrolling. A second experiment (37 students, a different text) found the same interaction with working memory and no overall effect of format.
  - **Caveats:** The scrolling contrast in the meta-analysis is suggestive only. The experiment is small and from 2009.
  - **Grade:** A for the screen effect, B for scrolling.
  - **Sources:** [Delgado2018] [SanchezWiley2009]
- **Finding 8.8: Progress displays work through how fast progress seems, and nearness to a goal speeds people up.**
  - **Evidence:** Villar, Callegaro and Yang (2013), 32 randomized web-survey experiments: a constant-speed progress bar "does not significantly help reduce drop-offs", fast-to-slow bars reduced them and slow-to-fast bars increased them, and with a small promised incentive a constant bar increased drop-off. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006), 949 completed coffee cards: time between purchases shortened by 20% from first to last stamp, which made a card about 16% quicker to complete than at the starting rate. The same paper tests "illusionary goal progress" (a longer card with bonus stamps as a head start), and the abstract reports that customers given a 12-stamp card with 2 bonus stamps completed the 10 purchases faster than customers given a plain 10-stamp card. Nunes and Dreze (2006), 300 car-wash cards: 34% redemption for a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps given against 19% for an 8-stamp card, the same real distance.
  - **Caveats:** Surveys and loyalty programs. The survey authors warn against progress displays that misstate real progress. My search for a controlled test of progress boxes on lesson completion returned these.
  - **Grade:** A for surveys, B for the application to lessons.
  - **Sources:** [Villar2013] [Kivetz2006] [NunesDreze2006]
- **Finding 8.9: Two panes compete for attention.**
  - **Evidence:** Kueker et al. (2024), 42 novices learning software from a video: two monitors side by side gave significantly higher pupil-measured load than toggling on one monitor, during instruction and testing. A randomized eye-tracking study (77 with a chatbot beside three algebra videos, 74 without): more gaze on the lesson area overall with the chatbot (beta = 2.16, p = .015), and a faster decline over time (interaction beta = -9.92, p < .001). The lesson area was the videos for the control group and the videos plus the chatbot for the chatbot group, and the abstract says the control group was the more attentive one at the initial stage. AI margin notes: 65% of participants ranked notes anchored in the document first and a separate side chat second, citing less switching, with no difference in comprehension. One virtual-classroom eye-tracking study concluded that a chat box and agents "interfere with students' learning performance and visual attendance". On the other side, 29 of 30 students liked having the book beside the chat because they could check the answer against it.
  - **Caveats:** None tests a page that the tutor itself writes while talking. The first study splits attention between a video and an application. The second counts gaze on the chatbot as gaze on the lesson, so it cannot show the chat drawing attention away from the lesson.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Kueker2024] [ConvAIEye2024] [MarginNotes2025] [VLEEyeTracking] [MuDoC2025]
- **Finding 8.10: An on-screen agent adds a little, text agents more than narrated ones, and a conversational voice helps in short lessons.**
  - **Evidence:** Schroeder, Adesope and Gilbert (2013): 43 comparisons, 3,088 participants, g = 0.19 [0.12, 0.27]; agents that communicated by on-screen text g = 0.51 against narration g = 0.12; larger for school pupils than college students. Castro-Alonso et al. (2021): 32 effects, N = 2,104, g+ = 0.20 [0.07, 0.34]; 2D agents 0.38 against 3D 0.11; voice, motion and gesture did not moderate. Ginns, Martin and Marsh (2013): 74 effects, 3,312 students; conversational style gave d = 0.30 on retention [.18, .41] and 0.54 on transfer; friendliness 0.46; interest 0.15 (not reliable); effects in studies longer than 35 minutes were small and not significant.
  - **Caveats:** The text-agent subgroup held 12% of participants. Conversational-style studies are mostly short.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Schroeder2013] [CastroAlonso2021] [Ginns2013]

### 9. Personalizing content to the student

- **Finding 9.1: Problems set in a learner's own interests raised performance and later learning in an algebra tutor, using variants written in advance.**
  - **Evidence:** Walkington (2013): 145 ninth graders (73 control, 72 personalized) in one unit of Cognitive Tutor Algebra. On the hardest skill (writing expressions) the odds of a correct answer were 1.53 times higher (53.66% against 43.2%, p < .001). Four units later, with the treatment gone, the personalized group was still ahead (odds 1.30, p = .0097). The gain was largest for struggling students. Bernacki and Walkington (2018): 150 students (51 control, 99 personalized) over four units; higher triggered interest, which predicted accuracy and efficiency in the tutor, and a total effect on classroom exam scores and interest in mathematics. The personalized problems were "written based on interests past students had discussed" and matched to "one of 10 discrete, semi-broad interest categories" from a survey: same mathematical structure, different story.
  - **Caveats:** One school each. Top and bottom performers fell outside the first sample. Personalization here is the cover story of a word problem.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Walkington2013] [BernackiWalkington2018]
- **Finding 9.2: Pooled, interest personalization has medium effects, strongest on interest itself and when the match is fine-grained.**
  - **Evidence:** Lin et al. (2024), 34 publications: interest g = 0.55 (26 effects, n = 5,335), cognitive load g = 0.54 (8 effects), retention g = 0.48 (46 effects, n = 5,991), transfer g = 0.36 (6 effects, n = 375). The interest effect varied with how interests were diagnosed, grain size and domain. A 2026 paper citing it reports g = 0.89 on interest for the finest grain (the individual student).
  - **Caveats:** Six transfer effects. Abstract only.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Lin2024] [TeacherLoop2026]
- **Finding 9.3: Results outside the tutor studies are mixed, and live LLM personalization has thin evidence.**
  - **Evidence:** Dissertation, 343 rising eighth graders and about 6,000 word problems, within subjects: personalized problems were rated more interesting, and after controlling for interest rating they were answered no more accurately. Walkington et al. (2024), 458 students: a personalized problem-posing activity raised algebra learning when dosage was controlled, and for secondary students posing pop-culture problems lowered enjoyment of mathematics. Generative AI in German primary classrooms (N = 114, 60 minutes, GPT-4 placing word problems in each child's interest in real time): higher motivation, interest and performance. LLM tutor with style personalization from a learning-styles questionnaire (N = 40): equal quiz accuracy, lower pupil-measured load. In the 2026 Eedi trial, adding the student's learning history to the tutor's context gave +0.02 SD against -0.01 SD without it, both indistinguishable from the static package.
  - **Caveats:** Different meanings of "personalized": story context, communication style, learning history. Stream 2 lists matching to learning styles as a myth.
  - **Grade:** A to B, mixed.
  - **Sources:** [TPWP] [Walkington2024] [GAIContext2025] [Karerwa2026] [LearnLMEedi2026]
- **Finding 9.4: Irrelevant interest hurts, relevant interest helps, and the line between them is whether the personal detail is the vehicle for the idea.**
  - **Evidence:** Seductive details g = -0.33 (8.4) against interest personalization g = 0.48 on retention (9.2). In Walkington's design the interest story was the problem to be solved. In Learn Your Way, personalization replaced the text's own examples with ones from the learner's interest and marked them (4.1).
  - **Caveats:** This contrast is drawn across two literatures that do not test each other.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Sundararajan2020] [Lin2024] [Walkington2013] [LearnYourWay2025]

### 10. Scripted against improvised teaching in general

- **Finding 10.1: Programs that hand the teacher a structured lesson raise learning.**
  - **Evidence:** Snilstveit et al. (2016), 3ie systematic review of 238 studies in low- and middle-income countries: structured pedagogy (new content, materials, lesson plans and short training, 21 programs) had "the largest and most consistent positive average effects": language 0.23 SD [0.13, 0.34], mathematics 0.14 SD [0.08, 0.20]. Stockard et al. (2018): Direct Instruction across 328 studies, 413 designs and almost 4,000 effects, all positive, pooled estimate 0.54 (95% CI 0.49 to 0.59, Table 1 of the article). Its scripts are built from a logical analysis of the concept, tried in-house, field-tested in schools, revised, and field-tested again before publication. Teachers are told to follow the script and to bring "their own style and personality" to it.
  - **Caveats:** Children learning basic skills. Stream 1 records the dispute over Direct Instruction study quality.
  - **Grade:** A.
  - **Sources:** [Snilstveit2016] [Stockard2018]
- **Finding 10.2: Beyond structure, more scripting did not add learning.**
  - **Evidence:** Piper et al. (2018), 19 projects in 13 countries: programs with teacher guides gained 6.1 correct words per minute in oral reading. A scripting index explained 12% of impact, with a negative slope: "For every additional 10 scripting index points, program impact decreased by 1.4 correct words per minute." Guides that were fully scripted at the start and then tapered to structured plans did best. In classrooms teachers changed the lessons, often cutting the group work the guide described. The authors: "structured teachers' guides improve learning outcomes, but ... overly scripted teachers' guides are somewhat less effective than simplified teachers' guides that give specific guidance to the teacher but are not written word for word", and "we are not claiming that the relationship is causal".
  - **Caveats:** Cross-program regression by the implementing organization, not always significant, with newer and better-designed programs also being less scripted.
  - **Grade:** B.
  - **Sources:** [Piper2018]
- **Finding 10.3: In tutoring, programs run by paraprofessionals gave large and consistent results, and the meta-analysis could not isolate the role of structure.**
  - **Evidence:** Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan: pooled 0.288 SD in the published version and 0.37 in the working paper; teacher tutors 0.50 (17 studies), paraprofessionals 0.40 (47), nonprofessionals 0.21 (24), parents 0.23 (11), with study counts as the J-PAL review gives them (the working paper's table shows 18 teacher and 46 paraprofessional studies). In the J-PAL review's words, paraprofessional effects were "substantially more consistent than those for teacher-led programs". On structure: programs "like Reading Recovery and Number Rockets consist of highly structured lessons with clear and detailed directives to the tutors" (Number Rockets is "about 45 scripted lessons"), and "pedagogical approach and level of structure were not possible to reliably code across the full range of studies".
  - **Caveats:** The role of structure is the authors' qualitative reading.
  - **Grade:** A for the tutor-type effects, D for structure.
  - **Sources:** [Nickow2024] [Nickow2020] [JPALTutoring]
- **Finding 10.4: Scripted tutoring software exists at both ends: AutoTutor's expectation and misconception scripts, and Synthesis Tutor's linear scripts.**
  - **Evidence:** See 7.3 and 4.5. The observer of the Synthesis demo compared its branching to 1970s computer-assisted instruction and noted "an all-or-nothing gate at the end, without the fine-grained modeling that's the hallmark of ITS".
  - **Caveats:** One observer, one demo lesson, 2023.
  - **Grade:** C to D.
  - **Sources:** [Nye2014] [Matuschak2023] [SynthesisAbout]

## Evidence lined up against options A to D

This section sorts the findings above under each option and criterion. It describes what the evidence shows and leaves the choice to the report writer. Where the evidence for a cell is indirect, the entry says so.

### Option A - fully pre-authored textbook (the plan holds every page; the model edits or inserts)

- **Burden on the model:** Lowest of the four. The model's job shrinks to chat replies and occasional inserts, the split used in the tutor trials of finding 5.1 [Kestin2025] [Bastani2025] [LearnLMEedi2025]. Single tool calls are the reliable regime, 77 to 94% on one benchmark model [BFCL2025].
- **Adaptivity to the student:** The page is the same for everyone unless variants are stored. Adaptive hypermedia got its content adaptation from stored fragments shown or hidden per reader, and that improved comprehension in MetaDoc [DeBra1999]. Stored interest variants picked per student raised algebra performance (53.66% against 43.2%) [Walkington2013]. Static remediation trailed live tutoring on the immediate retry (65.4% against 91 to 93%) and matched it on a standardized test after 10 weeks [LearnLMEedi2025] [LearnLMEedi2026]. Pre-generating "means that all possible scenarios ... need to be anticipated in advance, which is extremely hard to achieve" [MWPTutor2024].
- **Ease of human editing:** Everything the student will read is on file to review, as in OLI, zyBooks and Direct Instruction, whose scripts are field-tested and revised before release [Bier2014] [Stockard2018]. The volume to write and maintain is the largest: OLI courses cost about $500,000 to $1,000,000 each [Rhoads2013], and tutor content ran 28 to 100 hours per instructional hour even with authoring tools [Aleven2016] [Razzaq2009].
- **Consistency and quality control:** Highest. "No 'new' errors or hallucinations since the LLM is not used after the design stage" [MWPTutor2024]. Checked, stored model hints taught as well as human hints [Pardos2024]. Every student sees the same items, so item statistics accumulate and weak items can be pulled, as VitalSource does [VanCampenhout2025].
- **Safety of the answer key:** Keys and feedback are authored and reviewed with the item, the arrangement every item format assumes [QTI3] [Torus]. The model keyed the wrong option in 15% of items in one study, and teachers judged 30% of stem, key and explanation sets invalid in another [Thoracic2025] [HEDGE2024], and review before release catches those.
- **Cost and latency:** A stored page replays without a model call. Generation cost moves to authoring time, where the Duolingo pattern (generate ten, keep three) applies [DuolingoLLM]. Chat replies still incur the live latency in finding 6.7.
- **Strength of the evidence:** Strongest for learning outcomes. Two trials of pre-authored courseware against lecture courses [Lovett2008] [Bowen2014], randomized lesson studies of interactive text [Edgcomb2014], the adjunct-question literature [Hamaker1986], and structured pedagogy and Direct Instruction meta-analyses [Snilstveit2016] [Stockard2018]. Against it: fully word-for-word scripting was associated with smaller gains than structured guidance [Piper2018], and readers given fixed text with fixed links mostly pressed Continue [Brusilovsky2003].

### Option B - building blocks (the plan holds milestone content and a block library; the model assembles each page)

- **Burden on the model:** The model selects and orders blocks. Selection accuracy from a list was about 98% at 10 options and 88% at 100 [HumanMCP2025]. Ordering over many steps is the weaker regime (62.5% on basic multi-turn tasks) [BFCL2025]. Kestin's team found a prompt alone "could not reliably provide enough structure" to keep parts in order and moved sequencing into the platform [Kestin2025].
- **Adaptivity to the student:** This is the classic adaptive hypermedia design: authored fragments, with the system choosing which to show. It worked for content (stretchtext) and for guiding novices with a single next step, and annotation of free choices helped only experienced learners [DeBra1999] [Brusilovsky2003] [WeberBrusilovsky2001]. OLI Torus selects from an activity bank per attempt by criteria [Torus]. Duolingo selects stored exercises per learner with a separate model [DuolingoLLM].
- **Ease of human editing:** Blocks can be reviewed one by one, and a block bank suits per-item statistics. The reusability paradox applies with full force: blocks written to fit any page hold less context and teach less in each place, and "striking a balance" tends to give material "neither particularly effective NOR particularly reusable" [Wiley2004] [Carpentries2015]. Wiley's working grain was the set of topics an expert could not imagine teaching apart [Wiley2004]. Parameterized templates pay back from the third instance [WPIVariabilization].
- **Consistency and quality control:** Each block is checked, and the assembled page is new each time. The evidence on coherence of assembled pages is thin. Wiley warns that "simple concatenation or sequencing of decontextualized educational resources will likely fail to produce a meaningful context for learning" [Wiley2004]. Run-to-run variation applies to the assembly decisions [Atil2024].
- **Safety of the answer key:** Same as A for stored quiz blocks, since each item holds its own key and scoring rule [QTI3] [Torus].
- **Cost and latency:** One short model call per assembly step and no long generation. Template-based item generation gives many instances per authored template [SayinGierl2024].
- **Strength of the evidence:** Moderate and old for the selection idea (small 1990s studies, grade B). Selection from stored items is widespread in practice (ASSISTments, OLI Torus activity banks, Duolingo), which is grade C evidence. Evidence on expository pages assembled from blocks is thin. The adaptive textbook history calls the fragment-tagging approach "hard to scale" [Brusilovsky2022].

### Option C - fully generative (the model writes every page from the plan's goals at runtime)

- **Burden on the model:** Highest. The model writes exposition, examples, quiz items, keys and feedback, and keeps a long tool sequence coherent. Reliability falls with sequence length and missing information [BFCL2025], and repeat runs differ [Atil2024]. Free-form interface generation needed long instructions, tools and post-processors to become robust, and older models failed 29 to 60% of the time [GenUIGoogle2026].
- **Adaptivity to the student:** Highest in principle: every block can reflect what the student just did. Measured benefits of that freedom are limited: a generated explanation equaled textbook examples on a test (p = .750) while raising interest [PhysicsAI2025]; live interest personalization helped in one primary-school trial [GAIContext2025]; adding learning history to a tutor's context changed a standardized outcome by +0.02 SD against -0.01 SD [LearnLMEedi2026].
- **Ease of human editing:** The expert edits goals and prompts and sees pages only after students do. Deployed products avoid this for lesson content: Duolingo's experts "always have the final say", Khan Academy routes the tutor through human-written hints, VitalSource declined LLM generation where "human oversight [was] unfeasible at scale" [DuolingoLLM] [KhanMath2025] [VanCampenhout2025]. A 2026 position paper argues runtime generation "might lead to inaccurate learning content due to the inability to conduct verification at scale" [DesignTimeGenUI2026].
- **Consistency and quality control:** Lowest. Unchecked error rates in the sources: 5.2% of runtime tutorial blocks [AutoPBL2025], 32% of raw math hints [Pardos2024], 40% of exam questions with a major error [Thoracic2025]. Parallel items written on the fly differ in difficulty (stream 1). No two students see the same item, so item statistics cannot accumulate.
- **Safety of the answer key:** The model writes the key at runtime, so a wrong key reaches the student unreviewed. Measured rates: 15% wrong key and 15% two correct options in 20 medical items [Thoracic2025]; 30% of stem, key and explanation sets judged invalid by teachers [HEDGE2024]; a programming study found GPT-4 items with a single correct choice in general [Doughty2024]. AutoPBL's runtime marking accepted over-general answers [AutoPBL2025].
- **Cost and latency:** Every page pays generation time and tokens: seconds to first token, 51 to 290 tokens per second, $2 to $3 per session in one system, and a minute or two for a rich generated interface [Requesty2026] [AutoPBL2025] [GenUIGoogle2026]. Waits above 4 to 10 seconds lower experience ratings or need a progress display [VRDelay2025] [Nielsen1993], while one study found short waits of 9 to 20 seconds were read as thoughtfulness [LatencyCHI2026].
- **Strength of the evidence:** Weakest. The one trial of a largely generated textbook experience started from a human-written chapter, used human-written tests, and confounded generation with quizzing [LearnYourWay2025] [LearnYourWay2026]. A trial in which a model writes lessons from goals alone and is compared with authored material on learning is still to be run. The nearest are [LearnYourWay2025] and [AutoPBL2025].

### Option D - hybrid skeleton (the plan fixes the block sequence per milestone and authors the stable parts; the tutor fills the rest and may insert or replace within rules)

- **Burden on the model:** Middle. The sequence and the checks are fixed, and the model writes the connecting exposition and responds to events. This is the division the tutor trials of finding 5.1 used: platform-held sequence, human-held answers, model-written help [Kestin2025] [Bastani2025] [LearnLMEedi2025]. Generating a structured plan before content improved generated interfaces [GenUIStanford2025].
- **Adaptivity to the student:** The fixed parts are the same for all, the filled parts respond to the student. AutoPBL, with a fixed outline and runtime blocks, beat a static tutorial plus chatbot on quiz scores (p = 0.02 and 0.03) [AutoPBL2025]. Tutors with a fixed script shape and live moves within it (AutoTutor) gained about 0.3 to 0.8 sigma over reading [Nye2014] [AutoTutorConversations]. Teacher guides that fixed structure and loosened wording did better than fully scripted ones [Piper2018].
- **Ease of human editing:** The expert reviews the skeleton, key ideas, quiz items and keys, a smaller volume than A. The shape matches an AutoTutor script (main question, 2 to 10 expectations, 0 to 5 misconceptions, hint ladder) and an OLI unit (objective, exposition, interspersed activities with hints and misconception feedback) [Nye2014] [StraderThille2012]. The generated filler is seen by the expert only through run records.
- **Consistency and quality control:** The assessed parts are constant across students and can gather statistics. The generated parts vary and hold the error rates listed under C at a lower exposure. AutoPBL's 5.2% block error rate arose in a design of this kind where the outline too was machine-made [AutoPBL2025].
- **Safety of the answer key:** Authored and reviewed keys stay with their items, as in A. Inserted or replaced quiz blocks written at runtime would bring back the risks listed under C for those blocks.
- **Cost and latency:** Stored blocks replay at once and filled blocks pay generation. AutoPBL's $2 to $3 and its authors' wish to cut "waiting time" are the nearest measurement [AutoPBL2025].
- **Strength of the evidence:** Moderate and indirect. One small controlled study of a like design [AutoPBL2025], the structure of the five tutor trials (finding 5.2), the scripted-but-not-word-for-word result [Piper2018], and expert opinion that the best design "might be a combination of both" [MWPTutor2024]. A trial of D against A on the same lesson is still to be run.

### What the evidence says about each kind of block

- **Quiz items, options, keys and option feedback.** Evidence favors writing and reviewing these ahead of time: wrong keys and weak distractors are the documented failure of model-written items [Thoracic2025] [HEDGE2024] [ItemWriting2025]; distractors built from real student errors are where experts add most (stream 5, finding 21); every item format stores key and scoring with the item [QTI3] [Torus]; and placement inside the text after the relevant passage is supported [Rubio2022] [Hamaker1986]. Model drafts that are checked before release performed like human items [Pardos2024] [VanCampenhout2021].
- **Worked solutions, reference answers and misconception lists.** Human-written in the three trials cited here and named by Khan Academy as what makes its tutor accurate [Kestin2025] [Bastani2025] [LearnLMEedi2025] [KhanMath2025].
- **The sequence of blocks and milestones.** Held by the platform or the author in the trials and in Direct Instruction, OLI, AutoTutor and AutoPBL [Kestin2025] [Stockard2018] [Bier2014] [Nye2014] [AutoPBL2025]. Novices did best with one clear next step [WeberBrusilovsky2001] [Brusilovsky2003].
- **Key ideas, summaries, headings and color roles.** These are signals (g = 0.53 retention) and work by being consistent and relevant [Schneider2018] [Ozcelik2009]. A test of authored against generated signals is still to be run.
- **Core exposition.** Human-written short text with embedded activity has trial support [Edgcomb2014] [Edgcomb2015] [Lovett2008]. A model-written explanation equaled textbook examples on a test in one trial [PhysicsAI2025], and re-leveled, interest-adapted text over a human source was rated accurate by experts [LearnYourWay2025]. Additions that are interesting and off-topic cost learning, g = -0.33 [Sundararajan2020].
- **Hints and second explanations.** The largest measured advantage of live generation: fixing a mistake on the next try (93.0% against 65.4% for a stored hint) [LearnLMEedi2025]. Stored model hints also worked [Pardos2024]. The long-run advantage over stored hints was about zero in one trial [LearnLMEedi2026].
- **"Tailored for you" notes.** Interest context helps (g = 0.48 retention, 0.36 transfer), most at the grain of the individual, and when it is the vehicle for the idea [Lin2024] [Walkington2013]. The classic studies picked from about 10 pre-written interest variants [BernackiWalkington2018]. Live generation of such context has one positive classroom trial [GAIContext2025].
- **Progress, milestone, success and Continue blocks.** Driven by rules in every system reviewed. The evidence that exists is about their effect on persistence, and it comes from surveys and loyalty cards [Villar2013] [NunesDreze2006].
- **Chat replies.** Generated live in each tutor trial that had a chat, with the fixed material in context [Kestin2025] [Bastani2025] [LearnLMEedi2026].

### What the evidence says about a hand-editable plan format

- **Structure is validated everywhere.** QTI has an XML schema and certification, H5P a semantics file per content type, GIFT began as XML, and Torus ingests typed JSON [QTI3Impl] [H5P] [SottilareComplexity] [TorusIngest].
- **Humans who write a lot of course text choose text-first formats and git.** PreTeXt, LiaScript and the Runestone books give any-editor access, diffs, forks and long life as reasons [PreTeXtGuide] [LiaScriptDocs] [Runestone2026].
- **Markup weight is the named cost.** PreTeXt's guide concedes XML can need more markup than content, LiaScript's makers chose Markdown so the source reads without tooling, and GIFT moved authors off raw XML to a visual editor [PreTeXtGuide] [LiaScriptDocs] [SottilareComplexity].
- **Items live apart from pages and are referenced.** Torus stores activities separately and pages hold references, which allows banks and reuse [Torus]. QTI keeps one item per file [QTI3Impl].
- **The lesson unit has a recurring shape.** Objective, skills, exposition, interspersed activity with hints and misconception feedback (OLI); main question, expectations, misconceptions, hint ladder (AutoTutor) [StraderThille2012] [Bier2014] [Nye2014].
- **Authoring effort concentrates in assessment logic and misconceptions** [GIFTArchitecture2018] [HEDGE2024].
- **The gap.** The evidence here is what mature projects did and why they say they did it. A measured comparison of formats on authoring time or error rate did not turn up.

## Disagreements and open questions

- **Does live generation add learning beyond good fixed content?** On the next attempt, yes: 93.0% against 65.4% [LearnLMEedi2025]. On a standardized test after 10 weeks, the difference was -0.01 to +0.02 SD [LearnLMEedi2026]. The 2026 authors offer tentative explanations and treat the gap between near and broad measures as unresolved. Stream 5 has the same pattern for LLM tutors in general.
- **Was the Learn Your Way gain caused by generation?** The developers report a 9 to 11 point advantage [LearnYourWay2025]. The control was a PDF with no questions, every treatment student took quizzes, and retrieval practice alone gives effects of that size (stream 2, finding 1). The authors list the missing component analysis as a limitation. A generated explanation with no added quizzing equaled the textbook [PhysicsAI2025].
- **Does forcing structured output hurt content quality?** Tam et al. say stricter formats degrade reasoning [Tam2024]. A vendor rebuttal found parity with a reasoning-first schema [StructuredRebuttal]. JSONSchemaBench found constrained decoding about 3% better on downstream tasks [JSONSchemaBench2025]. The shared ground is that field order matters.
- **Is a short wait bad?** Classic guidance says keep responses under 1 second for flow and under 10 for attention [Nielsen1993], on a base Nielsen called unevidenced [NielsenUsenet1991]. A spoken-agent study found harm above 4 seconds [VRDelay2025]. A text study found 9 to 20 second waits raised perceived thoughtfulness against 2 seconds [LatencyCHI2026]. None measured learning.
- **Do adaptive links and adaptive order help?** Positive for experienced learners, null for novices in two InterBook experiments, positive for novices given direct guidance [Brusilovsky2003] [WeberBrusilovsky2001] [DirectGuidance2009]. The studies are small and from 1997 to 2009.
- **Where should embedded questions sit?** After the relevant passage [Rubio2022], anywhere as long as they are answered, ideally twice [UnerRoediger2018], with elaborative feedback at the end [FeedbackPlacement2024]. Hamaker found no position effect for unrelated material and excluded feedback studies [Hamaker1986].
- **How scripted should a lesson be?** Direct Instruction's fully scripted programs show d = 0.54 [Stockard2018]. Across literacy programs more scripting went with less impact [Piper2018], a correlation the authors caution against reading causally. Stream 1 records the criticism of Direct Instruction study quality.
- **Can a model write assessment items unsupervised?** Programming and reading studies say the items are clear with one correct choice and sound statistics [Doughty2024] [SayinGierl2024]. Medical and mathematics studies found 30 to 40% with serious faults [Thoracic2025] [HEDGE2024]. Subject, model generation and the presence of a template differ across these.
- **Does interest personalization raise learning or only interest?** Raised both in two tutor studies and in the pooled estimate [Walkington2013] [BernackiWalkington2018] [Lin2024]. Raised interest and left accuracy level in a 343-student study [TPWP]. Lowered enjoyment for some secondary students [Walkington2024].
- **Is a screen page worse than paper, and is scrolling the reason?** The screen deficit is replicated (g = -0.21). The scrolling contrast points the same way and fell short of significance as a moderator [Delgado2018]. One small experiment supports paging [SanchezWiley2009].
- **Open: the architecture comparison itself.** The same lesson delivered as A, B, C and D with learning, time, cost and error rates measured is the missing study. AutoPBL against a static tutorial is the nearest [AutoPBL2025].
- **Open: adults learning a software tool.** The populations here are school pupils, undergraduates and online crowd workers. AutoPBL's 29 adult beginners learning machine learning by project are the closest match [AutoPBL2025].
- **Open: the coherence of pages assembled from blocks.** Wiley predicts failure for plain concatenation [Wiley2004]. Measured data on assembled expository pages did not turn up.
- **Open: a page that grows while the tutor talks.** The split-attention studies use a fixed lesson beside a chat [ConvAIEye2024] [Kueker2024]. A page written live by the same agent that is chatting is untested.
- **Open: formats.** Authoring time and error rates for markdown against JSON or XML lesson files are unmeasured in what I found.

## What the sources themselves recommend

These are the sources' prescriptions, each attributed. They are listed as evidence of practice and opinion.

- **Give the model the expert's solution before it speaks.** "We enriched our prompts with comprehensive, step-by-step answers, guiding the AI tutor to deliver accurate and high-quality explanations." [Kestin2025]. Problem-specific teacher material "ensures that GPT-4 does not provide incorrect feedback" [Bastani2025]. The tutor should "consistently [take] the additional step to gather context" from human-written exercises, steps, hints and solutions [KhanMath2025].
- **Hold the sequence in the platform.** The AI platform "was designed to guide students sequentially through each part of each problem" after the prompt proved unreliable at it [Kestin2025].
- **Hand-craft the pedagogy and let the model fill it.** "The overall pedagogical design still needs to be handcrafted for the best learning results", and on stored against live utterances "the best option might be a combination of both" [MWPTutor2024].
- **Generate at authoring time and have the instructor verify.** Representations should be produced "at learning design time to be verified by the instructor before it reaches any learner" [DesignTimeGenUI2026].
- **Keep the human approving.** Authoring tasks should "be automated no higher than level 5 ... the point at which the human still approves all decisions and/or suggestions made by the system" [Ososky2017]. "Our Spanish teaching experts always have the final say" [DuolingoLLM]. AI questions are unreliable "for unsupervised use" [Thoracic2025]. Item generation with language models needs "human experts ... for guiding item development, ensuring quality" [AIGReview2025].
- **Screen model drafts with self-consistency and a quick human check.** Generate ten, keep the majority answer, then check, at about 38 seconds per hint [Pardos2024].
- **Monitor items after release and replace weak ones.** A service that watches difficulty and ratings to "determine if questions need to be removed and replaced" [VanCampenhout2025].
- **Start each unit from an observable objective, keep exposition short, and intersperse practice with hints and misconception feedback.** [StraderThille2012]. Decompose objectives "only to the point of being actionable" [Bier2014].
- **Use less text.** "Great attention should be paid to using minimal text while teaching the same core topics" [Edgcomb2015]. Award a few points for doing the activities [EdgcombVahid2016].
- **Structure the lesson and stop short of a word-for-word script.** Guides should be fully scripted early and then taper to "structured lesson plans" with "a clear and consistent instructional model" [Piper2018]. Direct Instruction prescribes field-testing scripts with learners and revising before release [Stockard2018].
- **Match the adaptation to the learner's knowledge.** Direct guidance for novices, annotated choice for the experienced, and systems that can switch between them ("meta-adaptation") [Brusilovsky2003].
- **Write each script as a main question with expectations, misconceptions and a hint ladder.** [Nye2014].
- **Leave out interesting material that does not serve the goal.** "I strongly recommend excluding them from learning materials" [SundarAFT2020]. Mark structure with headings, highlighting and color [Schneider2018] [Fiorella2022]. Put text beside the graphic it explains [SchroederCenkci2018]. Write in a conversational voice [Ginns2013].
- **Show a progress indicator for waits over about 10 seconds, and keep progress displays honest.** [Nielsen1993]. Progress indicators that misstate progress raise "important ethical questions" [Villar2013].
- **Send the first token promptly and stream at reading speed.** [Andes2024]. Fill waits above 4 seconds with natural conversational cues [VRDelay2025].
- **Constrain the output schema and put reasoning before the answer field.** [JSONSchemaBench2025] [StructuredRebuttal]. Or answer in prose first and convert [Tam2024].
- **Plan the interface as a structure before generating it.** Interaction flows and state machines first [GenUIStanford2025]. Use tools, detailed instructions and post-processors [GenUIGoogle2026].
- **Show the model fewer, better-matched tools.** [RAGMCP2025].
- **Keep context in the learning material and license it so others can revise it.** [Wiley2004] [Carpentries2015]. Size a block as the set of topics an expert would not teach apart [Wiley2004].
- **Author in plain text under version control.** [PreTeXtGuide] [LiaScriptDocs] [Runestone2026]. Use a "language-based approach, instead of a tooling-centered one" [LiaScriptDocs].
- **Personalize at the grain of the individual and make the interest the vehicle for the idea.** [Lin2024] [Walkington2013].

### Verdict on the five claims the design leans on

- **1. "In every LLM tutor trial with a learning gain, humans fixed the problems, answers and sequence and the model generated only the help."** The design facts hold for the five trials named and are confirmed on each primary source: instructor answers in the prompt and platform-held sequencing [Kestin2025], teacher-written solutions and common mistakes [Bastani2025], hints generated ahead and screened [Pardos2024], expert misconception explanations and human approval of every message [LearnLMEedi2025] [LearnLMEedi2026]. "Every trial" is too wide a summary of the trials this file lists, for three reasons. First, [AutoPBL2025] had the model write the outline, the blocks, the checkpoint questions and the marking, and learners outscored a static tutorial plus chatbot, and [LearnYourWay2026] and [GAIContext2025] report gains with model-written questions or tasks. Second, two of the five do not show a gain on their far measure: Bastani's guarded tutor raised assisted practice by 127% and the paper says of the unassisted exam "we still do not observe a positive effect", and the 2026 Eedi trial found no gain over static remediation on Star Maths. Third, in [Pardos2024] the model wrote the help itself (worked-solution hints), with no chat. The claim is reworded to cover the five trials only. Use it as "the trials that tested a tutor around human-fixed content got these results", and treat "a model that writes the content cannot produce gains" as untested by them.
- **2. Static expert remediation matched live AI tutoring on a standardized test, and live help won the immediate retry.** Confirmed on both primary sources, with these readings. The -0.01 SD [-0.15, +0.14] and +0.02 SD [-0.12, +0.16] are posterior estimates with 95% credible intervals for baseline-to-endline change on Star Maths against standard remediation, for AI tutoring with question context and with personalised context. N = 1,179 is the whole four-arm trial. The AI messages were approved by human tutors before sending. After adjusting for units completed and gender mix the estimates are +0.05 and +0.09 SD, still spanning zero. The report is a developer technical report dated 29 October 2026 and is not peer reviewed. The 93.0% [90.4, 95.3] and 65.4% [63.8, 66.9] come from the separate 2025 trial (N = 165 in all) and are model-estimated rates of getting the same multiple-choice question right on the second try, where guessing among the remaining options gives 33.3%. On the first question of the next unit the gap was smaller: 56.2% after a static hint, 60.7% after a human tutor, 66.2% after the supervised model.
- **3. Unchecked model drafts have error rates of 5 to 40%.** Each number is confirmed on its primary source, and the three measure different things. 32% is the share of 75 problems whose ChatGPT 3.5 hint had wrong work or a wrong answer, averaged over six raters [Pardos2024]. 5.2% is the share of 291 GPT-4o tutorial blocks with an error, found by two researchers in a pre-study run with five volunteers [AutoPBL2025]. 40% is 8 of 20 Claude Sonnet 3.7 exam questions with a major error by an expert panel, 3 with two correct options, 3 with the wrong key and 2 medically inaccurate [Thoracic2025]. The range is a fair span of those three. The distractor result sits outside it: teachers adopted 37% of generated distractor sets as they stood, so 63% needed edits or were rejected [HEDGE2024]. Two of the four samples are 20 items each.
- **4. Checked and stored drafts taught as well as human-written content.** Confirmed for one trial and no more.
