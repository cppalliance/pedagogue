## Top findings

1. **Mastery-gated units raise end-of-instruction exam scores by about half a standard deviation.** Across 108 controlled studies (72 of Keller's PSI in colleges, 36 of Bloom's Learning for Mastery) the mean effect was 0.52 SD (PSI 0.48, LFM 0.59). Effects were larger on locally made tests (0.57) than on standardized tests (0.29), larger with a higher mastery bar, and larger for weaker students (0.61 vs 0.40 SD). Self-paced PSI lowered course completion (h = -0.14 across 32 studies). Grade A. [Kulik1990]
2. **Direct Instruction curricula show d = 0.54 (0.60 after adjustment) across 328 studies and 3,999 effects, 1966 to 2016.** The design rules behind them are small steps, examples ordered by five "juxtaposition" rules, cumulative review, and field-testing of scripts. The population is mostly school children learning basic skills, and a critical review of 40 reading studies found the gains were mostly word-level and the studies methodologically weak. Grade A for the pooled effect, with a real dispute attached. [Stockard2018] [Twyman2021] [Eppley2019]
3. **Programs built with the whole 4C/ID model show d = 0.79 on performance, from 12 studies with high heterogeneity (I2 = 78.6%).** The effect was stronger in higher education than in lower grades. Grade A, small k. [Costa2022]
4. **Merrill's First Principles have one randomized test in programming and a body of correlational support.** In a randomized experiment with 65 programming novices, explicit instruction set inside whole tasks beat the same instruction set inside topic-by-topic part tasks on near and far transfer. In a survey study of 464 students in 12 courses, students who agreed their course used First Principles and gave them successful engaged time were about 5 times more likely to be rated high masters by instructors. Grade B. [RosenbergKima2022] [Frick2010] [Merrill2002]
5. **Gagne's nine events beat lectures in 11 small health-professions studies (knowledge SMD 1.55, 825 participants), with I2 = 95% and low study quality by the authors' own account.** A three-semester nursing course that adopted the nine events saw better student ratings and a mean grade change that was not significant. Grade B. [Li2025] [Miner2015]
6. **Backward design, ADDIE, Dick and Carey, and elaboration theory tell the designer what order to work in. Their support is small quasi-experiments and expert consensus.** Backward design: one 11-school matched comparison (positive in reading, flat in language arts) and one three-class study. Elaboration theory: a 13-student formative study, and a 36-student experiment where elaboration order, Gagne's hierarchy order and random order did not differ significantly. Grade C to D. [Sterling2007] [Ostinelli2024] [HeasterEkholm2020] [DickCarey2015] [EnglishReigeluth1995] [ElabVsGagne]
7. **The frameworks agree on one order of design work.** Fix the end task, analyze it into skills and prerequisites, write an objective for each, write the check before the teaching, order from simple to complex, then for each unit activate prior knowledge, demonstrate, practice with feedback, check, correct or enrich, and finally try the plan on learners and revise. The components have grade A or B evidence. The order as a whole is grade D. [DickCarey2015] [Sterling2007] [Merrill2002] [TenSteps] [Guskey1988] [Li2025]
8. **Experts leave out about 70% of what they do when they describe it unaided, and training built from cognitive task analysis outperforms training built from expert self-report (Hedges g = 0.871, 20 studies, 56 comparisons).** Three surgeons teaching a procedure omitted 71% of knowledge steps, 51% of action steps and 73% of decision steps. In a second study, trauma surgeons giving unaided free-recall descriptions omitted 68.75% of steps. Prompted interviews raised recall from 44% to 66% of steps. Grade A for the training effect (heterogeneous), grade B for the omission rates (very small samples, one research group). [TofelGrehl2013] [Sullivan2014] [ClarkPugh2012] [Clark2008]
9. **Prerequisite orders drawn by experts agree only partly with learner data.** In logs from 20,577 students, a data test and the expert prerequisite list agreed on 56% of 3,832 unit pairs, and only 14% of pairs were prerequisites by both methods. Early work on Gagne's hierarchies found that teaching missing sub-skills let 18 of 20 children do the final task against 3 of 10 controls, and that hierarchies hold for intellectual skills and not for verbal facts. One validation statistic "validated" a random order as well as the designed one. Grade B. [Vuong2011] [Gagne1970] [White1974] [PhillipsKane1974]
10. **Learning-curve analysis can find a hidden skill, and redesigning the unit around it saved time.** A geometry tutor unit rebuilt from a data-refined skill model got students to mastery in 20.9 minutes against 28.4 (26% less, d = 0.6), with equal post-test scores overall (86.6% vs 85.5%) and a bigger gain on the hardest problem (0.67 to 0.85 against 0.50 to 0.55). N = 96, randomized in class (unconfirmed). The paper reports random assignment for the test forms only. Grade A, single study. [Koedinger2013] [Cen2006]
11. **The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction framework says the instructional move should fit the kind of knowledge.** Facts call for recall practice and spacing. Rules and skills call for worked examples, comparison and tutored practice. Principles with a rationale call for self-explanation and discussion. The authors state this mapping as a hypothesis that "require[s] further testing". Grade D for the mapping, built on grade A component effects. [Koedinger2012] [KoedingerBoothKlahr2013]
12. **Stating objectives helps a little. Turning them into questions the learner answers first helps more.** Undergraduates given objectives before each passage scored 0.55 against 0.47 with none (d = 0.49). In a second experiment, multiple-choice pretest questions gave 0.67 against 0.53 for objective statements (d = 0.84). An older review, seen through a secondary source, says the helpful part of an objective is naming what will be learned and that Mager's conditions and criteria add nothing for the learner. Grade A (lab, immediate test). [Sana2020] [Hamilton1985]
13. **For novices, examples before problems beat problems before examples, and yet problem-solving before instruction beats the reverse for conceptual understanding in a separate literature.** Example-only and example-problem pairs beat problem-only and problem-example pairs (N = 103, partial eta squared = .23). Worked examples in mathematics give g = 0.48 (55 studies). Problem-solving followed by instruction gives g = 0.36 on conceptual knowledge and transfer (53 studies), with the sign reversed for 2nd to 5th graders. Grade A on both sides. [vanGog2011] [Barbieri2023] [SinhaKapur2021]
14. **Breaking a task into parts costs transfer when the parts must later be done together, and costs nothing when the parts run in sequence.** Part-task training lost 13% of transfer overall and 29% when parts were concurrent (g = -0.35). Simple-to-complex ordering helped novices (g = +0.75), helped when difficulty rose adaptively (36% benefit), and hurt when it rose on a fixed schedule (23% cost). Grade A. [Wickens2013] [Plott2014]
15. **Cutting material into segments helps: retention d = 0.32, transfer d = 0.36 (56 studies, 88 comparisons), and it takes learners longer (time d = -0.92).** The minute counts in circulation rest on thinner ground. Knewton's "10 minutes of classroom time" is vendor guidance with no data attached. The "attention fades after 10 to 15 minutes" claim traces to a 1978 review of note-taking. The edX "6 minutes" figure measures how long people watched, and learning was not measured. Grade A for segmenting, grade C to D for any specific length. [Rey2019] [Knewton] [Bradbury2016] [Guo2014]
16. **A short quiz after each segment cut mind wandering from about 40% of probes to 19% and raised the final test to 90% against 76% (restudy) and 68% (no quiz).** One 21-minute online lecture, n = 48 in the key experiment. Grade A, small. [Szpunar2013]
17. **Comparing cases beats studying them one at a time (d = 0.50, 57 experiments, 336 tests), and concrete examples raise concept classification (d = 0.40 to 1.67).** Learners who compared two negotiation cases transferred the principle 48% of the time against 19% for those who read the same two cases separately. Effects were larger when learners were asked for similarities and when the principle came after the comparison. Grade A. [Alfieri2013] [Gentner2003] [Rawson2015]
18. **Interleaving is not a general rule for ordering a plan.** Overall g = 0.42 (59 studies), 0.34 for mathematics tasks, no reliable effect for expository text, and g = -0.39 for words, where blocking won. Grade A. [Brunmair2019]
19. **Concreteness fading (concrete, then a stripped-down form, then abstract) is promising and mostly indirectly supported.** One lab study found the three-step fade gave the best transfer immediately, at one week and at three weeks. A later school physics study found fading and the reverse order equally effective. Grade B. [Fyfe2014] [FadingPhysics2021]
20. **The famous "0.4 to 0.7" effect for formative assessment has no traceable source. The one meta-analysis gives 0.20, and that meta-analysis is itself disputed.** Kingston and Nash found 13 usable studies out of more than 300 (42 effects, median 0.25, weighted mean 0.20). Black and Wiliam declined to pool their studies at all. Grade A, contested. [Bennett2011] [KingstonNash2011] [Briggs2012]
21. **Writing wrong answers from known misconceptions is the standard recommendation, and its learning payoff is still unmeasured in the one large trial.** The Force Concept Inventory keyed its distractors to 28 misconceptions drawn from student interviews. Eedi tags 36,000 distractors to misconceptions. The 175-school Eedi trial lost its attainment outcome to the 2020 exam cancellation and found teachers set far fewer quizzes than planned. Grade C. [Gierl2017] [Hestenes1992] [Barton2018] [EEFEedi2021] [EediBlog]
22. **Items written to be "parallel" often differ in difficulty.** 25% of 20 AI-assisted isomorphic physics banks failed a homogeneity test. 20 of 50 medical item models showed variants more than 0.15 apart in proportion correct. In introductory Python, swapping parameter order, reversing polarity or changing data types cost 3 to nearly 10 percentage points, and having seen a version before was worth 7 to 11 points. Grade B. [Liu2026] [Westacott2023] [Fowler2024] [GoolsbyCole2023]
23. **Before any response data exist, difficulty estimates are rough.** Pooled expert judgments have correlated about .72 to .83 with real difficulty, single judges anywhere from near 0 to .85. Three LLMs reached Spearman correlations of .63 to .67 on primary-school mathematics items, and pairwise "which is harder" prompts did better than absolute ratings. Grade B. [Kolesnikova2026] [AlKhuzaey2023]
24. **For adult learners, the tested moderators are prior experience and the work setting, and Knowles's andragogy remains largely untested.** Learner control has a near-zero average effect (g = 0.02 in training, 0.05 in educational technology). It helped experienced trainees (g = +0.43) and control of pace (g = +0.15), and it hurt when learners chose how much practice or feedback to get (g = -0.19). Transfer to the job tracks cognitive ability (.37), voluntary participation (.34) and transfer climate (.27). Workplace training averages d = 0.60 to 0.63. Grade A. [Rachal2002] [TaylorKroth2009] [Plott2014] [Karich2014] [Blume2010] [Arthur2003]
25. **Model-drafted plans and skill labels reach roughly "good first draft" quality and still need an expert pass.** GPT-4's knowledge-component labels matched the course authors' labels on 56% (chemistry) and 35% (e-learning) of questions, and experts preferred the model's label in about two thirds of the mismatches. GPT-4 lesson plans scored a little above pre-service teachers' plans and reached exemplary-teacher level only after rounds of simulated teaching and revision. LLM prerequisite extraction "still require[s] expert revision". Grade B to C. [Moore2024] [Hu2025] [Yang2025] [ProstateLLM2025]

### 1. Design frameworks and their evidence

**1.1 Mastery learning units (Bloom's Learning for Mastery) and Keller's Personalized System of Instruction**

- **Finding:** Teaching in short units with a check after each, and a required standard before moving on, raises end-of-course exam scores by about half a standard deviation over conventional teaching.
- **Steps prescribed, in order:** (1) set the learning objectives and make instruction, feedback and evaluation consistent with them; (2) organize content into short units - Guskey's account of Bloom gives "about a week or two" per unit in schools; (3) teach the unit; (4) give a formative check; (5) give specific feedback with correctives that offer "alternative approaches and additional time", and enrichment for those who passed; (6) recheck. Guskey names two essential elements: the feedback-corrective-enrichment process, and "congruence among instructional components" (objectives, instruction, feedback and evaluation all line up). PSI as studied was largely self-paced (94% of PSI studies), college-level, with a high mastery standard that every student had to demonstrate formally.
- **Evidence:** 108 controlled field evaluations; 103 reported exam performance. Mean effect 0.52 SD, "from the 50th to the 70th percentile". PSI 0.48 (67 studies), LFM 0.59 (36 studies), difference not significant. 93% of PSI studies and 94% of LFM studies favored mastery. Randomized and non-randomized studies gave the same mean (0.52 each). Eleven studies with a follow-up exam (about 8 weeks later on average) showed 0.71. Low-aptitude students gained 0.61 SD, high-aptitude 0.40. Attitudes toward instruction 0.63 (18 studies), toward the subject 0.40 (14 studies).
- **Caveats:** Five study features predicted bigger effects: group pacing over self-pacing, a higher unit mastery level (mean 90.92%), locally made tests (0.57 across 88 studies) over standardized tests (0.29 across 11 studies; 0.15 across 4 with combined tests), control groups that received less quiz feedback, and social science content. None of the five LFM studies that used standardized tests found even a small effect on them. Slavin's 1987 best-evidence review, discussed in the paper, found a median of 0.25 for pre-college LFM. Guskey and Gates's 0.78 fell to 0.61 once formative-test outcomes and class-level standard deviations were removed. In PSI, 23 of 32 studies showed lower course completion than the conventional class (h = -0.14). Mastery programs "may increase student time on instructional tasks". Guskey adds that mastery learning "offers little or no direction to teachers as to how lessons should be presented".
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Kulik1990] [Guskey1988] [GuskeyQuoted]

**1.2 Direct Instruction (Engelmann)**

- **Finding:** Engelmann-Becker Direct Instruction programs show consistent positive effects on school achievement, and their design method is the most explicit of any framework about how to choose and order examples.
- **Steps prescribed:** Identify the "big ideas", rules and strategies; write clear, consistent wording; choose examples and non-examples and order them by five juxtaposition rules - same wording across neighboring examples; neighbors share as many features as possible; to show a difference, put minimally different examples side by side and treat them differently; to show sameness, put greatly different examples side by side with the same label; to test, use examples in an unpredictable order. Build "tracks": nothing is taught in only one lesson, each lesson adds a small amount of new teaching, everything taught is reviewed or folded into a more complex operation, and later teaching may extend earlier teaching and may never contradict it. Chain three or more tasks (3 to 12 minutes) so the learner applies what was just taught. Place students by tested skill. Field-test and revise.
- **Evidence:** 328 studies, 413 designs, 3,999 effects, 1966 to 2016. Total d = 0.54, 95% CI [0.49, 0.59]; reading 0.51, mathematics 0.55, language 0.54, spelling 0.66. After adjusting for study features the total estimate was 0.60. About one fifth of effects came from studies with random selection or matching of students. Effects declined little in maintenance periods and grew with more exposure. Published and unpublished reports did not differ. Twyman reports "nearly 50 published studies" by Carnine and colleagues testing individual design principles.
- **Caveats:** The studies are of commercial school curricula for children, mostly in reading, mathematics, language and spelling. Some of the included studies were written by the meta-analysis authors themselves (the paper codes for this). Eppley and Dudley-Marling reviewed 40 recent DI reading studies and concluded that the strongest support is "temporary efficacy at improving student performance on word-level skills" and that most studies have "a wide range of methodological problems". Engelmann's mastery numbers (70% first-time correct on new material, 90% on the last three lessons' material, 100% by lesson end) come from an interview and are a designer's rule, with no experiment attached in the source.
- **Grade:** A for the pooled effect; C for the individual design rules as stated here.
- **Sources:** [Stockard2018] [Twyman2021] [DIRubric] [EngelmannInterview] [Eppley2019]

**1.3 4C/ID and the Ten Steps (van Merrienboer and Kirschner)**

- **Finding:** Programs designed with all four components of 4C/ID outperform comparison instruction on knowledge and complex-skill measures.
- **Steps prescribed, in order:** (1) design learning tasks - whole, authentic tasks; (2) design performance assessments; (3) sequence learning tasks into simple-to-complex task classes, with high variability inside a class and support that fades within each class; (4) design supportive information - how the domain is organized and how problems are approached, available throughout a task class; (5) analyze cognitive strategies; (6) analyze mental models; (7) design procedural information - how-to steps given just in time for routine aspects; (8) analyze cognitive rules; (9) analyze prerequisite information; (10) design part-task practice for routine aspects that need automaticity. Steps 1, 4, 7 and 10 produce the four components; the other six are done "only when necessary".
- **Evidence:** 12 studies that used the whole model, found by a PRISMA search up to May 2020. d = 0.79, 95% CI [0.50, 1.08]. The effect did not differ by subject area, study design, or outcome type (knowledge vs complex skill). Grade level was a significant moderator explaining 76.22% of heterogeneity, with higher education stronger; the study with the smallest effect was in two elementary schools.
- **Caveats:** Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 78.6%). Twelve studies is a small base. Studies that used only part of the model were excluded, so the result speaks to the package and says little about which component does the work. The authors could not analyze duration.
- **Grade:** A (small k).
- **Sources:** [Costa2022] [TenSteps]

**1.4 Merrill's First Principles of Instruction**

- **Finding:** Instruction centered on whole real-world tasks, with activation, demonstration, application and integration phases, has one randomized test in programming and consistent correlational support.
- **Steps prescribed:** A problem-centered cycle of four phases: (1) activation of prior experience, including giving a structure to organize new knowledge; (2) demonstration - "show me"; (3) application - "let me", with feedback; (4) integration into the learner's own work. Corollaries: show the whole task the learner will be able to do, in place of an abstract objective statement; engage the learner at the task level and not only at the level of single commands or actions; use a progression of problems that are explicitly compared with one another. Merrill criticizes "topic based" instruction that teaches "all the prerequisites before introducing the real world whole task".
- **Evidence:** Randomized experiment, 65 students with no programming experience, online: explicit instruction in the context of whole tasks beat the same explicit instruction organized by objectives and topic-related part tasks on near and far transfer post-tests; confidence and cognitive load during learning partly mediated the effect. Survey study, 464 students in 12 university courses: students who agreed that First Principles were used and that they experienced academic learning time were about 5 times more likely to be rated high masters, and 26 times less likely to be rated low masters, by instructors rating about a month after the course.
- **Caveats:** Merrill's 2002 paper is a synthesis of design theories and reports no experiment of its own. The Frick study is correlational: student perceptions and instructor ratings, with good students perhaps rating good courses higher. The randomized study has Merrill as a co-author, one sample of 65, and I saw only the abstract.
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [Merrill2002] [RosenbergKima2022] [Frick2010]

**1.5 Gagne's nine events of instruction**

- **Finding:** Lessons structured on the nine events outscored lectures in a set of small health-professions studies; the evidence base is weak in quality.
- **Steps prescribed, in order:** gain attention; inform learners of the objectives; stimulate recall of prior learning; present the stimulus; provide learner guidance; elicit performance; provide feedback; assess performance; enhance retention and transfer.
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 11 studies (5 randomized trials and 6 cohort studies per the abstract), 825 participants, medical and nursing learners, comparison with lecture-based teaching. Knowledge exam SMD 1.55, 95% CI [0.81, 2.29], I2 = 95%. Practice score SMD 1.83 [1.19, 2.47], 8 studies, I2 = 90%. Learning compliance OR 4.92; satisfaction OR 7.86.
- **Caveats:** The authors write that included studies "generally exhibited relatively low quality and small sample sizes". Most are from Chinese databases. The comparison is a one-way lecture, so the result may reflect any structured active lesson. The risk-of-bias text counts 4 randomized trials where the abstract says 5. A US nursing course that adopted the events over three semesters (grade rosters of 80, 81 and 84 students) found improved student ratings and mean grades that "did not change significantly".
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [Li2025] [Miner2015]

**1.6 Backward design (Wiggins and McTighe, Understanding by Design)**

- **Finding:** Backward design prescribes the order of the designer's work. Its tests are few and small.
- **Steps prescribed, in order:** (1) identify desired results; (2) determine acceptable evidence; (3) plan learning experiences and instruction.
- **Evidence:** Empirical Education's study for ASCD compared 11 Georgia schools trained in Understanding by Design plus Differentiated Instruction with three matched schools each: a positive difference in the share of students meeting reading standards, and no difference in English language arts. A Swiss vocational-school study had one teacher teach two classes (15 and 18 students) with UbD and one (16) as usual: 6 of 12 test items showed significant differences favoring UbD, p from .002 to .03. Two further school studies (40 Turkish fourth-graders over five weeks; Pakistani fifth-graders) report gains, seen as abstracts only.
- **Caveats:** The ASCD study authors state that "the comparison group design and the very small sample ... put serious limitations on what we can conclude" and that they "cannot conclude that implementation of the program directly caused an improvement". The Swiss study has one teacher and 49 students. Ostinelli notes that UbD has "rarely ... been the object of experimental or quasi-experimental studies".
- **Grade:** C.
- **Sources:** [Sterling2007] [Ostinelli2024] [UbDTurkey] [UbDPakistan]

**1.7 ADDIE and the Dick and Carey systems approach**

- **Finding:** Both describe a project process for building instruction. Support is practitioner consensus plus small school studies of Dick and Carey.
- **Steps prescribed:** ADDIE: analyze, design, develop, implement, evaluate. Heaster-Ekholm calls ADDIE "not a distinct model as much as it is a design process", with no clear original author, "slim on details and specific guidelines". Dick and Carey, ten steps: (1) identify instructional goals; (2) conduct instructional analysis, step by step, down to entry skills; (3) analyze learners and contexts; (4) write performance objectives; (5) develop assessment instruments matched to the objectives; (6) develop an instructional strategy; (7) develop and select materials; (8) design and conduct formative evaluation - one-to-one, small-group, field trial; (9) revise; (10) summative evaluation. Each step's output feeds the next.
- **Evidence:** Small quasi-experiments in schools, for example 102 Nigerian secondary biology students with a significant effect on achievement, and two Indonesian school studies seen only as search snippets (DOIs 10.29303/jppipa.v11i7.12241 and 10.29303/jppipa.v11i10.11900).
- **Caveats:** Every study I found compares a unit designed with the model against usual teaching, so each tests design effort as much as the model. A comparison of the Dick and Carey order against another order of the same steps remains to be run.
- **Grade:** D for ADDIE, C for Dick and Carey.
- **Sources:** [HeasterEkholm2020] [DickCarey2015] [DickCareyBiology]

**1.8 Elaboration theory (Reigeluth)**

- **Finding:** Elaboration theory prescribes starting with an "epitome" - the simplest real version of the whole - and elaborating toward complexity. Its own authors describe the research base as thin, and the one controlled comparison I found showed no significant advantage.
- **Evidence:** English and Reigeluth revised four chapters of an electrical circuit analysis text to follow the theory and collected mostly qualitative reactions from 13 college students aged 18 to 50. Students praised the epitome for ease of understanding and focus. Post-test mean was 91.8 with no comparison group. The paper states "there has been relatively little empirical research on the theory". Separately, 36 community-college freshmen read a 2,000-word passage ordered by elaboration theory, by Gagne's hierarchy, or at random: the F-test found no significant difference, with both designed orders averaging above random.
- **Caveats:** n = 13 and n = 36. A 1992 critical review by Wilson and Cole exists (DOI 10.1007/bf02296843); I saw its citation record and did not read it.
- **Grade:** C.
- **Sources:** [EnglishReigeluth1995] [ElabVsGagne]

**1.9 Rosenshine's principles (a practice-derived checklist)**

- **Finding:** Rosenshine's ten principles prescribe a lesson order that matches the frameworks above: short review, new material in small steps with practice after each, many questions, models and worked examples, guided practice, checks for understanding, a high success rate, scaffolds, independent practice, weekly and monthly review.
- **Evidence:** In the fourth-grade mathematics study he cites, students of the most successful teachers answered 82% correctly against 73% for the least successful, and "the optimal success rate ... appears to be about 80 percent".
- **Caveats:** A synthesis for teachers that draws on classroom observation studies of school children. Correlational at its root.
- **Grade:** C.
- **Sources:** [Rosenshine2012]

### 2. Learning objectives

**2.1 How objectives are written**

- **Finding:** Three schemes are in common use and they answer different questions. Mager: what must the learner do, under what conditions, how well. Revised Bloom: what kind of knowledge and what cognitive process. Webb: how deep is the thinking the task demands.
- **Evidence (what each prescribes):**
  - Mager: an objective states (1) the performance - what the learner will be doing or producing; (2) the important conditions of the performance; (3) the criterion of acceptable performance. If the main intent is covert, add a simple indicator behavior. Leave out teaching procedures and audience descriptions.
  - Revised Bloom (Anderson and Krathwohl, reported by Krathwohl): two dimensions. Knowledge: factual, conceptual, procedural (which includes "criteria for determining when to use appropriate procedures"), metacognitive. Cognitive process: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create.
  - Webb's depth of knowledge: four levels - recall and reproduction; skills and concepts; strategic thinking; extended thinking. The guides stress that the level describes "the kind of thinking required by a task, not whether or not the task is difficult", and that the verb alone does not set the level.
- **Caveats:** These are classification schemes. Their evidence is about whether raters agree, and agreement takes training: two trained raters reached weighted kappa 0.92 in English language arts; the two mathematics raters missed the target in the first round and reached 0.93 after a second calibration. A separate comparison found DOK levels correlated .849 with item solution rates in one mathematics item set, against .696 for Bloom-based ratings, and five experts ranking item difficulty agreed at Krippendorff's alpha = 0.73 (unconfirmed). The mark applies to all three figures in that sentence.
- **Grade:** D for the schemes as guides to design; C for rater reliability.
- **Sources:** [Mager1997] [Krathwohl2002] [Hess2009] [DOKGuide] [YuanLe2012] [DifficultyRankings2023]

**2.2 Does telling learners the objectives help?**

- **Finding:** Yes, modestly, on the material the objectives name. Asking the learner to answer the objective as a question first works better than stating it.
- **Evidence:** Sana and colleagues, Experiment 1: undergraduates read five 5-minute passages (the first on mirror neurons). Final-test accuracy was 0.47 with no objectives, 0.55 with three objectives before each passage (d = 0.49, p = .044 against control), and 0.53 with all objectives up front (not significant against control). Experiment 2 (158 students, three randomized groups): multiple-choice pretest questions before each passage gave 0.67; objective statements gave 0.53 (d = 0.84); statements that included the answer gave 0.57 (d = 0.51 against pretest). Pretest accuracy itself was 0.35, and no feedback was given. Older reviews: Melton (1978) describes two opposed claims - objectives focus learners on what is required, and objectives narrow learning and depress incidental learning. Hamilton (1985), as quoted by a practitioner source: an objective "that generally identifies the information to be learned in the text will produce robust effects. Including other information (per Mager's, 1962, definition) will not significantly help and it may hinder". The same source cites Rothkopf and Billington (1979) for gains of 49% and 47% on objective-related material, and reports that objectives reduce attention to material they do not mention.
- **Caveats:** Sana's studies are single-session lab studies with a test minutes after reading, on factual passages. The authors call theirs "the first in the literature to directly compare" statements with pretests. The Hamilton and Rothkopf figures are secondary. Merrill argues on theoretical grounds that showing a worked instance of the whole task orients learners better than "a list of abstract objective statements".
- **Grade:** A for the lab effect; C for the older claims as reported here.
- **Sources:** [Sana2020] [Hamilton1985] [Melton1978] [Merrill2002]

**2.3 Constructive alignment (Biggs)**

- **Finding:** Lining up intended outcomes, activities and assessment is a shared requirement of backward design, Dick and Carey, mastery learning and Biggs. Direct tests are few and quasi-experimental.
- **Evidence:** A management information systems course in Macao taught with an alignment template to the 2020 cohort (62 students) was compared with the 2019 cohort (108 students): 14 of 16 course-experience items improved significantly (d from 0.34 to 0.67), and grades were higher (Mann-Whitney p = .010). An abstract-only study reports that students in more aligned courses were more likely to adopt deep learning approaches and less likely to use surface approaches. A 2026 systematic review of 76 higher-education design studies reports a mean d of 0.74 for constructive alignment, described by its authors as a descriptive aggregate across a handful of studies.
- **Caveats:** Successive cohorts, one instructor team, no randomization. The 0.74 figure pools four heterogeneous estimates (one of them a t value) in an open-review journal, and should be read as indicative only. Guskey makes the same alignment demand under the name "congruence among instructional components" and treats it as one of the two essentials of mastery learning.
- **Grade:** B to C.
- **Sources:** [Zhang2022] [AlignmentApproaches2012] [Wariyo2026] [Guskey1988]

### 3. Kinds of knowledge and which instruction fits which

**3.1 The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction (KLI) framework and knowledge components**

- **Finding:** KLI defines the unit of analysis for a lesson plan - the knowledge component - and proposes that the best instructional move depends on the kind of component.
- **Evidence (what the framework says):**
  - A knowledge component (KC) is "an acquired unit of cognitive function or structure that can be inferred from performance on a set of related tasks". The term covers facts, concepts, skills, production rules, schemas, principles, and also misconceptions.
  - Where to cut: analysis should "focus on the component level at which the novice student makes errors", a level "just above the level at which novices have achieved success and fluency". Lower levels can be treated as atomic if the target learners have mastered them.
  - Kinds of KC, by whether the condition and the response are constant or variable: facts and associations (constant-constant); categories and concepts (variable-constant); rules, skills, schemas (variable-variable); and, when a KC has a verbal form and a rationale, principles. The authors stress "knowing when": "learning the conditions of application of knowledge ... may be more difficult than learning possible responses".
  - Three kinds of learning process: memory and fluency building; induction and refinement; understanding and sense-making.
  - The mapping (Table 4 and section 7 of the paper): for facts, memory and fluency processes matter most and there is "nothing to explain". For rules, induction and refinement matter most, memory still matters, and explanation may add little. For principles, sense-making matters most, and memory and induction also matter. Stated as an "asymmetry hypothesis": simple processes support complex knowledge, and complex processes (such as argumentation) "may fail to support simple knowledge".
  - Matching instructional principles (Table 5, simplest to most complex): spacing and testing; optimized scheduling; timely feedback; feature focusing; worked examples; prompted self-explanation; accountable talk. In the authors' words: simple facts and perceptual categories "may imply instructional approaches that emphasize recall, spacing of practice, tutored practice"; complex rules "imply instruction that includes comparison / blocking and more worked example study"; principles in both procedural and verbal form "imply instruction that prompts for self-explanations".
  - Stage matters too: for a rule, induction is critical first, then "memory and fluency process are important to improve retrieval reliability and application speed".
- **Caveats:** The authors write that "these hypotheses require further testing" and that the taxonomy "does not resolve these debates". The single plus signs in their table mark places where "research is insufficient and there are opposing views". A companion paper counts 30 separate instructional techniques and shows that 15 of them at three dosage levels, chosen separately for early and late instruction, give 3 to the power 30, about 205 trillion, combinations - their argument that the space is too large to settle by trial alone and needs a knowledge-type theory to narrow it.
- **Grade:** D for the mapping as a whole. The component effects it cites (spacing, testing, worked examples, self-explanation, feedback) have grade A evidence in their own literatures.
- **Sources:** [Koedinger2012] [KoedingerBoothKlahr2013]

**3.2 Declarative, procedural and conceptual knowledge - which comes first**

- **Finding:** Conceptual and procedural knowledge feed each other in both directions, and no study was found that directly compares teaching concepts first with teaching procedures first.
- **Evidence:** Rittle-Johnson, Schneider and Star review longitudinal and experimental work in mathematics. A brief lesson on a procedure improved children's grasp of the concept, and a brief lesson on the concept improved procedure use, each against a no-lesson control. Practice problems sequenced by a conceptual principle improved both kinds of knowledge; the same problems in random order gave only modest procedural gains. They write: "we could not find a study that compared the effectiveness of instruction on concepts-then-procedures to instruction on procedures-then-concepts". The search summary of the same review reports that the one ordering study they describe found an iterative order gave equal conceptual learning and more procedural learning than concepts-first.
- **Caveats:** School mathematics. The CTA meta-analysis, in adults and professionals, found CTA-based training helped procedural outcomes (g = 0.872) and declarative outcomes (g = 0.926) about equally.
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [RittleJohnson2015] [TofelGrehl2013]

**3.3 Evidence on fit between task type and instructional move, from training research**

- **Finding:** A set of Army-funded meta-analyses on adult skill training found that the same method helps or hurts depending on the kind of task and the learner's experience.
- **Evidence:** Learner control: a small cost for factual knowledge (g = -0.06) and a small benefit for procedural and problem-solving tasks (g = +0.09). Part-task training: a strong cost on low-complexity tasks (transfer ratio 0.60) and little cost on high-complexity tasks (0.83). Arthur and colleagues' meta-analysis of workplace training likewise reports that "the training method used, the skill or task characteristic trained, and the choice of evaluation criteria were related to the effectiveness of training programs".
- **Caveats:** Moderator cells are small (some with 1 to 5 effect sizes).
- **Grade:** A for the overall analyses, B for the moderator cells.
- **Sources:** [Plott2014] [Wickens2013] [Arthur2003]

### 4. Task analysis and prerequisite structure

**4.1 Cognitive task analysis (CTA) and the expert blind spot**

- **Finding:** Experts cannot fully report their own skill. Structured elicitation recovers part of what free description misses, and training built on it works better.
- **Steps prescribed (Clark and colleagues' five-stage CTA):** (1) collect preliminary knowledge - read documents, observe, pick two or more experts with a record of success, and interview them separately "to avoid premature consensus"; (2) identify knowledge representations - sub-tasks and types of knowledge, for example a learning hierarchy; (3) apply focused knowledge elicitation methods; (4) analyze and verify the data acquired; (5) format the results for the intended use.
- **Evidence:**
  - Training effect: 20 studies with a non-CTA control gave 56 comparisons; Hedges g = 0.871 (SD 0.846). By outcome: procedural knowledge 0.872, declarative knowledge 0.926, self-efficacy 0.893, performance speed 0.611. By method, where reported: Critical Decision Method 0.329, PARI 1.598, unidentified methods 0.729.
  - Omission rates: three surgeons videotaped teaching a cricothyrotomy omitted 71% (10 of 14) of clinical knowledge steps, 51% (14 of 27) of action steps and 73% (3.6 of 5) of decision steps, measured against a "gold standard" task list built from CTA interviews with those three surgeons and three more. They explained how to do an action step only 13% of the time. In CTA interviews the share of steps described rose from 44% unprompted to 66% prompted. In a second study of ten trauma surgeons (four gave unaided free recall, five gave free recall with instruments and visual aids, one had a CTA interview), the unaided descriptions of a femoral shunt procedure agreed with the CTA-derived protocol on 31.25% of steps, an omission of 68.75%; the CTA interview was about 70% more complete and accurate than free recall. The CTA chapter attributes "about 70% of the important decisions" to several studies.
  - Blind spot in judging difficulty: 48 pre-service teachers with more advanced mathematics training were more likely to assume students must master equations before story problems, "in contrast with students' actual performance patterns" - students solved simple story problems better than the matching equations.
- **Caveats:** The meta-analysis authors say the literature is "highly divergent and relatively sparse", that only 15 of 56 comparisons named their CTA method, and that the PARI figure rests on few studies. The omission studies involve 3 and 9 or 10 surgeons, come from one group (Clark, Sullivan, Yates), and define "omitted" against a list that the same method produced. The "70% rule" is their label. Even prompted, experts covered 66% of steps, which is why the method calls for several experts.
- **Grade:** A for the training effect (with wide spread); B for omission rates.
- **Sources:** [TofelGrehl2013] [Clark2008] [Sullivan2014] [ClarkPugh2012] [Nathan2003] [KoedingerNathan2004]

**4.2 Gagne's learning hierarchies**

- **Finding:** Working backward from a target skill to its sub-skills gives a usable teaching order for intellectual skills. The classic way of validating such a hierarchy from test data is unreliable.
- **How the order is derived:** Ask of the target, "what must the learner be able to do to learn this?", and repeat downward until reaching skills the learners already have. Clark and colleagues describe the result as "a chart of tasks for each intellectual skill".
- **Evidence:** In a study reported by Gagne, 30 of 31 sixth-graders could not do a physics inclined-plane task. They were tested downward through the hierarchy, and two groups of 10 were then retested upward on alternate forms of the items (one group with demonstrations). 18 of the 20 then did the final task and 19 of 20 did a transfer task, against 3 of 10 controls, and those three had no missing prerequisites to begin with. White's 1974 nine-stage validation procedure led to "strong evidence" that "generalized intellectual skills are learned hierarchically while specific individual facts, termed verbalized knowledge, are not".
- **Caveats:** Phillips and Kane built a hierarchy for whole-number addition and tested 111 children per form in grades 3 to 6. The standard index (proportion of positive transfer between adjacent levels, with .90 as the bar) came out at .90 or above for the designed order in all cases but one (.84), and it also "validated" a randomly ordered version. Their conclusion: the procedure "is not a sufficient criterion for ordering the subordinate levels". White's 1973 review, seen as a search summary only, called the hierarchy research to that date inconclusive because of weak validation methods.
- **Grade:** B (small, old studies).
- **Sources:** [Gagne1970] [White1974] [PhillipsKane1974] [Clark2008]

**4.3 Knowledge space theory (Doignon and Falmagne) and ALEKS**

- **Finding:** Knowledge space theory turns prerequisite judgments into a formal structure of feasible knowledge states, built first from expert answers to a fixed kind of question and then pruned with student data.
- **How the order is derived:** Experts are asked questions of the form "Suppose that a student is not capable of solving problem p. Could this student nevertheless solve problem p'?" and the harder multi-item form "Suppose that a student has not mastered problems p1 ... pn. Could this student nevertheless solve problem p0?". Many answers can be inferred from earlier ones; even so "an expert may be required to respond to a few thousand questions". Several experts are interviewed and their answers combined. The structure allows more than one prerequisite set for the same item.
- **How it is validated:** With student data. States that rarely occur are deleted. In most assessments one extra problem is added that plays no part in choosing the student's state; the state predicts whether the student will get it right. For Beginning Algebra the correlation between predicted and observed answers "hovers between .7 and .8".
- **Evidence of learning effect:** A meta-analysis of ALEKS (15 studies, 24 samples, 2005 to 2015) found g = 0.08 under a fixed-effect model (95% CI [0.02, 0.15]) and 0.10 under random effects ([-0.08, 0.27], not significant): "as good, but not better than, traditional classroom teaching".
- **Caveats:** The ALEKS paper says expert consensus "alone does not guarantee the validity of the knowledge structure". A well-validated prerequisite structure did not by itself yield a learning advantage over classroom teaching.
- **Grade:** C for the building method (developer's description); A for the ALEKS outcome meta-analysis.
- **Sources:** [FalmagneALEKS] [Fang2019]

**4.4 Checking a skill model and a prerequisite order with learner data**

- **Finding:** Two data methods are documented with results: learning-curve analysis of knowledge components, and comparing learners who did or did not take an earlier unit.
- **Evidence:**
  - Learning Factors Analysis fits a logistic regression with an intercept per student, an intercept per skill (how hard it is at the start) and a slope per skill (how fast it is learned), then searches over alternative skill models that split or merge skills using expert-proposed "difficulty factors", scoring each by AIC or BIC. A skill whose curve is bumpy or flat is a sign of a hidden component.
  - Closing the loop: in a geometry tutor, the "compose-by-addition" skill showed a rough curve and a slope estimate of 0. It was split into three (scaffolded composition, unscaffolded decomposition planning, plain subtraction), the tutor gained new problems that isolated the planning skill, and an in-class randomized (unconfirmed) comparison followed (103 students enrolled, 96 analyzed, 48 per arm). The paper calls it an "in vivo experiment" and reports random assignment for the test forms only. Time to mastery 20.9 against 28.4 minutes (p = .03, d = 0.6) while solving more problems (14.0 against 10.4). Post-test 86.6% against 85.5% (no reliable difference). On the hardest, unscaffolded problem the treatment group rose from .67 to .85 and the control from .50 to .55.
  - Prerequisite checking: Carnegie Learning used the fact that 85% of teachers customized their unit sequence. For each of 3,832 unit pairs they compared the rate of "graduating" from the first section of unit B between students who had and had not done unit A (binomial test, alpha = .01; 20,577 students, 888 schools). About 43% of candidate pairs were significant. Against the expert list built from shared skills in the cognitive models, agreement was 56%, and 14% of pairs were prerequisites by both methods.
  - Learning progressions are defined as "empirically-grounded and testable hypotheses" about how understanding grows "with appropriate instruction", to be tested for construct validity ("does the hypothesized sequence describe a path most students actually experience?") and consequential validity ("does instruction based on the learning progression produce better results?"). They are contrasted with "selecting sequences of topics ... based only on logical analysis of current disciplinary knowledge and on personal experiences in teaching".
- **Caveats:** In the geometry study the two tutors used different mastery criteria, so the authors rest the claim on the shared post-test. The redesign also depended on "human inspection of learning curves". The prerequisite study is observational: students whose teachers skipped a unit may differ, the authors say many early-unit "prerequisites" failed the test perhaps because students already knew the material, and they note that "since there is not already an agreed-upon way to objectively determine prerequisites, it is difficult to assess the validity of our method".
- **Grade:** A for the redesign trial (one study); B for the prerequisite method.
- **Sources:** [Cen2006] [Koedinger2013] [Vuong2011] [Corcoran2009]

### 5. Sequencing

**5.1 Whole task against part task, and simple to complex**

- **Finding:** Keep the whole task intact where its parts interact, and raise difficulty in response to the learner's performance.
- **Evidence:** Wickens and colleagues pooled 37 transfer studies of adult skill training (the fuller Army report lists 22 part-task studies giving 65 transfer-ratio estimates and 35 Hedges g estimates).
  - Part-task training against whole-task training: transfer ratio 0.87 (a 13% cost), g = -0.06.
  - When the parts are performed at the same time in the real task: transfer ratio 0.71, g = -0.35. When the parts follow one another in sequence: "neither cost nor benefit".
  - Variable-priority training (the whole task stays intact and the emphasis shifts between its parts): transfer ratio 1.27, g = +0.74.
  - With no instructor present, part-task training turned into a benefit (transfer ratio 1.11, g = +0.40), and likewise in computer-based instruction (1.09, g = +0.32).
  - Increasing difficulty: no overall effect (transfer ratio 1.22, g = +0.03). It helped inexperienced trainees (g = +0.75). Raising difficulty adaptively, from the trainee's performance, gave a 36% benefit over the comparison; raising it on a fixed schedule gave a 23% cost.
  - 4C/ID builds on the same idea: whole tasks in simple-to-complex task classes [TenSteps], and a randomized test in programming favored whole-task over part-task organization [RosenbergKima2022].
- **Caveats:** Many of the pooled studies train perceptual-motor and military tasks. Several moderator cells hold very few effects. The benefit of part-task training in instructor-free, computer-based settings runs against the headline and bears directly on self-study software.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Wickens2013] [Plott2014] [TenSteps] [RosenbergKima2022]

**5.2 Example-problem pairs**

- **Finding:** For novices, study a worked example and then solve a matching problem. The reverse order performed like no examples at all.
- **Evidence:** 103 Dutch secondary students, novices at electrical-circuit troubleshooting, randomized to four conditions (26, 26, 25, 26): problem solving only, problem-example pairs, example-problem pairs, examples only. Test performance differed by condition (F(3,91) = 9.00, partial eta squared = .23). Examples only and example-problem pairs both beat problems only (p < .05) and problem-example pairs (p < .01). Examples only and example-problem pairs did not differ. Mental effort during training was lower in the two example-first conditions (partial eta squared = .20). The wider literature: worked examples in mathematics, 55 studies and 181 effect sizes, g = 0.48.
- **Caveats:** One session, one domain, an immediate test. The authors note that example-problem pairs were "not more effective or efficient than examples only", against the expectation that practice after each example adds something. In the Barbieri meta-analysis, adding self-explanation prompts lowered the effect, and correct examples alone did better than incorrect or mixed examples. Most of those studies involve middle-school students and older, and mathematics only.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [vanGog2011] [Barbieri2023]

**5.3 Instruction first or problem first**

- **Finding:** A separate body of work finds that letting learners attempt a problem before the explanation improves conceptual understanding and transfer, at no gain for procedural skill.
- **Evidence:** 53 studies, 166 comparisons of problem-solving-then-instruction (PS-I) against instruction-then-problem-solving. Conceptual knowledge and transfer: g = 0.36, 95% CI [0.20, 0.51]. With high fidelity to "productive failure" design: g between 0.37 and 0.58. In a related physics study, adolescents who invented formulas from contrasting cases before being told matched a tell-and-practice group on word problems and did better on recalling deep structure (d = .30) and on transfer three weeks later (d = .33).
- **Caveats:** The search covered only papers that cite the productive-failure papers, which tilts the sample toward that tradition. For 2nd to 5th graders and for domain-general skills the effect favored instruction first. The authors' bias-adjusted estimate (0.87) is a model output. This result and the worked-example result (5.2) point in different directions for the first minutes of a unit; see Disagreements.
- **Grade:** A, contested.
- **Sources:** [SinhaKapur2021] [Schwartz2011]

**5.4 Concreteness fading**

- **Finding:** Start concrete, pass through a stripped-down intermediate form, and end with the abstract symbol. The review's own verdict is that direct evidence for the full three steps is limited.
- **Evidence:** Fyfe and colleagues: "most evidence in favor of this method is admittedly indirect and restricted to the domains of mathematics and science". Direct tests they cite: undergraduates learning a science principle from simulations transferred best when display elements switched from concrete to abstract (a two-step fade). In combinatorics, concrete-first beat concrete-only and abstract-first, and matched abstract-only. Undergraduates learning modular arithmetic with a full three-step fade showed the best transfer immediately, one week later and three weeks later. A later study of high-school electromagnetic induction found fading and the reverse order "equally effective" (abstract only).
- **Caveats:** Few studies, several unpublished at the time of the review, mostly short lab sessions.
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [Fyfe2014] [FadingPhysics2021]

**5.5 Blocked against interleaved order at the plan level**

- **Finding:** Interleaving helps learners tell similar categories apart. It does not help, and can hurt, for text and vocabulary.
- **Evidence:** 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples. Overall g = 0.42, 95% CI [0.34, 0.50]. Paintings 0.67; naturalistic photographs 0.35; mathematical tasks 0.34 [0.11, 0.57]; expository texts and tastes not significant; words -0.39 (blocking better). Effects were stronger when categories were similar to one another, when items within a category were dissimilar, and for more complex material. The KLI framework files comparison and blocking under induction of rules, and spacing and interleaved recall under memory for facts, and notes that "blocked comparison of examples is at odds with spacing" unless the kind of knowledge is taken into account. Rawson and colleagues found that interleaving examples of different concepts helped when definitions were absent and made no difference when definitions were shown.
- **Caveats:** Most studies use category-learning materials and short delays. The authors advise caution "especially for expository texts and words".
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Brunmair2019] [Koedinger2012] [Rawson2015]

**5.6 Spiral curriculum**

- **Finding:** Revisiting topics at rising depth is widely used and has little direct evidence as a whole design.
- **Evidence:** An ERIC research brief states: "there is no clear empirical evidence of the overall effects of the spiral curriculum on student learning", while its features (revisiting, rising complexity, linking new to old) each have support. A Cambridge Assessment review notes that Bruner's book offered little evidence, and lists Snider's (2004) criticisms of spiral mathematics programs: superficial treatment because another visit is promised, the same time allotted to easy and hard topics, and too long a gap between visits for review to work.
- **Caveats:** The spiral is usually delivered together with inquiry methods, which makes its own effect hard to isolate, as the brief itself says. Direct Instruction's "track" design is a tighter version of revisiting: taught over two or more consecutive lessons and then reviewed or folded into later work [DIRubric].
- **Grade:** D.
- **Sources:** [Johnston2012] [IrelandMouthaan2020] [DIRubric]

### 6. Grain size

**6.1 The segmenting principle**

- **Finding:** Material presented in meaningful segments is learned better than the same material presented as one continuous unit, and it takes longer.
- **Evidence:** 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons, mean participant age 19.71. Retention d = 0.32, 95% CI [0.20, 0.43] (67 comparisons, 6,100 participants). Transfer d = 0.36 [0.24, 0.48] (56 comparisons, 4,754 participants). Overall cognitive load d = 0.23 (lower with segments). Learning time d = -0.92 [-1.64, -0.20]: segmented material took more time. When the designer sets the segments and the system paces them: retention 0.42, transfer 0.35. When the learner controls the pace: retention 0.19 (not significant), transfer 0.45.
- **Caveats:** Heterogeneity is large on every outcome. The extra time means part of the benefit may simply be more time on the material; the authors test this as one of three explanations. The abstract reports that learners with high prior knowledge benefited more than those with none or little on retention, which runs against the usual expectation. Most materials were short multimedia lessons, and most participants had little prior knowledge. The meta-analysis does not give a segment length in minutes.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Rey2019]

**6.2 How long should one unit be - the minute counts**

- **Finding:** Each of the three figures in circulation has a narrower basis than its use suggests.
- **Evidence:**
  - Knewton's developer guidance asks "what is the smallest meaningful level at which to track students' knowledge?" and offers three examples of a reasonable objective size: one "that takes 10 minutes of classroom time to teach", one "taught by a 3-page subsection of a textbook", one that "can be assessed sufficiently with 10 questions". It warns against objectives that are too broad ("Math") or too narrow (a single question), and says the right size "depends on the level of the learners". The page gives the examples as rules of thumb and shows no data for them.
  - The "attention drops after 10 to 15 minutes" claim: Bradbury traced the citations to a 1978 review by Hartley and Davies whose subject is note-taking. His conclusion: "the available primary data do not support the concept of a 10- to 15-min attention limit", and the largest source of variation in attention is the teacher.
  - The edX "6 minutes": across four courses from autumn 2012, median engagement time was at most 6 minutes whatever the video's length, students often got less than halfway through videos longer than 9 minutes, and the share of sessions followed by an attempt at the next problem fell from 56% for the shortest videos to 31% for the longest. The authors say engagement time "cannot capture whether a watcher is actively paying attention" and learning was not measured.
  - Direct Instruction's rubric gives 3 to 12 minutes for a task chain and "only a small amount of new teaching" per lesson [DIRubric]. Bloom-style mastery units in schools ran one to two weeks [GuskeyQuoted]. One nursing course following Gagne delivered "new content every 10-15 minutes" [Miner2015].
- **Caveats:** None of these sources tests unit length against learning with adults studying a technical tool.
- **Grade:** C for Knewton and the DI rubric (deployed practice); B for the edX data (large observational); the 10 to 15 minute attention claim is unsupported.
- **Sources:** [Knewton] [Bradbury2016] [Guo2014] [DIRubric] [GuskeyQuoted] [Miner2015]

**6.3 A check between segments**

- **Finding:** Short tests placed between segments keep attention on the material and improve learning of later segments.
- **Evidence:** A 21-minute online statistics lecture in four segments. Experiment 2 (n = 48): students tested after every segment were caught mind wandering on 19% of probes, against 39% in a group that restudied the questions with answers and 41% in a group with no tests. Final cumulative test, which the paper analyzes across both experiments: 90% for the tested group, 76% restudy, 68% untested (first taken from two secondary pages that summarize the paper; the citation check found the same three figures in the paper itself). Experiment 1 (n = 32): tested students took notes on 17% of slides against 6%.
- **Caveats:** One short lecture, undergraduates. A commentator on the paper warns that the benefit may wash out over the length of a real course. Rosenshine's "small steps with practice after each step" is the classroom form of the same idea [Rosenshine2012].
- **Grade:** A (small).
- **Sources:** [Szpunar2013] [SzpunarSummary] [Rosenshine2012]

**6.4 Microlearning**

- **Finding:** "Microlearning" has no agreed definition or length, and the pooled estimates that exist come from weak venues.
- **Evidence:** A 2022 scoping review in a leading instructional-design journal set out to map how microlearning is used in academic and workplace settings (abstract only). A practitioner report drawing on it says microlearning "addresses one concept or learning objective and is generally less than 10 minutes", sometimes 15 or 20, and that "sometimes the effectiveness of microlearning may be confounded with actually providing training (versus none)". A nursing scoping review defines modules as "approximately 90 seconds to 5 minutes" and found "inconsistent definitions of time limitations". A 2025 meta-analysis reports SMD = 0.74 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.90; 42 studies, 15,673 participants, 18 of them randomized; I2 = 71%).
- **Caveats:** The 2025 meta-analysis appeared in an Indonesian mathematics-education journal, mixes many kinds of intervention under one label, and I could not judge its study selection. Treat the 0.74 as unverified.
- **Grade:** C.
- **Sources:** [TaylorHung2022] [Bozarth2024] [MicroNursing2025] [Jainuri2025]

### 7. Cognitive load theory

**7.1 What the theory says, in its developer's account**

- **Finding:** Cognitive load theory's design effects hold for material where many elements must be handled at once and for learners who are new to it. The theory reached that position through its own failed replications.
- **Evidence:** Sweller's 2023 account: the worked-example effect at first failed to replicate when examples split the learner's attention between a diagram and text, which led to the split-attention effect (integrate the text into the diagram). The split-attention effect then failed for some diagram-text pairs, which led to the redundancy effect (if one source is intelligible alone, adding the other harms). Further failures led to element interactivity - "the number of interacting elements determines intrinsic load" - and to the expertise reversal effect: as learners gain expertise they chunk interacting elements into one, the load falls, and effects "disappear or reverse". He reports that the worked-example advantage "is obtained for high element interactivity information", with the reverse pattern at low element interactivity.
- **Evidence for the component effects:** Worked examples in mathematics g = 0.48 (55 studies). Spatially integrated text and diagrams g = 0.63 (58 comparisons, 2,426 participants). Segmenting d = 0.32 to 0.36. In adult training, load-reducing methods helped novices more than experienced trainees: increasing difficulty g = +0.75 for inexperienced trainees; learner control g = +0.43 for experienced trainees and about zero for novices. Koedinger, Booth and Klahr summarize: novices "benefit from studying many worked examples in place of many problems, whereas shifting to pure problem-solving practice becomes more effective as students develop expertise".
- **Programming-specific evidence:** Worked examples with labelled sub-goals across a semester-long introductory programming course (265 students, sections assigned to condition): quiz average 48% against 41% (d = 0.44), exam averages not significantly different, lower variance in exam scores and fewer withdrawals and failures in the sub-goal group. The authors link the method to instructors' "tacit knowledge about low-level procedures that they have automatized".
- **Caveats:** See 7.2. The semester study assigned sections, so instructor effects are mixed in.
- **Grade:** A for worked examples, split attention and segmenting; B for the sub-goal study; D for the theory's account of why.
- **Sources:** [Sweller2023] [Barbieri2023] [SchroederCenkci2018] [Rey2019] [Plott2014] [KoedingerBoothKlahr2013] [Margulieux2020]

**7.2 Critiques of the theory**

- **Finding:** The main criticism is that the theory can explain any result after the fact, because its three kinds of load are not measured separately.
- **Evidence:** De Jong (2010): in many studies "there is no direct measurement of cognitive load; the level of cognitive load is induced from results on knowledge post-tests". The common one-item effort rating has "no agreed upon meaning"; the same score is linked with good performance in one study and poor performance in another. Three standing problems: measures are relative only, a single overall rating cannot separate the kinds of load, and the measures cannot detect overload because "the critical level indicating overload is unknown". Because a process can be called germane when learning improves and extraneous when it does not, the theory "fits within the theory post-hoc" (as a later critic quotes him). Sweller's reply, in effect, is his 2023 paper: each failed conceptual replication exposed a missing condition and the theory grew to include it.
- **Caveats:** The dispute is about the explanation. The design effects in 7.1 stand on their own data. Sweller's account makes two conditions decide whether an effect appears: the learner's prior knowledge and the number of elements that must be processed together. He also concedes that element interactivity "cannot be accurately measured because it relies on knowledge of the exact contents of long-term memory, for any individual learner, but it can be estimated".
- **Grade:** D (theory dispute).
- **Sources:** [deJong2010] [Sweller2023]

### 8. Examples and representations

**8.1 Comparison, analogy and contrasting cases**

- **Finding:** Having learners compare cases produces better learning than giving them the same cases one after another, and the gain is larger when the principle is stated after the comparison.
- **Evidence:**
  - Meta-analysis: 57 experiments, 336 tests. Case comparison against sequential cases, single cases, non-analogous cases, traditional instruction or control: d = 0.50, 95% CI [.44, .56]. Four of 15 moderators were reliable: asking learners to find similarities, giving the principle after the comparison, perceptual content, and testing immediately were each linked with larger effects.
  - Analogical encoding: 128 undergraduates read two negotiation cases. Those asked to compare them transferred the principle to a new case 48% of the time; those who read them separately, 19%. For contingent contracts 38% against 16%; for trade-offs 59% against 22%.
  - Contrasting cases as preparation: in three classroom studies, college students who analyzed contrasting cases and then heard a lecture made more accurate predictions about a new experiment a week later than students who read about the distinctions and heard the lecture, summarized a text and heard the lecture, or analyzed the cases twice with no lecture (abstract; no effect sizes seen). In the adolescent physics study, inventing with contrasting cases before being told gave d = .27 to .33 on transfer with equal word-problem skill (N = 128 and N = 120).
  - Direct Instruction's juxtaposition rules prescribe the same thing at the level of single examples: minimally different pairs to teach a distinction, greatly different pairs to teach sameness [Twyman2021].
- **Caveats:** The pooled 0.50 mixes several kinds of control condition. The effect was larger on immediate tests. Much of the primary work is short lab sessions. The Schwartz and Bransford result shows that cases alone, with no telling afterward, did worse.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Alfieri2013] [Gentner2003] [SchwartzBransford1998] [Schwartz2011] [Twyman2021]

**8.2 Concrete examples of declarative concepts**

- **Finding:** Adding illustrative examples to a definition improves learners' ability to recognize new instances.
- **Evidence:** Three experiments with undergraduates (176 in Experiment 1b). Students who studied definitions followed by examples classified examples better than students who studied the definitions for the same time: studied examples d = 0.74 and 1.43; new examples d = 0.40 and 1.22 across two experiments. Whether examples came before or after the definition made no significant difference. In Experiment 2, interleaving examples of different concepts mattered only when the definitions were not shown alongside.
- **Caveats:** The authors note that before their study "the available outcomes provide weak evidence at best". Psychology concepts, single sessions.
- **Grade:** A (lab).
- **Sources:** [Rawson2015]

**8.3 Multiple representations (Ainsworth's DeFT framework)**

- **Finding:** More than one representation can help in three ways - by complementing, constraining, or supporting the construction of deeper understanding - and the benefit depends on the learner being able to read each one and relate them.
- **Evidence:** Ainsworth: "a number of studies have found that learners benefit ... but unfortunately, just as many studies fail to find these benefits". Learners "tend to treat representations in isolation and find it difficult to integrate information from more than one source". Evidence that offering a choice of representation to suit a learner's style helps is "limited", and expertise explains individual differences better than style: in one cited study novices in physics gained from still graphics over text and experienced students showed no difference. When two sources must be read together, placing them together helps: g = 0.63 across 58 comparisons.
- **Caveats:** DeFT is a framework for asking design questions and gives no single prescription.
- **Grade:** D for the framework; A for spatial integration.
- **Sources:** [Ainsworth2006] [SchroederCenkci2018]

**8.4 Variation theory (Marton)**

- **Finding:** Variation theory gives an order for varying examples: hold everything constant except the feature to be noticed, then vary features together.
- **What it prescribes:** Four patterns - contrast (a quality is noticed only against what it is not), separation (vary one aspect while the others stay fixed), generalization (hold the value fixed while other aspects vary), fusion (vary the critical aspects at the same time). Separation should come before fusion.
- **Evidence:** A review reports quasi-experimental "learning studies" with large differences, such as 70% against 30% of students reaching the target understanding of an economics concept, and 85% against 25% in accounting. In a later two-study paper on supply and demand, 74.4% of students using the text built to the theory reached the target conception against 40.0% with the other text. The second study in that paper found the best pattern depended on the learners' starting grasp: each of two designs worked for one group and both were "equally ineffective when used for the wrong group of learners". A study with 375 kindergarten children randomly assigned to conditions found contrast most effective, random pairing second, and induction from similar examples worst (abstract only).
- **Caveats:** Most studies are by the theory's originators and their students, often with teachers co-designing the lesson, so the design process is mixed in with the pattern of variation. The finding that contrast beat similarity-based induction for young children learning characters sits beside the meta-analytic finding that asking adults for similarities works well [Alfieri2013]; the materials and tasks differ.
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [MartonPang2006] [Akerlind2015] [PangMarton2013] [VariationChinese2025]

### 9. Assessment design inside a lesson

**9.1 Formative assessment: the effect and the dispute about its size**

- **Finding:** Checking understanding during teaching and acting on the result helps. The widely quoted size of the effect has no solid source.
- **Evidence:** Bennett traced the "0.4 to 0.7" figure through the advocacy literature to Black and Wiliam's 1998 review, and reports that the review is not a meta-analysis and that the quantitative claims derive "from untraceable, flawed, dated, or unpublished sources". A commentary confirms that Black and Wiliam "said specifically that they could not conduct a meta-analysis" because the studies differed too much. Kingston and Nash screened more than 300 K-12 studies, found 13 with usable data and 42 effect sizes: median 0.25, weighted mean 0.20; English language arts 0.32, mathematics 0.17, science 0.19 (the article and its abstract print 0.09 for science; a 2015 erratum corrected it to 0.19, 95% CI [0.06, 0.31], after eight science studies were found coded as reading studies); professional-development-based implementations 0.30 and computer-based formative systems 0.28. Within mastery learning, effects shrank when the control group received the same amount of quiz feedback [Kulik1990], which suggests the feedback is a working part.
- **Caveats:** Briggs and colleagues, and separately McMillan and colleagues, criticized the Kingston and Nash study selection and effect-size handling and concluded that "considerable uncertainty remains". Bennett's central point is that "formative assessment" names many different practices, so one number cannot describe it. Guskey's version specifies the practice more tightly: a check, feedback that says what was learned and what was not, correctives in a different form, and enrichment.
- **Grade:** A, contested.
- **Sources:** [Bennett2011] [KingstonNash2011] [Briggs2012] [McMillan2013] [Kulik1990] [Guskey1988]

**9.2 Misconception-based distractors and diagnostic questions**

- **Finding:** The recommended way to write wrong answers is to start from errors learners actually make, so that each wrong choice points to a specific gap.
- **What the sources prescribe:** Gierl and colleagues' review finds two strategies in the literature. The first and most common builds distractors from "common misconceptions or errors in thinking", found from students' open-ended answers, from think-aloud studies, or from asking experienced teachers questions such as "What do students usually confuse this concept or idea with?". The second builds distractors similar in content and structure to the right answer. The Force Concept Inventory offers "a forced choice between Newtonian concepts and commonsense alternatives"; its taxonomy lists 28 misconceptions, each tied to the answer choices that suggest it, and its distractors "were culled from extensive student interviews". Its authors add that "errors on the Inventory are more informative than correct choices" and that single items should not be given great weight. Barton's rules for a diagnostic question: "You should learn something from each incorrect response without the student needing to explain", and "It cannot be answered correctly while still holding a key misconception". He separates mistakes (one-off slips the student can find when told) from misconceptions (which recur, and where telling the student they are wrong does little).
- **Evidence from a deployed system:** Eedi reports tagging 12,000 questions by construct and mapping their 36,000 distractors to misconceptions, with over 4 million responses collected. A published example item shows the pattern: 58% correct, and the two common wrong answers (17% and 19%) indicating different difficulties that call for different teaching. The Education Endowment Foundation funded a 175-school trial. The 2020 exam cancellation removed the attainment outcome. What remains: teachers reported about 28 minutes a week less homework-related work; quizzes were set far less often than intended (25 in Year 10 and 4 in Year 11 against planned figures of about 80 and 52); teachers and students reported guessing; students eligible for free school meals were less likely to start or finish quizzes.
- **Caveats:** The design practice is well specified and its effect on learning is untested at scale. The KLI framework counts a misconception as a knowledge component in its own right [Koedinger2012], which gives a place to store them in a plan.
- **Grade:** C.
- **Sources:** [Gierl2017] [Hestenes1992] [Barton2018] [EediBlog] [Foster2021] [EEFEedi2021]

**9.3 Parallel or isomorphic items**

- **Finding:** Items built to test the same thing with different surface features are often unequal in difficulty, and the size of the gap depends on what was changed.
- **Evidence:**
  - Physics, 2026: a human-in-the-loop generative-AI pipeline produced isomorphic problem banks; 20 banks were used in three midterms (more than 220 students). 75% of banks were statistically homogeneous in difficulty (Fisher's exact test, alpha = .05). Bank-level accuracy ran from 47.8% to 94.6%. Causes of unequal variants included "ambiguous wording, overlooked implicit verbal constraints, and configurations that invite common physics reasoning errors".
  - Medicine, 2023: 50 item models, four generated variants each, 2,218 final-year students in 12 UK schools. Twenty item models had a difference in proportion correct greater than 0.15 between variants. Changes "that could alter clinical reasoning strategies had the greatest impact".
  - Introductory Python, five semesters (576 to 720 students each): regression on exam scores. "Less complex" changes (renaming variables or functions, adding a prototype) had small, often non-significant effects. "Complex" changes (swapping parameter order, changing constants, reversing polarity such as greater-than to less-than, changing data types) "often" cost 3 to nearly 10 percentage points. A question seen before on homework scored 7 to 11 points higher than a hidden variant, about 8 on average.
  - University science courses, 2023: quantitative questions with changed numbers were the easiest to vary without changing performance; conceptual questions were harder, and the authors attribute their surprises to "our own expert blind spot".
- **Caveats:** The medical result is confounded with school, since each school took one paper. The Python coefficients vary by semester. None of these studies tested a two-in-a-row mastery rule; they speak to whether the second item is a fair repeat of the first.
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [Liu2026] [Westacott2023] [Fowler2024] [GoolsbyCole2023]

**9.4 Estimating item difficulty before data exist**

- **Finding:** Two routes exist - human judgment and text-based prediction - and both give a rough ordering that needs correcting once responses arrive.
- **Evidence:** A 2026 study summarizes the human record: pooled expert judgments correlated about .72 to .83 with empirical difficulty (Thorndike 1982) and .78 (Impara and Plake 1998); individual raters ranged from .40 to .85 (Lorge and Kruglov 1953); other studies found below .50 even with trained judges (Bejar 1983) and from near zero to .80 for individual judges in standard-setting. The same study tested three off-the-shelf LLMs on primary-school mathematics items from an online learning system across six domains: Spearman correlation with empirical difficulty, averaged over conditions, .673 for GPT-4o (95% CI [.629, .712]), .633 for DeepSeek Chat, .665 for Qwen. Asking "which of these two is harder" beat asking for an absolute rating by .12 to .15 on average, with the largest gain (.18 to .28) in the plain zero-shot setting; the gap shrank to between .02 and .10 when token probabilities and a few calibrated example items were supplied. A systematic review of text-based difficulty prediction reports that expert judgment and pretesting remain the traditional methods, "criticised for being costly, time-consuming, subjective and difficult to scale"; that 34% of the reviewed papers checked their models against expert judgment, with three experts on average; and that linguistic features "play a major role" in predicting difficulty. Webb's guides add a design caution: depth-of-knowledge level is not difficulty [DOKGuide].
- **Caveats:** One item bank, primary mathematics, skewed toward easier items. Generalization to code exercises for adults is untested in these sources.
- **Grade:** B.
- **Sources:** [Kolesnikova2026] [AlKhuzaey2023] [DOKGuide]

### 10. Adult learners

**10.1 Andragogy (Knowles) and its empirical record**

- **Finding:** Knowles's assumptions about adult learners are widely taught and have a thin, inconclusive research record.
- **What it claims:** The adult learner (1) is self-directing; (2) brings a larger stock of experience that can serve as a basis for learning; (3) learns on a need-to-know basis tied to adult roles; (4) is problem- and task-centered; (5) responds more to internal than external motivators. A sixth assumption in later statements is that adults "need to know the reason for learning something".
- **Evidence:** Rachal (2002): the empirical literature "remains, after over three decades, both inconclusive and beset by considerable variability in definition", and much of it sits "in unread dissertations". Taylor and Kroth (2009) list four obstacles to testing it: whether it is a theory at all (Knowles himself retreated to "a model of assumptions"); no agreed set of practices that count as andragogical; a bind in which achievement tests are the usual measure and are themselves contrary to the approach; and the fact that the traits are not confined to adults - "some adults are highly dependent on a teacher for structure, while some children are independent self-directed learners".
- **Caveats:** Both papers are reviews of a weak literature. Two of the assumptions map onto things that have been tested under other names: task-centering (finding 1.4) and self-direction (10.2).
- **Grade:** D.
- **Sources:** [Rachal2002] [TaylorKroth2009]

**10.2 Learner control - the tested form of "self-direction"**

- **Finding:** Giving learners control over their instruction has about zero average effect. It helps experienced learners and control of pace, and it hurts when learners decide how much practice or feedback they get.
- **Evidence:** Educational technology: 18 studies, 29 effects, g = 0.05, with near-zero effects for most kinds of control. Adult training (Army meta-analyses): 40 studies, 144 effects, overall g = +0.02. Experienced trainees g = +0.43; novices g = -0.004. Control over pace g = +0.15. Control over the amount of feedback or practice g = -0.19 on transfer. Factual knowledge g = -0.06; procedural and problem-solving tasks g = +0.09. Exploratory learning was less effective than guided training overall. In the segmenting meta-analysis, learner-paced segments helped transfer (d = 0.45) and did not reliably help retention (d = 0.19).
- **Caveats:** "Learner control" bundles pace, sequence, content and amount. The cells for experienced trainees are small.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Karich2014] [Carolan2014] [Plott2014] [Rey2019]

**10.3 Prior knowledge**

- **Finding:** What a learner knows beforehand strongly predicts what they know afterward and does not predict how much they gain.
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 493 studies, 8,776 effect sizes, 126,050 participants. Correlation of prior knowledge with post-test knowledge r = .531 (95% CI [.509, .552]) in the results text and table; the paper's abstract prints .534. Correlation with normalized gain r = -.059, with a 95% prediction interval from -.688 to .621. The authors say this "falsifies general statements such as 'knowledge is power' or 'prior knowledge is negligible'" and calls for study of the conditions.
- **Caveats:** None of the gain-score studies was a randomized trial. The wide interval is the finding: the same prior knowledge helps in some designs and hurts in others, which is consistent with the expertise reversal effect in 7.1.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Simonsmeier2022]

**10.4 Workplace training and transfer to the job**

- **Finding:** Organizational training works on average, and whether it shows up on the job depends on the learner and the workplace as well as the course.
- **Evidence:** Training effectiveness: d = 0.60 on reaction measures (15 effects), 0.63 on learning (234 effects, N = 15,014), 0.62 on behavior on the job (122 effects), 0.62 on results (26 effects). Transfer of training, correlations with transfer after removing same-source bias: cognitive ability .37, voluntary participation .34, conscientiousness .28, motivation .23, pre-training self-efficacy .22, work environment .23 (transfer climate .27, support .21; supervisor support .31, peer support .14). Age .04, education .07, experience .09. When predictor and transfer were both self-reported at the same time, correlations were inflated: environment .54 against .23, motivation .41 against .23.
- **Caveats:** Most cognitive-ability studies were lab studies with transfer measured right after training. Arthur and colleagues note that within studies that measured several levels, effects fell from learning to behavior to results.
- **Grade:** A.
- **Sources:** [Arthur2003] [Blume2010]

**10.5 A cognitively designed course for adults, taken in half the time**

- **Finding:** A university statistics course built on cognitive analysis of the subject, with practice and feedback built in, let students finish in about half the calendar time with equal or better results.
- **Evidence:** Carnegie Mellon, spring 2007: of 68 volunteers, 22 were randomly selected for the Open Learning Initiative course in hybrid form (8 weeks, two 50-minute class meetings a week) and 42 formed the control in the 15-week lecture course. In-class exams showed no significant difference. Dropout was similar (21 of 22 and 40 of 42 took the final). Time-use surveys were collected to check that the accelerated group was not simply cramming. The What Works Clearinghouse review reports the standardized concept test at 73% correct for the OLI group against 53% and judged the effect positive and statistically significant; its study page lists 68 against 53 for 61 students.
- **Caveats:** Small, volunteers, one institution, developers as evaluators. The two clearinghouse pages disagree on the intervention-group figure.
- **Grade:** A (one small randomized study).
- **Sources:** [Lovett2008]

### 11. Model-drafted lesson plans, skill labels and prerequisite links (bears on the "80% rule")

- **Finding:** In 2024 to 2026 studies, LLM output on lesson-design tasks sits near novice-teacher level on first pass, improves with structured revision loops, is strongest on structure and alignment and weakest on depth and domain judgment, and is recommended by its own evaluators as a draft for expert revision.
- **Evidence:**
  - Knowledge components: GPT-4 generated a KC label for each of 80 chemistry and 80 e-learning multiple-choice questions, given only the course level and subject. The best prompting strategy matched the course authors' label for 56% of chemistry and 35% of e-learning questions. Where they differed, three domain experts per subject preferred the LLM label in 23 of 35 chemistry cases (66%) and 32 of 52 e-learning cases (62%); a binomial test on the combined data gave p = 0.017. An algorithm also clustered questions by KC with no labels.
  - Lesson plans, mathematics: plans generated by GPT-4 with a problem-chain structure "slightly outperformed those written by pre-service teachers". After one round of simulated teaching, reflection and revision, GPT-4's plans scored slightly above high-quality plans by exemplary teachers; after two rounds they exceeded them on most dimensions.
  - Lesson plans, evaluation method: a study that built a tuned lesson-plan model had three expert teachers score plans on structure, content accuracy, logic and language (kappa 0.83) and found that using GPT-4 as the judge left "large gaps" from the human scores. In a head-to-head comparison of 50 model plans with teacher-written plans from two Chinese lesson-plan platforms, three experienced teachers judged "about 80%" of the model's plans comparable to the human ones.
  - Teaching plans, medicine: 25 urology faculty at three hospitals blind-rated plans on prostate cancer from four LLMs and from an experienced professor. GPT-4 and Gemini 1.5 Pro beat the other two models and "still lagged behind human-generated plans" (abstract only).
  - Lesson plans, social studies: in a blind review of six plans, AI plans outscored pre-service teachers' plans on clarity, alignment and inquiry integration, while the human plans showed "greater historical nuance and critical engagement" (abstract only).
  - Prerequisite relations: GPT-3.5, GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o were asked to generate course concepts and judge prerequisite links for computer science and mathematics courses, with four experts evaluating. GPT-4o did best on relation identification, richer prompts mostly helped, and the authors conclude that "LLM outputs still require expert revision", with implicit prerequisites the hard case.
- **Caveats:** Small samples of plans, raters from single institutions, fast-moving models, and K-12 or medical content. None measured student learning from the plans. The expert-preference result for KC labels also shows that human-authored labels are themselves imperfect, which fits the expert blind spot findings in 4.1.
- **Grade:** B to C.
- **Sources:** [Moore2024] [Hu2025] [Zheng2025] [ProstateLLM2025] [BotsBeginners] [Yang2025]

## Disagreements and open questions

**Disagreements in the evidence**

1. **Show first or struggle first.** Worked-example research says novices should study an example before solving ([vanGog2011]: problem-then-example did no better than problems alone; [Barbieri2023]: g = 0.48). Productive-failure research says attempting a problem before instruction improves conceptual knowledge and transfer ([SinhaKapur2021]: g = 0.36; [Schwartz2011]). The sources suggest three ways the two may fit together: the outcome measured (procedural skill against conceptual understanding and transfer), the learner's age and prior knowledge (the PS-I effect reverses for young children), and element interactivity (Sweller holds that worked examples win when many elements interact and problem solving wins when few do [Sweller2023]). A direct test of these three against one another in adult technical training is still to be run, as far as the sources I read show.
2. **Whole task or small parts.** Merrill and 4C/ID say teach inside whole tasks from the start and criticize prerequisite-first, topic-by-topic teaching ([Merrill2002], [TenSteps], [RosenbergKima2022]). Direct Instruction and Rosenshine say teach in small steps and master each before the next ([DIRubric], [Rosenshine2012]). The training meta-analysis gives a condition: splitting hurts when the parts must be performed together and is neutral when they run in sequence, and in computer-based instruction with no instructor part-task training came out ahead ([Wickens2013], [Plott2014]).
3. **How large the mastery-learning effect is.** 0.52 SD in [Kulik1990]; 0.78 in Guskey and Gates's review; a median of 0.25 in Slavin's best-evidence review; and roughly nothing on standardized tests in the group-based studies that used them. The Kulik paper explains the gaps by test type, by counting formative tests as outcomes, and by which studies were admitted.
4. **Direct Instruction.** A positive 50-year meta-analysis [Stockard2018] against a critical review that calls the supporting studies weak and the gains narrow [Eppley2019].
5. **Formative assessment.** "0.4 to 0.7" (untraceable, per [Bennett2011]) against 0.20 [KingstonNash2011], with the 0.20 itself challenged on method [Briggs2012] [McMillan2013].
6. **What an objective should contain and whether to show it.** Mager's three-part objective [Mager1997] against the report that only the "what will be learned" part helps learners [Hamilton1985]; Merrill's preference for showing the task over stating the objective [Merrill2002]; the warning that objectives pull attention away from material they do not mention [Hamilton1985] [Melton1978]; and the finding that a pretest question outperforms a stated objective [Sana2020].
7. **Blocked comparison against spaced or interleaved practice.** Side-by-side comparison of cases helps (d = 0.50, [Alfieri2013]) and interleaving helps for some materials and hurts for others [Brunmair2019]. The KLI authors name this as one of the apparent contradictions that only an account of knowledge type can sort out [Koedinger2012].
8. **Look for similarities or look for contrasts.** Asking for similarities gave larger effects in the comparison meta-analysis [Alfieri2013]; variation theory and Direct Instruction put contrast (minimally different pairs) first, and a 375-child experiment found contrast beat similarity-based induction [MartonPang2006] [Twyman2021] [VariationChinese2025].
9. **Self-explanation prompts.** KLI lists prompted self-explanation as the fitting move for principles [Koedinger2012]. In the mathematics worked-example meta-analysis, adding self-explanation prompts reduced the benefit [Barbieri2023].
10. **Self-direction for adults.** Andragogy assumes adults should direct their own learning [Rachal2002]. The pooled effect of learner control is about zero, positive for experienced learners and for control of pace, negative for control of how much practice and feedback to take [Karich2014] [Plott2014].
11. **Who gains from segmenting.** Cognitive load reasoning predicts novices gain most. The segmenting meta-analysis found high-prior-knowledge learners gained more on retention [Rey2019].
12. **Prerequisites by expert or by data.** Experts and data agreed on 56% of unit pairs in one large system [Vuong2011]; a carefully validated prerequisite structure (ALEKS) produced learning no better than classroom teaching [Fang2019]; one classic statistical check passes random orders [PhillipsKane1974].
13. **Whether cognitive load theory explains anything.** [deJong2010] against [Sweller2023].
14. **How firm the "70% rule" is.** A large pooled training effect [TofelGrehl2013] sits on omission-rate studies with 3 and 10 surgeons from one group [Sullivan2014] [ClarkPugh2012].

**Open questions the sources leave**

- **Unit length for adults.** The segmenting evidence supports cutting material into meaningful pieces and gives no length in minutes; the minute figures come from vendor guidance, video watch-time, and a misread review ([Rey2019], [Knewton], [Guo2014], [Bradbury2016]). The segmenting studies also show a time cost.
- **Which gate for which knowledge.** KLI offers a mapping from kind of knowledge to kind of instruction and labels it a hypothesis [Koedinger2012]. A study that assigns different mastery checks (perform, answer, read) by knowledge type and compares outcomes is still to be found; every source here stops at the level of instructional method.
- **Whether a second "parallel" item is a fair retest.** The item-equivalence studies show variants often differ, and that having seen a version is worth 7 to 11 points in one programming course ([Fowler2024], [Liu2026], [Westacott2023]). How that interacts with a two-in-a-row mastery rule is untested in these sources.
- **Durability.** Most of the lab effects here (objectives, examples, comparison, segmenting, interpolated tests) were measured minutes after study. The mastery-learning follow-ups (about 8 weeks) and the Direct Instruction maintenance data are the main longer-term evidence in this stream.
- **Population.** Very little of this evidence comes from working software engineers. The nearest populations are undergraduates, adult trainees in military and organizational settings, medical trainees, and CS1 students.
- **Model-drafted plans.** The studies rate the plans with expert rubrics; what students learn from such plans is the measurement still to be made ([Hu2025], [Zheng2025], [Moore2024]).
- **Misconception-keyed distractors.** The practice is specified in detail and its effect on learning is unmeasured in the one large trial [EEFEedi2021].
- **Redundancy effect.** The only account in this file is Sweller's narrative [Sweller2023]; a meta-analysis is still to be fetched.

## What the sources themselves recommend

These are practices the sources prescribe, each attributed. They are reported, not endorsed.

**On the order of design work**

- **Dick, Carey and Carey:** follow ten steps in order - goals, instructional analysis down to entry skills, learner and context analysis, performance objectives, assessments matched to objectives, instructional strategy, materials, formative evaluation (one-to-one, small group, field trial), revision, summative evaluation [DickCarey2015] [HeasterEkholm2020].
- **Wiggins and McTighe, as described by Empirical Education:** design in three stages - desired results, acceptable evidence, then learning experiences [Sterling2007].
- **Guskey, for Bloom:** make objectives, instruction, feedback and evaluation consistent with one another; after each unit give a check, then correctives that take a different approach from the first teaching, and enrichment for those who passed [Guskey1988].
- **Van Merrienboer, Kirschner and Frerejean:** build on whole authentic tasks; group them in simple-to-complex task classes; vary tasks within a class; fade support within a class; keep supportive information available throughout a class; give procedural information just in time; add part-task practice only for routine aspects that need automaticity [TenSteps].
- **Merrill:** show learners the whole task they will be able to do; activate prior experience and give a structure; demonstrate; let learners apply with feedback; have them integrate the skill into their own work; use a progression of problems that are explicitly compared [Merrill2002].

**On finding what to teach**

- **Clark and colleagues:** use two or more experts with a record of success; interview them separately; follow the five CTA stages; verify the result [Clark2008]. **Sullivan and colleagues:** use CTA prompts because free recall leaves most decision steps out [Sullivan2014]. **Tofel-Grehl and Feldon:** report which CTA method was used [TofelGrehl2013].
- **Koedinger, Corbett and Perfetti:** define knowledge components at the level where novices make errors; choose instruction to fit the kind of component; treat misconceptions as components [Koedinger2012].
- **Koedinger and colleagues (2013):** inspect learning curves; where a skill's curve is rough or flat, look for a hidden component; split it; write tasks that isolate the hard component; do not credit the hard skill through success on easier scaffolded steps [Koedinger2013] [Cen2006].
- **Falmagne and colleagues:** build the prerequisite structure by querying several experts, then refine with student data and check it by predicting a held-out problem [FalmagneALEKS].
- **Vuong, Nixon and Towle:** test each assumed prerequisite against performance data and investigate the pairs where experts and data disagree [Vuong2011].
- **Corcoran, Mosher and Rogat:** treat a learning progression as a hypothesis to be tested for whether students follow it and whether teaching to it improves results [Corcoran2009].

**On objectives and alignment**

- **Mager:** state the performance, the conditions and the criterion; add an indicator behavior when the intent is covert; leave teaching procedures out of the objective [Mager1997].
- **Webb, via the guides:** assign depth-of-knowledge by the thinking the task requires, not by the verb and not by difficulty; when torn between two levels pick the higher [DOKGuide] [Hess2009].
- **Sana and colleagues:** present objectives as questions for the learner to attempt before the material [Sana2020].
- **Knewton (vendor guidance):** size objectives at "the smallest meaningful level at which to track students' knowledge"; report student interactions at the finest grain available; keep content aligned to one objective, discrete, modular and plentiful [Knewton].

**On sequencing and grain**

- **Van Gog, Kester and Paas:** give novices the example before the problem [vanGog2011]. **Barbieri and colleagues:** use correct worked examples; pairing them with self-explanation prompts "may not be a fruitful design modification" [Barbieri2023].
- **Sinha and Kapur:** for conceptual understanding, let learners generate solutions to a well-designed problem before instruction; favor instruction first for young children and domain-general skills [SinhaKapur2021].
- **Wickens and colleagues; Plott and colleagues:** where parts are performed together, keep the whole task and shift emphasis among its parts; raise difficulty adaptively and not on a fixed schedule; take trainee experience into account [Wickens2013] [Plott2014].
- **Brunmair and Richter:** use interleaving for discriminating similar categories and "with caution ... especially for expository texts and words" [Brunmair2019].
- **Fyfe and colleagues:** move from concrete to an intermediate faded form to abstract, and link the forms explicitly [Fyfe2014].
- **Rey and colleagues, after Mayer:** present material in meaningful, coherent segments [Rey2019]. **Guo, Kim and Rubin:** plan video in chunks shorter than six minutes [Guo2014]. **Szpunar and colleagues:** place short tests between segments [Szpunar2013].
- **Rosenshine:** begin with review; present new material in small steps with practice after each; guide practice; check understanding; aim for a success rate of about 80%; scaffold hard tasks; review weekly and monthly [Rosenshine2012].
- **Engelmann and Carnine, via Twyman and the DI rubric:** order examples by the five juxtaposition rules; teach each thing across two or more lessons; add little new per lesson; review or incorporate everything; never contradict earlier teaching; field-test and revise [Twyman2021] [DIRubric]. **Engelmann:** design so learners are 70% correct on new material at first try, 90% on recent material, and fully firm by the end of the lesson [EngelmannInterview].

**On examples and representations**

- **Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn:** have learners compare cases, ask for similarities, and state the principle after the comparison [Alfieri2013]. **Gentner and colleagues:** present two cases together with a prompt to compare, not one after the other [Gentner2003].
- **Schwartz and Bransford:** have learners analyze contrasting cases first to create "a time for telling", then tell [SchwartzBransford1998].
- **Rawson and colleagues:** add illustrative examples to definitions [Rawson2015].
- **Marton and Pang:** vary one critical aspect at a time against a constant background, then vary aspects together [MartonPang2006]. **Pang and Marton:** choose the pattern in light of what the learners already grasp [PangMarton2013].
- **Ainsworth:** decide what each representation is for, and check that learners can read each one and relate them [Ainsworth2006].

**On checks inside a lesson**

- **Gierl and colleagues:** write distractors from known common errors; collect them from open responses, think-alouds or experienced teachers; review how often each is chosen [Gierl2017].
- **Barton:** one correct answer and three wrong ones that each reveal a specific misconception; a question that cannot be answered correctly while holding the key misconception [Barton2018]. **Hestenes and colleagues:** read the pattern across items, not single items [Hestenes1992].
- **Liu and colleagues:** separate structural from contextual variation when generating item variants; validate each bank's difficulty with student data; screen with language-model simulation first [Liu2026]. **Goolsby-Cole and colleagues:** change few variables per variant and "test drive" new questions in low-stakes settings [GoolsbyCole2023].
- **Kolesnikova and colleagues:** when using an LLM for a first difficulty estimate, prefer pairwise comparisons, or give the model a few items of known difficulty [Kolesnikova2026].
- **Bennett:** define the formative practice precisely before claiming an effect for it [Bennett2011].

**On adult learners and on model-drafted material**

- **Carolan and colleagues; Plott and colleagues:** give learners control of pace; be wary of handing novices control over how much practice and feedback they receive [Carolan2014] [Plott2014].
- **Rachal:** adopt agreed criteria for what counts as andragogical practice before testing it [Rachal2002].
- **Hu and colleagues:** improve a model-written plan through rounds of simulated teaching, reflection and revision [Hu2025]. **Moore and colleagues:** have the model propose its top candidate knowledge components and let an expert choose [Moore2024]. **Yang and colleagues:** keep expert revision in the loop for concept and prerequisite extraction [Yang2025]. **Zheng and colleagues:** rely on human raters, since a GPT-4 judge diverged from them [Zheng2025].
